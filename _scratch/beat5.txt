The mechanism behind this week's numbers reaches back months, not days. Sakurajima sits on the edge of a much larger, mostly submerged volcanic basin, the caldera under the bay, and Japan's monitoring agency has tracked magma slowly refilling that deep reservoir for an extended stretch. A field survey earlier this month measured the mountain's sulfur dioxide output at roughly two thousand one hundred tonnes a day, a rate the agency calls generally abundant. Gas at that volume signals fresh magma still pushing up from depth. That refilling explains something that looks contradictory in the daily count. Volcanic earthquakes at Sakurajima have been falling, from nine a day down to three a day across four days, and explosions dropped to zero in that same window. Yet the summit crater still produced six separate eruptions in that period. Fewer quakes usually track with less rock breaking underground as magma forces a path upward. But the agency's own bulletin ties the pattern to a deep supply that keeps accumulating steadily rather than surging, so the mountain vents in a more continuous rhythm instead of the sharp, quake heavy pulses that come with sudden pressure spikes. Continuous does not mean minor. In May, Sakurajima produced a pyroclastic flow, a fast moving cloud of hot gas and rock, which travelled roughly six hundred meters down the mountainside. It was the volcano's first flow of that kind in roughly eight years. Around that same period, monitors also recorded the mountain's own body swelling, as magma pooled inside it ahead of that flow. The agency reads this week's steady eruptions, the falling quake count, and the abundant gas discharge as different signs of the same refilling process that produced May's flow. Nothing in the current data suggests that process has stopped. It suggests the mountain has settled into a quieter, steadier way of releasing what keeps arriving from below the bay.
