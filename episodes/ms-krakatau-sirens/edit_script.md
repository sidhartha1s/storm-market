## C1 — WHAT A SIREN LISTENS FOR (1:20–2:20)

### C1-S1 — narration
> A tsunami siren does not listen for the sea. It listens for the ground. The ground shakes, seismographs on land measure the earthquake within a minute or two, and a computer matches it against a library of pre-run scenarios. If the sea floor moved enough, the warning goes out to sirens in about fifty five coastal towns, to phones, to television. Indonesia can do all of that in under five minutes, and it has. But look at the first link in that chain. Every step waits for an earthquake. The twenty two buoys that were supposed to feel the wave itself had all been dead since twenty twelve, vandalised, used as anchor points, some simply stolen. So that night the country had a fast siren, and nothing to pull the trigger. Because what happened at three minutes past nine was not an earthquake.

## C5 — ALASKA, 1958 (5:31–6:45)

### C5-S1 — narration
> Alaska. Before I tell you the number, guess how tall the tallest wave ever recorded on Earth was. A hundred feet? Three hundred? In nineteen fifty eight a magnitude seven point five earthquake hit the Fairweather fault, and thirty million cubic metres of rock fell a thousand metres into a fjord called Lituya Bay. The wave that came off that rockfall climbed the far shore to five hundred and twenty four metres. Seventeen hundred and twenty feet. It stripped the forest off the mountainside to that line, and the line is still visible from the air today. Three fishing boats were anchored in the bay. Howard Ulrich and his eight year old son rode the wave and lived. The Swansons were lifted over the treetops and lived. Orville and Mickey Wagner were never found. Ulrich said afterwards: it was not a wave at first, it was like an explosion. Two minutes from the shaking to the water. No siren on Earth is that fast. And there is one place where scientists are watching this exact problem unfold in slow motion, right now.

## C6 — BARRY ARM, TODAY (6:45–7:45)

### C6-S1 — narration
> It is a slope above Barry Arm, in Prince William Sound, Alaska. About five hundred million cubic metres of rock, sixteen Lituyas, held up by a glacier that is melting out from under it, forty eight kilometres from the town of Whittier. The first worst case said three hundred metres of run-up at the source. A second look brought Whittier's number down to about two feet offshore, while the fjord itself could still see two hundred. So how do you warn a town about a wave before the rock falls? You watch the rock. Water level gauges, seismic stations, an infrasound sensor, ground radar, a camera, and satellite radar from orbit. And on September fourth this year the reading was: no signs of large scale movement. Notice what that is. It is not a siren. It is a measurement of the mountain moving before it falls, and it is the same instrument Krakatau's observatory is relying on this week. The wrong number was the plume. ## C8 — THE ARITHMETIC (8:45–9:35)

### C8-S1 — narration
> Do the arithmetic. From the collapse to the first beach, the water needed twenty seven minutes. A tide gauge cannot see a wave until the wave is passing it, so the earliest any instrument could know was minute twenty four, and it did. Now add what a warning needs after that: a person to confirm it, a decision to sound the alarm, and the sirens themselves. In the best system on Earth that is five to ten minutes. So the siren lands at the same moment as the water, or after it. There is simply not enough time. That is not a failure of Indonesia. It is the shape of the problem, for any volcano that sits inside its own strait. And it means the only thing that can be faster is a person looking at the sea.
