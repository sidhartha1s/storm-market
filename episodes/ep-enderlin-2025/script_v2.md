## I · The night (0:18)
**Picture:** master map, the track draws east from a point south of town · popfig "11:02 PM" · g-clock starts.

The Enderlin tornado came down a little after eleven on a Friday night, June twentieth, twenty twenty five.
`[verify: touchdown and lift times, NWS PDF 11:02 to 11:21 PM vs Wikipedia 11:04 to 11:20 PM]` It touched the ground
about four miles south of Enderlin, a small town in the southeast corner of North Dakota. For nineteen minutes it
moved east across open farmland, and at its widest it was more than a mile across.

**Picture:** map pin on Highway 46 · text-only name cards "Michael and Katherine Dehn · 73", then "Marcario Lucio · 89".

One mile east of town, on Highway forty six, it hit the home of Michael and Katherine Dehn.
`[verify: name, "Katherine Dehn" vs "Katherine Pfaff-Dehn"]` They were both seventy three, and neither of them
survived. On another stretch of the path, it hit the farm of Marcario Lucio, who was eighty nine. His wife, Roberta,
made it through the night, and the storm took Marcario's life. No North Dakota tornado had taken that many lives since
nineteen seventy eight.

**Picture:** NWS survey b-roll (PD), a team walking a debris field · popfig "EF3" · then a North Dakota map where
twenty two small tracks land one by one.

Within days, surveyors from the weather office in Grand Forks walked the path and gave it a number. They called it
an EF three, with winds of about a hundred and sixty miles an hour. That is a violent tornado but not a rare one, and
North Dakota had twenty two tornadoes that same night.

**Picture:** split card returns: "El Reno 2013 · EF3" left, "Enderlin 2025 · EF3" right, a day counter under the
right stamp starts to run.

So for a hundred and eight days, Enderlin sat on the books with the same number as El Reno. Then the office that
wrote EF three put out a statement saying the number had been wrong. `[loop L2 open]` Its first number came from the
same method every American tornado is rated by, and that method has a limit built into it.

## A1 · How a tornado gets its number (1:47)
**Picture:** NWS survey team b-roll (PD), tape measures, a clipboard, a snapped tree · then g-ef-scale: the six
ratings stack up.

Nobody measures the wind inside a tornado at the spot where it hits the ground. The instruments are rarely there,
and the few that are there do not make it through. So the rating comes afterwards, from what the wind left behind.

A survey team walks the path, sometimes for days, and looks at every broken thing. They match it against a list of
twenty eight kinds of damage the scale recognizes. `[verify: 28 damage indicators, EF scale 2007]` The list runs from
a barn to a school to a hardwood tree. Each kind has steps, from a few shingles gone to nothing left, and each step
carries a wind speed. The tornado gets the highest number the damage can prove.

**Picture:** g-ef-scale zooms into one indicator, a tree; its steps climb and hit a lid drawn across the top. Then a
house indicator: two slabs side by side, one with anchor bolts drawn, one with nails.

That word, prove, is the whole problem, because every kind of damage has a ceiling. Once a tree has been stripped of
its bark and snapped, the scale has nothing higher to give it, whatever the wind was.
`[verify: hardwood tree damage indicator, top step and its expected wind, roughly 140 to 170 mph]` A farmhouse can go
higher, but only if it was built to hold. When a house is swept off its foundation, surveyors check how it was tied
down. Bolted to the concrete, a clean slab can mean EF five. Held on by a few nails, the same empty slab only proves
a strong EF three or a low EF four. `[verify: EF scale degree-of-damage adjustment for poor anchoring]`

**Picture:** El Reno 2013 map, the 2.6 mile outline over open section roads · two labels land on the voice:
"What the Radar Saw" high on the bar, "What the Damage Could Prove" low on it, a gap between them.

That ceiling is where El Reno's number came from. `[loop L1 closes]` Radar trucks measured its winds from a
distance, but the rating goes by damage, and a radar reading is not damage. Across most of its path, El Reno crossed
open fields with little on them that could prove more than an EF three.
`[verify: El Reno 2013 crossed mostly open country; radar data not used for the final rating]` Enderlin had the same
kind of path, and for a hundred and eight days it had the same answer. `[loop L2 continues]`

**Picture:** master map v2: farmsteads spaced far apart, shelter belts, the Maple River, power towers; Sentinel-2
ground scar if the tile and licence clear.

Now look at what the Enderlin tornado had to work with. There were farmsteads spread far apart, shelter belts of
trees, power towers and a river bank. There was no town in its path, no school and no neighborhood of modern homes.
It stripped the trees along the Maple River down to bare wood, and the survey could only count them as trees. It
swept farmhouses clean, and the survey had to ask how they had been fastened down.

So the surveyors wrote down what the damage could prove, and in June the damage could prove about a hundred and
sixty miles an hour. That was an honest number, and it was also a ceiling rather than a measurement.
`[loop L2 closes]`

**Picture:** NWS PD photo of derailed freight cars (check the credit line photo by photo) · the rail line on the
master map pulses.

But one piece of the damage did not fit anything on the list. `[loop L3 open]` South of town, the tornado had crossed
a rail line, and thirty three freight cars were off the tracks. The scale had no entry for a train, so in June the
train could not raise the number.

**Picture:** g-drought-timeline teaser: the Moore 2013 mark, then a long empty bar, still drawing.

That same ceiling, the one that held Enderlin at EF three, has a much bigger charge against it. A federal study says
it is the likely reason America went twelve years without a single EF five. `[loop L4 open]`

## A2 · Twelve years (5:37)
**Picture:** g-drought-timeline from the start: popfig "Feb 2007" · marks land one by one with PD stills: Greensburg
(FEMA), Parkersburg, the four of April 27, 2011, Joplin (FEMA), El Reno and Piedmont 2011, Moore (FEMA).

The Enhanced Fujita scale replaced the old Fujita scale in February, two thousand seven. Three months later, it had
its first EF five. That was Greensburg, Kansas, where the tornado destroyed about ninety five percent of the town and
eleven people lost their lives. A year later, Parkersburg, Iowa, got the second, and the toll there was nine.

Then came April twenty seventh, twenty eleven, when four EF fives touched down in Mississippi and Alabama on one day.
No single day since the new scale began has matched it. The one at Hackleburg and Phil Campbell, in Alabama, took
seventy two lives. Less than a month later, the Joplin tornado took a hundred and fifty eight lives in Missouri. No
single American tornado had taken more since nineteen forty seven.

**Picture:** the El Reno and Piedmont 2011 mark lands, then a thin line reaches forward to a second El Reno mark in
2013 stamped "EF3".

Two days after Joplin, an EF five hit El Reno and Piedmont, in Oklahoma, and six people lost their lives. That was a
different storm from the El Reno tornado two years later, the widest one, which stayed an EF three. So one Oklahoma
town has an EF five and an EF three on the books, and the weaker number belongs to the bigger storm.

**Picture:** Moore 2013, FEMA PD footage of the Plaza Towers area · popfig "May 20, 2013".

Then, on Monday, May twentieth, twenty thirteen, an EF five crossed Moore, Oklahoma. Twenty four people lost their
lives, and seven of them were children at Plaza Towers Elementary School. That made nine EF fives in six years.

**Picture:** the timeline keeps drawing past Moore with no marks; a day counter runs to "4,414 Days".

After Moore, there were none. The count ran for four thousand four hundred and fourteen days, from Moore to the night
of Enderlin. It was the longest stretch without an F five or an EF five since official counts began in nineteen
fifty. For twelve years, the worst tornadoes we saw on the news were all rated EF four or lower.

**Picture:** four EF4 cards slide in, each with a PD NWS or FEMA damage still: "Mayflower–Vilonia 2014",
"Rochelle–Fairdale 2015", "Mayfield 2021", "Rolling Fork 2023".

Strong tornadoes kept coming in those years, and some of them started long arguments. Meteorologists argued over
Mayflower and Vilonia in Arkansas in twenty fourteen, and over Rochelle and Fairdale in Illinois a year later.
`[verify: each of these four is named in the EF5-drought debate as a probable EF5; name one source per storm]` They
argued over Mayfield, Kentucky, in twenty twenty one, and over Rolling Fork, Mississippi, in twenty twenty three. Each
one was rated EF four, and each one had people saying it deserved a five.

That left meteorologists with a question they could not settle for years. Either the most violent tornadoes had
become rarer, or the way they were rated had changed.

**Picture:** g-person Tony Lyza (text-only card, no photo slot) · "NOAA National Severe Storms Laboratory" · beige
citation card: "Where Have the EF5s Gone?" Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society, 2025.

Tony Lyza, a research meteorologist at NOAA's National Severe Storms Laboratory, set out to answer it. His study,
published in twenty twenty five by the American Meteorological Society, was called "Where Have the EF Fives Gone?" He
looked at the gap with statistics, and he concluded that a real drop in tornado strength was highly unlikely. The
likelier cause, he found, was how surveys and ratings had shifted after the switch to the new scale in two thousand
seven. `[loop L4 closes]`

**Picture:** the ceiling lid from A1 slides over the whole drought bar · then the master map, the track glowing.

So the ceiling behind the twelve years was the same ceiling the Enderlin survey hit in June. And the proof that
Enderlin had gone past it was lying somewhere on a twelve mile path through Ransom and Cass counties.
`[loop L5 open]`

## A3 · Nineteen minutes (9:34)
**Picture:** SPC watch map (PD) · popfig "3:45 PM" · GOES satellite loop (NOAA PD) of storms building over eastern
Montana.

At three forty five that afternoon, forecasters put out a severe thunderstorm watch for southwest and south central
North Dakota. The storms behind it were building over eastern Montana. At eight fifteen, the Storm Prediction Center
sent out a mesoscale discussion, which is a short bulletin about one patch of the map.

**Picture:** the discussion as a beige text card, key words lifting on the voice: "surface based", "Red River
Valley" · then popfig "120 to 150 MPH".

It said the storms in eastern North Dakota were becoming surface based as they moved toward the Red River Valley,
and that raised the tornado threat. It put the strongest tornado winds at a hundred and twenty to a hundred and fifty
miles an hour. That was the forecast for the night, and the number the Enderlin tornado ended up with sits far above
it.

**Picture:** g-radar, a self-made Level II velocity re-plot (NEXRAD open data); two supercells labelled as they cross
the state · popfig "22 Tornadoes".

That night, two supercells, the rotating kind of thunderstorm, made twenty two tornadoes in North Dakota. Two storms
making that many is rare, and the Enderlin tornado was one of the twenty two. It was part of a larger outbreak that
ran from June nineteenth to June twenty second, with a derecho, a long line of damaging straight winds, in the middle
of it.

**Picture:** g-clock at 11:02 · master map zooms to the touchdown point on 58th Street SE, Ransom County.

At two minutes past eleven, it touched down along Fifty Eighth Street Southeast, in Ransom County. From there, it had
nineteen minutes and twelve miles of farm country ahead of it.

**Picture:** master map: the rail line pulses south of town · NWS PD photo of tipped grain cars (credit checked) ·
popfig "33 Cars".

South of Enderlin, the path crossed a rail line where freight cars stood on the tracks.
`[verify: cars were standing, not a moving train; and the strike order rail, river, Highway 46, Cass County farmsteads, from the NWS survey narrative]`
When the survey team got there, thirty three cars were off the rails. Nineteen were grain cars, still loaded, and
fourteen were empty tanker cars. One of the tankers was lying almost five hundred feet from the track. In June, that
was one strange detail on a long list, and it took months to turn it into a number. `[loop L3 continues]`

**Picture:** PD or credit-cleared photos of the Maple River woods, the trunks stripped pale · slow push, no hold
over 6 s.

Farther on, the tornado crossed the Maple River and flattened the woods along the bank. The trees that stayed
standing were stripped of their bark, which surveyors call sandpapering. It looks like the worst thing a tornado can
do to a tree, and on the scale, even a tree stripped that bare can only prove so much.

**Picture:** Highway 46 on the map · a PD or credit-cleared photo of a downed transmission tower · pins for the
homes near town.

East of Enderlin, it crossed Highway forty six. It knocked down four electrical transmission towers there, and about
ten homes near town were damaged. This was the stretch where Michael and Katherine Dehn had their home.

**Picture:** the track enters Cass County; NWS PD photo of a swept foundation (credit checked) · text on the voice:
"Swept Clean".

In Cass County, it ran across farmsteads along County Road thirty eight and the roads around it. Several farmhouses
there were wiped off their foundations, and the survey described the debris as swept clean.

**Picture:** g-clock hits 11:21 · the track ends five miles southwest of Alice · the full Enderlin track at scale,
then the El Reno outline ghosts in beside it at the same scale.

At twenty one minutes past eleven, it lifted about five miles southwest of Alice. Its path ran just over twelve
miles, and at its widest it measured one point oh five miles. El Reno, at its widest, was two point six miles, more
than twice as wide. So the narrower tornado ended up with the higher rating, and the reason sat on that rail line
south of town.

**Picture:** NWS survey b-roll (PD) · the EF3 stamp from I, holding · g-person Carl Jones (text-only card) slides in.

The survey logged no injuries on the whole path. Within days, the Grand Forks office had its EF three, and the rating
could have stayed there for good. Carl Jones, a meteorologist in that office, explained later why it did not.

## A4 · The second look (14:03)
**Picture:** beige quote card, words land in narration order: "This particular set of damage that we saw kind of
stood out as unique. We wanted to investigate further." · Carl Jones, NWS Grand Forks, MPR News, Oct 8, 2025.

He said, and this is a direct quote, "This particular set of damage that we saw kind of stood out as unique." Then he
said, "We wanted to investigate further." The damage he meant was on the rail line.
`[verify: the Jones quote refers to the train damage (MPR News, 8 Oct 2025)]` `[loop L6 closes]` And nothing in the
scale's list of twenty eight could turn a freight car into a wind speed. `[loop L0 continues]`

**Picture:** g-ef-scale returns: the house indicator with its steps and wind speeds, then an empty row labelled
"Freight Car" with no steps in it.

For a barn or a house, the scale says how much wind it takes to lift the roof or push over the walls. For a freight
car there was no such entry, so the office had to find one somewhere else.

**Picture:** no drone footage (chaser footage, no licence); the master map with survey points appearing, and an
attributed text card "Drone Video · Aaron Rigsby".

Part of the second look came from the air. A storm chaser named Aaron Rigsby had flown a drone over the damage, and
his video ran about thirteen minutes. `[verify: Rigsby's drone video, about 13 min, was reviewed by NWS in the re-survey]`
The office went through that footage as part of the review. An aerial view shows a whole debris field at once, which
a team on foot cannot see.

**Picture:** map pulls north across the border · text card "Northern Tornadoes Project · Western University, Canada".

The office also brought in help from Canada. Engineers from the Northern Tornadoes Project, at Western University,
joined the re-survey as wind damage experts. Their team, which includes Greg Kopp of Western University, is credited
with the forensic side of the work. They worked out how much wind it takes to tip over a loaded grain car, and loaded
grain cars had gone over south of Enderlin.

**Picture:** beige citation card: Miller et al., Monthly Weather Review, 2024, "Estimating Wind Speeds in Tornadoes
Using Debris Trajectories of Large…" · g-person Connell Miller (text-only).

The biggest piece came from a paper published in twenty twenty four. Its first author was Connell Miller, a former
wind researcher with the Northern Tornadoes Project. He now directs the Northern Mesonet Project. The paper came out
in Monthly Weather Review, and it showed how to estimate a tornado's wind from how far it carried very large
objects. `[verify: full title of Miller et al. 2024, MWR 152(8), DOI MWR-D-23-0251.1]`

**Picture:** g-mechanism: a tanker silhouette, a weight arrow pointing down, a wind arrow pushing sideways, an arc to
a landing mark; a ruler on the ground marked "50 m · about 160 ft".

Wind pushes on a car, the car's weight pulls it down, and the car only leaves the ground when the push wins. The
farther a heavy, compact object travels through the air, the stronger that push had to be. The paper drew a line at
fifty meters, which is about a hundred and sixty feet. A large, compact object carried farther than that generally
means winds at the EF five level.

**Picture:** master map rail segment · the tanker's arc draws to "almost 500 ft", the 160 ft line flashes about a
third of the way along · popfig "About 3×".

The Enderlin tanker weighed about seventy thousand pounds, empty. It had gone almost five hundred feet, nearly three
times the line. `[loop L3 closes]` So the scale finally had something to measure besides houses and trees, and it
pointed well past EF three. `[loop L0 closes: a thrown tanker, a paper about thrown objects, and the engineers who
used it]` `[loop L7 open]`

**Picture:** quote card, one line only.

Miller put it this way. "A powerful new tool for rating storms that produce extreme yet unconventional damage." Okay,
so a train is about as unconventional as damage gets. By early October, the Grand Forks office had its new number
ready, and it was the one the whole country had gone twelve years without.

## A5 · October 6 (18:18)
**Picture:** beige citation card, NWS Grand Forks statement, Oct 6, 2025 (PD) · popfig "Oct 6, 2025" · the EF3 stamp
struck through, red EF5 lands.

On Monday, October sixth, twenty twenty five, the Grand Forks office put out its revised rating. The Enderlin tornado
was now an EF five, with peak winds estimated at more than two hundred and ten miles an hour. The rating rested on the
train rather than on a house, which is rare for an EF five.

**Picture:** g-then-now: a wind ladder with three rungs landing on the voice: "8:15 PM Forecast · 120 to 150",
"June Survey · 160", "October · 210+".

At eight fifteen that night, forecasters expected the strongest tornadoes to reach a hundred and twenty to a hundred
and fifty. In June, the survey said about a hundred and sixty. In October, it said more than two hundred and ten.
Nothing about the storm changed between June and October, but the survey found a way to prove more.

**Picture:** g-drought-timeline closes: the counter stops at 4,414, the Enderlin mark lands, the long bar turns from
grey to red.

It was America's first EF five since Moore, and the twelve year gap was over. It went onto the official list of
American EF fives, the same list the twelve years had been counted from. Lyza's study had counted from that list too.

**Picture:** a PD survey photo of an empty farmhouse slab (credit checked), slow push · text-only names card, no
photo slot.

The new number did not change the path, the twelve miles, or the three people the storm took. It did not change what
anyone knew at eleven o'clock that night either, because a rating always comes after the storm. What it changed was
the history, and that history reaches well past North Dakota.

**Picture:** the four EF4 cards from A2 return, each with a small "?" pulsing beside the EF4.

Enderlin sat at EF three for a hundred and eight days before it moved up two full steps. That put the same question
back on every high end EF four since Moore, including Mayfield and Rolling Fork. `[loop L8 open]` Months earlier, a
scientist at a federal lab had already written down a change he thought the scale needed. `[loop L9 open · cta-hinge]`

## A6 · The open case (22:00)
**Picture:** real footage first (PD NWS survey b-roll), then g-then-now: a bar with the EF4 and EF5 bands and the line
between them sliding down a notch; g-person Tony Lyza returns.

Tony Lyza's change was to lower the line between EF four and EF five, slightly. `[loop L9 closes]` His study argued
that a small move would line up the new scale with the old Fujita scale used before two thousand seven. A lower line
would pull the top of the EF four range into EF five, and that is where the arguments over Mayfield and Rolling Fork
have always sat. `[loop L8 closes]` Enderlin was rated on the scale as it stands, with the line where it has been
since two thousand seven.

**Picture:** g-mechanism returns small: the tanker arc, then the same arc over an empty field with nothing to throw.

The Enderlin method has a limit of its own. It needs a large, heavy object in the path, thrown far enough to measure,
and most tornadoes never cross a train. El Reno had radar and open fields, and it still has its EF three.

**Picture:** a map of Oklahoma with an Enid pin · text card "Enid, Oklahoma · 2026".

In twenty twenty six, a tornado at Enid, Oklahoma, landed in the same argument over whether it reached EF five.
`[verify: Enid 2026 EF5 debate and its status as of the script date]` The ratings matter past any one town, because
they are the data scientists use to ask whether violent tornadoes are becoming rarer. That is the question Lyza's study
put to the twelve years, and the Enderlin rating ended the gap without settling it.

**Picture:** PD NWS warning graphic (polygon over a county map) · then the survey b-roll, a team walking at dawn.

For anyone in tornado country, the number that matters on the night is the warning, and it comes before any survey.
The rating is written weeks or months later, by people walking the path. On the Enderlin path, one part of what they
found was never going to change with any rating. `[loop L10 open]`
