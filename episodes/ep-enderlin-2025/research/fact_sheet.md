## 3. The EF5 evidence and the engineering

- The rating hinged on damage to the derailed train south of Enderlin, not on structural (home) damage indicators, which is unusual for an EF5 rating. https://www.mprnews.org/story/2025/10/08/enderlin-tornado-earns-rare-ef5-category-after-derailing-33-train-cars
- Carl Jones, meteorologist at NWS Grand Forks, on why the office pursued a re-survey: "This particular set of damage that we saw kind of stood out as unique. We wanted to investigate further." https://www.mprnews.org/story/2025/10/08/enderlin-tornado-earns-rare-ef5-category-after-derailing-33-train-cars
- NWS Grand Forks and its wind-damage-expert partners built a forensic wind-speed estimate from: tipping of fully loaded grain hopper cars (~129,700 kg / ~286,000 lb each) [unverified exact per-car weight — figure from Western University summary of the analysis, not the primary PDF] and lofting of tanker cars, including one empty tanker car (~32,600 kg / ~72,000 lb) thrown roughly 475.7 ft (145 m) from the tracks. https://news.westernu.ca/2025/10/ef5-tornado-enderlin/ ; https://www.mprnews.org/story/2025/10/08/enderlin-tornado-earns-rare-ef5-category-after-derailing-33-train-cars
- The methodology applied a 2024 peer-reviewed study, lead-authored by Connell Miller (Director of the Northern Mesonet Project, formerly a wind-impacts researcher with the Northern Tornadoes Project), published in Monthly Weather Review, vol. 152, issue 8 (2024), DOI MWR-D-23-0251.1, on "Estimating Wind Speeds in Tornadoes Using Debris Trajectories of Large [objects]" [unverified full title — truncated in the source page title tag]. https://journals.ametsoc.org/downloadpdf/view/journals/mwre/152/8/MWR-D-23-0251.1.pdf
- That study's finding used by NWS: lofting a large, compact object (like a tanker car) more than 50 meters generally indicates EF5-level winds (≥338 km/h, i.e. ≥210 mph); the Enderlin tanker car traveled roughly three times that threshold distance. https://news.westernu.ca/2025/10/ba-ef5-tornado-enderlin/ [note: canonical link is https://news.westernu.ca/2025/10/ef5-tornado-enderlin/]
- Connell Miller, quoted on the significance of using this method operationally: "This case exemplifies the importance of integrating engineering analysis and scientific research into operational meteorology," and "The National Weather Service's use of this research underscores its significance for future tornado assessments, offering a powerful new tool for rating storms that produce extreme yet unconventional damage." https://news.westernu.ca/2025/10/ef5-tornado-enderlin/
- Dr. Greg Kopp and the Northern Tornadoes Project (Western University, Canada) are credited generally with the forensic engineering collaboration, though no direct Kopp quote on Enderlin was found in the sources reviewed [unverified attribution of a direct quote]. https://educationnewscanada.com/article/education/level/university/1/1163759/western-research-helps-rate-historic-ef5-tornado-in-north-dakota.html
- Additional damage indicators cited in secondary reporting: complete destruction of a farmstead with debris "swept clean," and severe debarking ("sandpapering") of trees near the Maple River. https://www.kxnet.com/news/historic-ef5-tornado-enderlin/

Note: the 31 May 2013 El Reno, Oklahoma tornado (the widest ever recorded, 2.6 miles) was rated EF3, not EF5, despite mobile-radar wind measurements far exceeding EF5 thresholds — frequently cited in the drought debate as a rating anomaly. **The "EF5 drought" debate:**
- The ~12-year gap between Moore (2013) and Enderlin (2025) is the longest F5/EF5 drought since official US tornado records began in 1950. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EF5_drought
- Tony (Anthony) Lyza, research meteorologist at NOAA's National Severe Storms Laboratory, led a 2025 peer-reviewed study, "Where Have the EF5s Gone? A Closer Look at the 'Drought' of the Most Violent Tornadoes in the United States," published in the Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society (BAMS). https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/bams/106/8/BAMS-D-24-0066.1.pdf
- Lyza's statistical analysis concluded the drought is highly unlikely to reflect a real decline in tornado intensity, and more likely reflects how survey and rating practices shifted after the 2007 F-to-EF scale transition; the study argues a slight downward adjustment of the EF4/EF5 wind-speed boundary would better harmonize the old and new scales. https://yaleclimateconnections.org/2025/03/new-study-reveals-potential-cause-of-a-drought-in-violent-ef5-tornadoes/
- Named high-end EF4 tornadoes repeatedly cited in the controversy as probable EF5s that were not rated as such: the 2014 Mayflower–Vilonia, Arkansas tornado; the 2015 Rochelle–Fairdale, Illinois tornado; the 2021 Western Kentucky (Mayfield) tornado; and the 2023 Rolling Fork–Silver City, Mississippi tornado. [unverified — no single named meteorologist attached to each of these claims in the sources reviewed this pass] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EF5_drought
- Three broad camps described in the debate: (1) ratings have become too conservative and there have been near-EF5 events; (2) the damage genuinely has not met EF5 criteria in this period; (3) it's case-by-case, likely a mix of both. [unverified — a synthesized framing from secondary aggregation, not a single named source] https://theticker.org/17689/science/2025-enderlin-tornado-upgraded-marking-u-s-s-first-ef5-in-12-years/
- A related post-Enderlin case: the 2026 Enid, Oklahoma tornado has been the subject of similar debate over whether it reached EF5 wind speeds. [unverified — flagged as a developing/adjacent story, not fully researched this pass] https://www.nwahomepage.com/news/enid-oklahoma-ef5-debate/

## 7. Ten candidate retention spikes

1. Cold open on the tanker car — a 32,600 kg (72,000 lb) rail tanker thrown 475 ft — before naming the town or the rating.
2. The reveal that the original rating was EF3, and it took a re-survey four months later to become EF5 — a "the story changed" hook.
3. The 12-year gap framing: showing the last EF5 (Moore, 2013) side by side with Enderlin, and what happened in between (all the near-misses).
4. The engineering pivot — a scientific paper about throwing train cars becoming the deciding evidence, not a flattened house.
5. 24 tornadoes in one state on one night, many from two supercells — scale-of-outbreak visual (radar loop).
6. The human cost against the record-setting frame: three named victims, two of them a couple, one an 89-year-old whose spouse survived.
7. The "EF5 drought" controversy itself — meteorologists disagreeing for over a decade about whether ratings were too conservative, resolved (or not) by this one storm.
8. The debarked, "sandpapered" trees along the Maple River as a visceral, lesser-known EF5 indicator distinct from house damage.
9. The Carl Jones quote — "this particular set of damage... stood out as unique" — as the moment the investigation restarted.
10. The open loop into a follow-on/adjacent story: the 2026 Enid, Oklahoma tornado facing the same EF5 debate, suggesting Enderlin may not be the last word.
