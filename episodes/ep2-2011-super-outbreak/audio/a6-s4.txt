And it was not finished. The tornado left Tuscaloosa, wobbled briefly, and then re-intensified as it bore down on the western suburbs of Birmingham. A million people live in that metro area, and for a terrifying stretch of the evening the storm seemed aimed at the heart of it. Then, in the way tornadoes sometimes spare one place by destroying another, its track bent just enough to miss downtown. In Concord, Pleasant Grove, and McDonald Chapel, it killed twenty more people as a high end EF four. The cameras that had watched it hit Tuscaloosa now watched it march across an entire metro area, live, for an hour and a half. Along a path of nearly eighty-one miles, it destroyed two point four billion dollars of homes, businesses, and infrastructure. That made it the costliest tornado in American history at that moment. And like the death toll record set one hour earlier, that record would stand for less than a month. But the day still was not over. As this storm finally faded northeast of Birmingham, the far corner of Alabama was already producing the fourth EF five of the day. One afternoon. Four of the strongest tornadoes on earth.
