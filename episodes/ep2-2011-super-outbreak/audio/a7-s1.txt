At six nineteen in the evening, the last of the four giants touched down in DeKalb County, in the hill country of northeast Alabama. It moved through Fife and Shiloh and then into Rainsville, producing winds over two hundred miles per hour. The damage there had a signature all its own. An eight hundred pound safe was ripped out of a home's anchoring, carried six hundred feet, and found with its steel door torn off. A school bus was thrown across a highway. The local high school took millions of dollars in damage. Twenty-five people died along a thirty-four mile path before the storm crossed toward Georgia and lifted, just before seven in the evening. Even then, the outbreak refused to end. The same supercell dropped a new EF four on Ringgold, Georgia, where seven people died, and the storm kept killing as it ran on toward Tennessee. Around eight o'clock, another EF four tore through lakeside cabins at Lake Martin, back in Alabama, and seven more people were gone. Through the night the line pushed east, spinning out weaker tornadoes across Tennessee, Georgia, and Virginia, sirens sounding over towns that had already been hit once that day. In the first minutes of April twenty-eighth, a tornado erased the tiny community of Vaughn, Georgia, in under a minute, killing two people. An entire community, gone between one minute and the next, while most of the country was asleep. And then, gradually, mercifully, the atmosphere spent itself. Sunrise found seven states counting their dead. And it found Alabama silent. For the first time in nearly a full day, there was not a single tornado warning anywhere in the state. A broadcaster said it out loud, almost in disbelief. That silence is where the story of the storm ends. It is where the harder story begins.
