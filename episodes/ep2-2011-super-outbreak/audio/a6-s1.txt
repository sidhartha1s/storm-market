At four forty-three in the afternoon, a new supercell dropped a tornado in rural Greene County, southwest of Tuscaloosa. Ahead of it sat a city of ninety thousand people, a third of them students at the University of Alabama. At four fifty, a meteorologist turned videographer named John Oldshue was streaming live when the funnel condensed in front of him. His stream gave broadcasters visual confirmation while the storm was still half an hour from the city, and it has been credited with saving lives ever since. That confirmation matters more than it sounds, because radar has a blind spot. The beam travels in a straight line while the earth curves away beneath it, so at long range a radar can only see thousands of feet above the ground. A camera on the ground closes that gap. The National Weather Service declared a tornado emergency, the rarest and most severe wording it has. And an entire state watched live on television as a wedge tornado, growing toward a mile wide, walked toward its fifth largest city in broad daylight. On the air, meteorologist James Spann said the words Alabama still remembers. This will be a day that will go down in state history. All you can do is pray for those people.
