At about three oh five in the afternoon, a tornado touched down in Marion County, Alabama, and began moving northeast at highway speed. Within minutes it grew from a rope into a wedge over half a mile wide, and its winds climbed to two hundred and ten miles per hour. At around three twenty it reached Hackleburg, a town of roughly fifteen hundred people. Three quarters of the town was destroyed in under two minutes. The high school was flattened. The pharmacy, the grocery store, the churches, gone. And the Wrangler jeans plant, the town's largest employer for generations, was reduced to a debris field. In the days that followed, people found pairs of jeans scattered across fields more than forty miles away. Eighteen people died in Hackleburg alone. No tornado of this strength had struck Alabama since nineteen ninety-eight, and this one was still growing. It crossed county lines without weakening, and its forward speed left the towns ahead almost no time to act. And it was only getting started. The next town in its path had about four minutes.
