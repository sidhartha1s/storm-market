When the government's own investigators later wrote their assessment of this day, they documented exactly what that first wave destroyed. Across that single day, more than one hundred high tension transmission towers were destroyed, most of them across northern Alabama, and the power began failing hours before the worst storms arrived. And the NOAA weather radio network, the backbone of the warning system, began to fail with it. Six transmitters across the region went dark, the first of them before dawn, and in some areas the power never came back before the afternoon storms moved in. Think about what that means. The sirens in many towns had no power behind them. The weather radios that should have screamed in kitchens and bedrooms were silent. Televisions were dark in the living rooms that sat directly in the path. The afternoon warnings would be issued on time, with better lead time than almost any event in history. But north Alabama walked into the most violent hours of the outbreak with much of its warning voice already gone. The official report lays this out in tables and transmitter call signs, listing which counties lost their radio coverage and at what hour. Almost no telling of this day mentions it, and it should be the starting point of every one. Keep it in mind through everything you are about to see, because it explains so much of what the numbers alone cannot. At half past two that afternoon, in Mississippi, the second act began.
