The average tornado warning that day arrived twenty-two minutes early, and nearly every violent tornado was warned. But in the region around Birmingham, in the years leading up to the outbreak, eighty-three percent of tornado warnings had been false alarms. People had learned to wait for proof before they moved. And the storms themselves had cut power across whole counties and, one by one, silenced six weather radio transmitters, so for thousands of families, proof never arrived at all. And of one hundred and thirty-nine deaths the investigators studied closely, eighty-six happened in ordinary, well built, permanent homes. The kind of homes we are told are safe. Above ground, against two hundred mile per hour winds, they are not. The system learned from this. Within weeks of the outbreak, the first dual polarization radar in the national network went operational, technology that can see airborne debris itself and confirm a tornado in the dark, without waiting for human eyes to find it. By twenty thirteen, the entire national fleet had been upgraded. Researchers dug into the false alarm problem, and into how people actually behave in the minutes after a warning, because the outbreak had proved that the physics was only half of the problem. The other half is human. A warning is not a sound. It is a decision waiting to be made, and every person in the path has to make it with the information they have. On April twenty-seventh, twenty-two minutes was enough time for almost anyone to reach safety. What too many people did not have was a working way to hear the message, a reason to believe it, or a safe place to go. The machines improved. The lesson about that last mile was paid for in lives.
