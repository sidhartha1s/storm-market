Phil Campbell took the core of the storm at full strength, and twenty-one people died there. For a town that size, that is more than one in every hundred residents. The survey teams that came afterward documented things that are hard to write in an official report. A twenty-five foot section of asphalt road, torn out of the ground and thrown a third of a mile. Home foundations swept so clean that surveyors struggled to tell where houses had stood. This is what engineers mean by EF five. It is not a bigger version of an ordinary tornado. It is a different kind of event, one where brick, concrete, and pavement stop mattering. The advice we give people, get to an interior room, put walls between you and the storm, quietly assumes the walls will still be there. In the core of a storm like this one, that assumption fails, and there is no meaningful shelter above ground at all. The tornado ran on through Lawrence County, where fourteen more people died, then past Mount Hope, Tanner, and Harvest. It did not leave the ground for one hundred and thirty-two miles. Seventy-two people were killed along that single track. It was the deadliest single American tornado since nineteen fifty-five. And that terrible record stood for exactly twenty-five days, until a city in Missouri called Joplin.
