The first EF five of the outbreak touched down at about two thirty in the afternoon near Philadelphia, Mississippi. It carved a twenty-nine mile path across four counties, with winds around two hundred and five miles per hour. In places it dug trenches two feet deep into open ground, peeling away the earth itself. Three people were killed, and the number stayed that low largely because the path threaded between towns, through timber and farmland that recorded the storm's full violence without offering it many victims. And here is the strange thing about this tornado. Almost nobody remembers it. An EF five is supposed to be a once in a generation event. At that moment, America had not seen one anywhere in almost three years. In any other year, this storm would have led every national newscast and been studied for a decade. On April twenty-seventh it barely made the evening news, because by sunset it was not even among the three worst tornadoes of the day. Meteorologists sometimes describe it as the warning shot. It proved the atmosphere could now produce the strongest category of tornado on earth, and it proved it before three o'clock, with the day's fuel still building. The storms lining up behind it were stronger, and the towns in their path had names that nobody outside the region had ever heard. Hackleburg. Phil Campbell. Smithville. Within about ninety minutes, all three would become famous for the worst possible reason.
