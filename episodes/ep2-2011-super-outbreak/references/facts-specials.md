Verified 2026-07-26. Provenance ladder used: primary agency records (NRC, NWS/SPC) > news archives (AP/Reuters/local outlets at the time) > secondary tornado-database sites. Each item below is CONFIRMED / CORRECTED / UNVERIFIABLE with the number the script should use and the citation.

## 1. Browns Ferry Nuclear Plant — CONFIRMED (with corrections to the draft assumption)

**What actually happened:**
- At **5:01 PM CDT, April 27, 2011**, all three Browns Ferry reactors automatically **scrammed** (shut down) after a tornado knocked out transmission lines, causing loss of offsite (grid) power to the plant.
- The tornado crossed the Tennessee River into Limestone County roughly **4.8 km (~3 miles) from the plant** — it did not hit the plant directly, but destroyed/damaged the transmission infrastructure feeding it.
- **Seven of the plant's eight emergency diesel generators** started and carried the plant's safety and cooling loads. A small backup 161kV "Athens line" also remained available intermittently.
- TVA declared an **NRC "Notification of Unusual Event"** — the *lowest* of the NRC's four emergency classifications (Unusual Event → Alert → Site Area Emergency → General Emergency). This is an **NRC-specific scale, not INES** (the International Nuclear Event Scale is an IAEA scale rarely applied to U.S. events of this type; no INES rating was assigned). The declaration was triggered specifically because loss of normal+alternate offsite power exceeded 15 minutes per NRC reporting rules.
- The Unusual Event was **not closed until offsite power was fully restored on Monday, May 2, 2011** — meaning the plant ran on emergency diesel power for parts of **~5 days** total following the storm (some offsite capacity was intermittently available before full restoration).
- No radiological release, no reactor damage, no injury to public or staff — TVA and NRC both confirmed safety systems performed as designed.

**Script-usable number:** "All three reactors scrammed when the storm knocked out the power lines. Diesel generators kept the reactors cool for the better part of a week — until May 2 — before offsite power was fully restored." Avoid claiming a specific "3 days" or similar without hedging; multiple sources describe a five-day span between declaration and stand-down, with generators as primary or backup power intermittently through that window.

## 2. Dual-Polarization Radar Rollout — CONFIRMED

- The national NEXRAD (WSR-88D) network upgrade to **dual-polarization** was a ~$60 million, congressionally funded program. Site-by-site upgrades began in **2011**; the **first operational dual-pol radar went live in May 2011** — i.e., essentially *simultaneous with, not caused by,* the outbreak (April 27, 2011 predates that first operational conversion by about a month). The **entire NEXRAD fleet (~160 radars) was fully converted to dual-pol by the end of 2013**.
- **No standard NWS NEXRAD radar covering the outbreak was operating in dual-pol mode on April 27, 2011.** However, **research/experimental dual-pol radars were active and did collect data during the outbreak**: the University of Alabama in Huntsville's **ARMOR** (C-band dual-pol) radar and the **Mobile Alabama X-band (MAX)** dual-pol radar were both operating and used afterward in post-event scientific analysis of the storms (debris signatures, etc.). The NWS's own operational KOUN test radar in Norman, OK had been demonstrating dual-pol capability since the 2002-03 Joint Polarization Experiment (JPOLE), but that's a research asset, not a warning-operations radar.
- **What dual-pol adds:** it measures both horizontal and vertical pulse returns, letting forecasters see debris being lofted by a tornado — the "tornadic debris signature" (TDS) — which is near-definitive confirmation a tornado is on the ground and doing damage, distinct from rain/hail signal alone.
- The outbreak is frequently cited in retrospectives as reinforcing the case for accelerating the already-funded rollout, but the timeline (upgrade began in 2011 on a schedule set by the 2010 budget) shows the program was already underway before April 27 — it was not launched *because of* the outbreak.

**Script-usable framing:** "Dual-polarization radar was rolling out across the country that very year — the first upgraded radar went live just weeks after the outbreak — but on April 27 itself, forecasters were mostly working with older single-polarization radar. Research radars nearby did capture debris signatures that would soon become standard equipment nationwide."

**Sources:**

- [AMS Journals — Early Performance Evaluation of NEXRAD Dual-Pol Rainfall Estimates (2013)](https://journals.ametsoc.org/waf/article/28/6/1478/39824/An-Early-Performance-Evaluation-of-the-NEXRAD-Dual)

## 3. Wrangler / VF Corporation Hackleburg Plant — CONFIRMED USABLE (with the full arc)

This is a clean, positive-arc fact — usable as written, with the fuller timeline:
- The Wrangler distribution/sewing center in Hackleburg, AL was **destroyed** by the EF5 Hackleburg–Phil Campbell tornado on April 27, 2011, displacing about **150 workers**.
- VF Corporation did **not** decide immediately — a July 2011 report noted the rebuild was still undecided. **In August 2011, VF Corp announced it would rebuild**, committing to grow employment from **150 to 200 jobs**.
- During the roughly two-year rebuild, displaced employees were shuttled to a Holly Pond, AL facility and a smaller interim Hackleburg sewing plant so no jobs were permanently lost in the interim.
- The new facility broke ground and was completed, with an **official reopening ceremony in 2013** — Alabama Governor Robert Bentley and VF Corp CEO Eric Wiseman cut the ribbon. Local coverage frames it explicitly as "a symbol of home" / recovery for the town.

**Script-usable number:** "VF Corporation, Wrangler's parent company, rebuilt the plant and reopened it in 2013 — growing employment from 150 to 200 jobs, a rare case of a small town's biggest employer choosing to come back bigger."

## 4. Warning-Response Science — CONFIRMED, with the closest circa-2011 figure identified

**False alarm rate:** The commonly cited "~75-80%" figure is well-supported but not a single clean number — it varies by study/window:
- A national climatology (2000–2004 data) found false alarm rates around **80% for the smallest counties** (<1000 km²).
- **NWS Birmingham (the office that covered much of this outbreak) reported an 83% false alarm rate for itself, October 2007–March 2011** — i.e., in the years immediately preceding the outbreak, essentially validating the "~80%" figure specifically for the region the documentary covers.
- Broader NWS national tornado-warning stats around 2011 show **Probability of Detection ≈ 70%, False Alarm Ratio ≈ 76%**.
- **Best script-usable, sourced number: NWS Birmingham's own pre-outbreak false alarm rate of 83% (Oct 2007–Mar 2011)** — directly tied to the region and just before the event, from NWS's own published research page.

**Fatality pattern (permanent homes):** CONFIRMED as stated in the brief, sourced to NWS's own outbreak tally:
- **139 direct fatalities** are broken down as: **86 in permanent (site-built) homes/structures, 46 in manufactured/mobile homes, 2 in vehicles, 2 outdoors.**
- This confirms the "unusually high share of victims were in permanent homes" framing — about **62% of the fatalities counted here were in permanent structures**, which is notable because manufactured homes are usually the disproportionate killer in most tornado events; this outbreak's violence (many long-track EF4/EF5s) was strong enough to kill at scale even in well-built, permanent housing.

**Script-usable framing:** "Even before this outbreak, the NWS office covering much of the path had an 83% false-alarm rate on its tornado warnings — 'cry wolf' fatigue was a real, quantified problem. And unlike most tornado disasters, this one killed people overwhelmingly in permanent homes, not just mobile homes: 86 of the 139 direct deaths were in site-built houses."

## 5. EF-Scale Wind Estimation & Tuscaloosa's EF4 Rating — CONFIRMED, crisp explainer available

**How ratings are assigned:**
- The Enhanced Fujita Scale (operational since Feb 1, 2007) rates tornadoes not by direct wind measurement but by **damage survey**. Assessors compare observed damage to **28 standardized Damage Indicators (DIs)** — types of structures/objects (single-family homes, mobile homes, schools, hardwood/softwood trees, transmission towers, etc.) — each with its own **Degree of Damage (DoD)** scale, roughly 8 levels per indicator, describing exactly what failure looks like at each wind speed band (e.g., "roof deck removed" vs. "exterior walls collapsed" vs. "structure leveled, swept away").
- Each DoD maps to an estimated 3-second gust wind speed range from NIST/wind-engineering studies. The **overall tornado rating is set by the highest, best-supported DoD observed anywhere along the path** — not an average, and it must be corroborated by construction quality/anchoring, not just "the roof came off."
- **EF5 (>200 mph) is intentionally open-ended** — there is no upper bound specified, partly so that media and public don't fixate on a "maximum" number, and because wind speeds above that level are not reliably distinguishable by damage alone with current engineering knowledge.

**Why Tuscaloosa stayed EF4:**
- The Tuscaloosa–Birmingham tornado (April 27, 2011) was rated **EF4, ~190 mph peak winds, 80.68-mile path, 64-65 fatalities on that track**.
- Post-event analyses (including detailed aerial-photo reviews) found genuinely EF5-caliber damage in isolated spots — e.g., an apartment complex nearly leveled and a nearby home swept clean off its foundation — but the survey teams concluded the **corroborating indicators, notably tree damage (denuding/debarking) and the broader consistency of construction-quality evidence, did not support an EF5 designation across the path**. Because EF ratings require a defensible, engineering-grounded consensus across multiple damage indicators (not just the single worst-looking site), the survey settled on EF4 despite genuine "shades of gray" debate among analysts — which is exactly why this is still cited as one of the field's most contested individual ratings.

**Script-usable framing:** "Tornado ratings aren't measured in real time — they're reconstructed afterward from the wreckage, using a checklist of 28 building and tree types, each with its own ladder of failure. EF5, the top rating, has no upper wind limit at all — because past 200 miles an hour, damage alone can't tell you exactly how much faster the wind was blowing. Tuscaloosa's tornado had pockets of damage that looked EF5 — an apartment block leveled, a house swept to its slab — but the survey team couldn't find that same level of damage consistently enough, especially in the trees, to rate the whole path EF5. It stayed EF4. To this day, meteorologists still argue about that call."

## 6. Synoptic Setup, April 27, 2011 — CONFIRMED, usable numbers identified

- A powerful surface low tracked northeastward across the Mississippi/Tennessee Valleys through the day, deepening under a strongly amplified **500mb trough**.
- A very strong **mid-level jet of 80–100 knots** moved into the Ohio/Tennessee Valley region behind the trough, providing exceptional deep-layer shear.
- **CAPE (instability):** widely cited estimates put afternoon mixed-layer CAPE in the **2,000–3,000 J/kg** range over Louisiana/southern Mississippi/southern Tennessee Valley on the 27th (with even higher values, ~3,000–4,000 J/kg, over the ArkLaTex region the evening before, April 26).
- **Helicity:** storm-relative helicity values of roughly **450–600 m²/s²** were present in the most favorable corridor — values strongly associated with long-track, violent (EF4/EF5) tornadoes.
- **Deep-layer shear:** roughly **50–80 knots of 0–6 km bulk shear** across the most active corridor.
- **Temperatures/moisture:** surface temperatures ranged from the **70s°F up to the low 90s°F**, with ample Gulf moisture feeding the instability.
- This combination — extreme shear/helicity paired with strong-to-extreme instability, an amplified jet, and a fast-moving surface low — is the textbook "high-end, classic" tornado-outbreak setup meteorologists still reference as a benchmark case.

**Script-usable framing:** "The atmosphere that day had almost everything lined up at once: CAPE — the fuel for thunderstorm updrafts — pushing 3,000 joules per kilogram in spots; wind shear of 50 to 80 knots twisting those storms into rotation; and helicity values of 450 to 600, the kind of number that correlates with long-track, violent tornadoes. A jet stream howling at 80 to 100 knots overhead sealed it. It's still used as a textbook case of a maximized tornado environment."

**Sources:**
- [Jon Davies Severe Weather Notes — 27 April 2011: An extraordinary environment for tornadoes](http://davieswx.blogspot.com/2011/05/27-april-2011-extraordinary-environment.html)
- [Wikipedia — Meteorological history of the 2011 Super Outbreak (aggregates SPC mesoanalysis figures)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorological_history_of_the_2011_Super_Outbreak)
- [SPC — Severe Weather Event Review for Wednesday, April 27, 2011](https://www.spc.noaa.gov/exper/archive/event.php?date=20110427)
- [Alabama Weather Network — Analysis of the Super Outbreak (2011)](https://www.alabamawx.com/?p=160723)

**Note:** for the final script, pull the exact SPC mesoanalysis archive plots (spc.noaa.gov/exper/archive) directly if you want a specific hour/location value rather than the range figures above — the range figures are well-supported for narration but a single "at 5 PM CDT over Tuscaloosa" number would need a direct SPC mesoanalysis pull, which the sources above don't provide at that granularity.

## 7. 911 Audio Availability — UNVERIFIABLE as a ready-made source list; usable leads only

I could **not confirm a specific, ready-to-license archive of April 27, 2011 dispatch/911 audio** analogous to the well-known Joplin, MO tornado recordings (which *are* publicly available and widely used in documentaries). Findings:
- **Confirmed volume, not confirmed public audio:** Huntsville-Madison County's 911 center took **~1,700 calls that day**, including **~500 calls in the single hour between 4–5 PM** as the storm hit — reported by WHNT, but the article is about dispatcher workload/technology, not a released recording.
- Individual dispatcher first-person accounts exist (e.g., Michael Posey, Fort Payne PD dispatcher) but these are interviews, not raw audio releases.
- **By contrast, the Joplin, MO tornado (May 2011, one month later) does have a well-documented public 911/dispatch audio release**, covered by the Joplin Globe — that could be a candidate if the documentary widens scope, but it is a different event/date and would need to be flagged as such on screen.
- Existing April 27, 2011 documentaries I could identify by title (WBRC's "April's Fury," PBS Independent Lens interactive "After the Storm," Rogue Earth's "Alabama's April 27th Tornado Nightmare" on YouTube) rely primarily on **news helicopter/ground footage, storm-chaser video, and survivor interviews** rather than released 911 audio — none of the search results confirmed embedded dispatch-call audio in these.

**Recommendation:** Do not assume usable 911 audio exists for this outbreak without direct outreach. Concrete next steps for a researcher/producer to actually source this:
1. **FOIA/public-records request** directly to the specific county E-911 boards along the path (Tuscaloosa County E-911, Jefferson County E-911/Birmingham, Madison County 911 Huntsville, Marion/Franklin County (Hackleburg) dispatch) — Alabama's public records law generally makes 911 audio obtainable, though retention windows for 2011 calls may have lapsed at some agencies.
2. **News station archives**: WBRC (Birmingham), WHNT (Huntsville), and WAFF (Huntsville) all did contemporaneous 2011 coverage referencing dispatch center operations — their newsroom archives (not necessarily public) are the most likely holders of any broadcast-cleared dispatch audio.
3. **archive.org**: no confirmed hit found in this pass; would need a direct archive.org catalog search (not performed here — recommend a follow-up query directly against archive.org's search, since general web search does not reliably surface archive.org holdings).
4. If April 27 audio proves unobtainable, the **Joplin, MO tornado (May 22, 2011)** dispatch audio is a confirmed, precedented public source other documentaries have used — but using it would require the film to either broaden scope to "Spring 2011 tornado season" or clearly label it as a different, related event.
