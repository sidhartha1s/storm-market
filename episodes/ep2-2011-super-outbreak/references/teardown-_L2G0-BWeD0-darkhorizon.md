## 5. Notable Techniques (worth stealing or noting)

- **Found-footage bookending:** opens AND revisits the same "day that will go down in state history" TV chopper/anchor line twice — once cold-open (decontextualized), once in full context at the Tuscaloosa segment. This callback rewards close viewers and reinforces the video's central "day" framing.
- **Micro-embedded quotes as connective tissue:** raw chaser/spotter audio snippets ("Slow down, slow down," "This is not good, guys, pull up," "It's gone") are dropped every 60-90 seconds throughout the disaster act — these do a lot of pacing work, breaking up narrator VO without needing new b-roll setups.
- **The "march" structure:** strictly chronological town-by-town progression by exact timestamp (2:28 p.m. → 2:30 p.m. → 3:00 p.m. → 3:47 p.m. → 4:43 p.m.) — gives the illusion of live/real-time unfolding and lets each new timestamp function as a mini cliffhanger ("but this was just the beginning").
- **Scale-translation devices:** several stats are converted to vivid physical images rather than left as raw numbers — jeans found 40 miles away, an SUV crushed to "a few feet across," debris falling from the sky over a city 20 miles distant, pavement torn up and found a third of a mile off. This is the "translate every stat to human scale" instinct our own formula calls for, and it's the strongest technique in the video.
- **Escalating superlative stacking:** "the deadliest tornado in Alabama history" → "the costliest single tornado in American history at that time" → "first time 4 EF5s had occurred in a single day since records were kept" — layers multiple distinct records rather than resting on one.
- **Zoom-out act after the ground-level march:** after four consecutive town-level tracks, the video pulls back to state/national aggregate stats (62 in AL in 18 hrs vs. the annual average of 50) — this recontextualizes everything just narrated at a bigger scale, which lands well structurally.
- **Rhetorical "each one had a name" closing device:** repetition of "Each one... each one... each one..." as an anaphora closer, common eulogy technique, cheap to produce but effective.
