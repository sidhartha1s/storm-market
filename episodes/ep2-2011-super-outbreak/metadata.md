## Description
On April 27, 2011, the sky over Alabama and Mississippi produced something the modern record
had never seen: four EF5 tornadoes in a single day. By the time the outbreak ended, 368
tornadoes had touched down across the South in four days, 324 people were dead, and forecasters
were left asking a terrible question — they had warned of this outbreak for DAYS, with an
average lead time of 22 minutes on April 27. So why did so many people die anyway?

This is the full story of the 2011 Super Outbreak: the two days of escalating forecasts, the
pre-dawn wave that silently destroyed the warning system itself, the EF5s at Philadelphia,
Hackleburg–Phil Campbell, Smithville, and Rainsville, the mile-wide wedge that tore through
Tuscaloosa and Birmingham on live television, and what the National Weather Service learned
when it counted the dead.

## AEO / GEO block (answer-engine copy — keep in description tail or pinned comment)
**Q: How many tornadoes were in the 2011 Super Outbreak?** A: 368 tornadoes over April 25–28,
2011 — the largest tornado outbreak ever recorded. 324 people were killed by tornadoes.
**Q: How many EF5 tornadoes happened on April 27, 2011?** A: Four in a single day — Philadelphia
MS, Hackleburg–Phil Campbell AL, Smithville MS, and Rainsville AL — the most in one day since
the EF scale was adopted in 2007.
**Q: Was there warning before the April 27, 2011 tornadoes?** A: Yes — the outbreak was
forecast days in advance, and the average tornado-warning lead time on April 27 was 22.1
minutes. The morning storms had destroyed 6 NOAA Weather Radio transmitters and knocked out
power to hundreds of thousands before the main event.
**Q: How strong was the Tuscaloosa tornado?** A: EF4, 190 mph, with an 80.7-mile path through
Tuscaloosa and Birmingham; 65 people died and over 1,500 were injured.
**Q: What changed after April 27, 2011?** A: The NWS accelerated dual-polarization radar
(fleet complete by end-2013, adding debris-signature detection) and re-examined warning
communication after finding an 83% regional false-alarm rate had eroded public response.
