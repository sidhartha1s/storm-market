# SCRIPT (TTS-safe prose — resolved fact ledger only; segments parsed by scripts/tts_episode.py)

### A0-S2 — narration
> It is late afternoon on April twenty-seventh, two thousand eleven. A tornado more than a mile wide is on the ground in Alabama, and it has been for over an hour. The warning went out twenty-two minutes before it arrived, and that is better lead time than almost any storm in history. And yet, right now, people are dying inside of it. By midnight, this will be the deadliest tornado day in the modern American record. But the hardest question of this day will not be what went wrong with the forecast.

### A0-S3 — narration
> Because the forecast was nearly perfect. Over four days, more than three hundred and sixty tornadoes will tear through the South, and three hundred and twenty-four people will not survive them. Four of those tornadoes will earn the highest rating that exists, EF five, all in a single day. That has never happened in the era of the modern scale, before or since. This is the story of the twenty eleven Super Outbreak. And it is the story of why nearly perfect warnings were not enough.

### A1-S1 — narration
> The outbreak did not begin in Alabama. It began two days earlier, on April twenty-fifth, in Arkansas. That afternoon, forecasters issued a rare kind of tornado watch, one flagged as a particularly dangerous situation, covering four states. And that evening a tornado dropped out of the darkness onto Vilonia, a small town north of Little Rock. It ground through neighborhoods street by street, staying on the ground far longer than most tornadoes ever manage. Four people were killed and sixteen more were injured, and dozens of other tornadoes touched down across the region before midnight. On an ordinary week, that would have been the disaster. The forecasters watching the pattern understood that it was only the opening act, because the ingredients were not being used up. They were building. On April twenty-sixth the storms came again, more than fifty reports in a single day, and still the atmosphere kept loading. By that night, the forecasters at the Storm Prediction Center were staring at a setup for the twenty-seventh unlike anything most of them had ever worked. To understand what they were seeing, you need to know what a violent tornado actually requires. Because on the afternoon of April twenty-seventh, everything it requires arrived at once, over one of the most vulnerable stretches of the American South.

### A1-S2 — graphic with VO
> A violent tornado is built from three things. The first is fuel. Warm, humid air near the ground, and meteorologists measure that fuel in joules per kilogram. On an ordinary stormy day in the South, the number might reach one thousand. On April twenty-seventh it climbed past two thousand, and in places past three thousand, air so buoyant it wanted to launch upward like a balloon cut loose. The second ingredient is a lid. A thin layer of stable air that holds the fuel down for hours, so it builds instead of burning off as scattered showers. And the third is spin. Winds that change speed and direction as you go up, so that any storm punching through the lid begins to rotate. That day, the jet stream roared overhead at up to one hundred knots while moist Gulf air raced north underneath it. The measure of spin that forecasters track, called helicity, reached values between four hundred and fifty and six hundred. Any one of those numbers would get a forecaster's attention. All of them together are almost never seen. Separately, these ingredients make thunderstorms. Together, they make supercells that rotate like machines, and they had all day to work.

### A1-S3 — narration
> The Storm Prediction Center saw it coming with stunning clarity. At seven fifty-five that morning they upgraded the day to high risk, the highest category that exists, reserved for a handful of days in a decade. By late morning their charts showed a forty-five percent chance of a tornado near any given point across a corridor from Mississippi into Alabama. Numbers like that are almost never printed. But the forecasters knew something the public did not. Even a perfect setup can fail. Five years earlier, in April of two thousand six, a similar high risk day had collapsed into a messy line of storms, and the violent tornadoes never came. Too much fuel, spent too quickly, can choke the very supercells it should feed. So the question that morning was not whether storms were coming. The question was what shape they would take. A messy line of storms, and the day would be bad. Discrete, isolated supercells, each one free to breathe in that loaded atmosphere, and the day would be historic. The first answer arrived while most of Alabama was still asleep. And in a cruel piece of irony, that early answer would end up making the real one so much deadlier.

### A2-S1 — narration
> In the hours before dawn on April twenty-seventh, a violent line of storms swept across Alabama in the dark. It spun out more than two dozen tornadoes before eight in the morning, killed three people, and injured more than forty. Television meteorologists had been on the air since the middle of the night, tracking damage county by county. In Birmingham, James Spann joined his station's coverage before sunrise, telling viewers that many of them were waking up to the sound of sirens already going off. It was not yet six in the morning, and it was already one of the worst weather days of the year. And then the line moved on, and the sun came up, and the sky over Alabama turned quiet and strangely warm. To millions of people it felt like the storm had already come and gone. It had not. The atmosphere behind it was reloading for something far worse. But that morning line had already done something that would quietly shape the entire day. Something almost nobody noticed at the time, and something almost nobody talks about even now.

### A2-S2 — narration
> When the government's own investigators later wrote their assessment of this day, they documented exactly what that first wave destroyed. The morning storms tore down more than one hundred electrical transmission towers across northern Alabama. Hundreds of thousands of homes lost power before noon. And six NOAA weather radio transmitters, the backbone of the warning system, were knocked off the air across the very counties the afternoon storms would hit. Think about what that means. The sirens in many towns had no power behind them. The weather radios that should have screamed in kitchens and bedrooms were silent. Televisions were dark in the living rooms that sat directly in the path. The afternoon warnings would be issued on time, with better lead time than almost any event in history. But north Alabama walked into the most violent hours of the outbreak partly deaf. The official report lays this out in tables and transmitter call signs, listing which counties lost their radio coverage and at what hour. Almost no telling of this day mentions it, and it should be the starting point of every one. Keep it in mind through everything you are about to see, because it explains so much of what the numbers alone cannot. At half past two that afternoon, in Mississippi, the second act began.

### A3-S1 — narration
> The first EF five of the outbreak touched down at about two thirty in the afternoon near Philadelphia, Mississippi. It carved a twenty-nine mile path across four counties, with winds around two hundred and five miles per hour. In places it dug trenches two feet deep into open ground, peeling away the earth itself. Three people were killed, and the number stayed that low largely because the path threaded between towns, through timber and farmland that recorded the storm's full violence without offering it many victims. And here is the strange thing about this tornado. Almost nobody remembers it. An EF five is supposed to be a once in a generation event. At that moment, America had not seen one anywhere in almost three years. In any other year, this storm would have led every national newscast and been studied for a decade. On April twenty-seventh it barely made the evening news, because by sunset it was not even among the three worst tornadoes of the day. Meteorologists sometimes describe it as the warning shot. It proved the atmosphere could now produce the strongest category of tornado on earth, and it proved it before three o'clock, with the day's fuel still building. The storms lining up behind it were stronger, and the towns in their path had names that nobody outside the region had ever heard. Hackleburg. Phil Campbell. Smithville. Within about ninety minutes, all three would become famous for the worst possible reason.

### A4-S1 — narration
> At about three oh five in the afternoon, a tornado touched down in Marion County, Alabama, and began moving northeast at highway speed. Within minutes it grew from a rope into a wedge over half a mile wide, and its winds climbed to two hundred and ten miles per hour. At around three twenty it reached Hackleburg, a town of roughly fifteen hundred people. Three quarters of the town was destroyed in under two minutes. The high school was flattened. The pharmacy, the grocery store, the churches, gone. And the Wrangler jeans plant, the town's largest employer for generations, was reduced to a debris field. In the days that followed, people found pairs of jeans scattered across fields more than forty miles away. Eighteen people died in Hackleburg alone. No tornado of this strength had struck Alabama since nineteen ninety-eight, and this one was still growing. It crossed county lines without weakening, and its forward speed left the towns ahead almost no time to act. And it was only getting started. The next town in its path had about four minutes.

### A4-S2 — narration
> Phil Campbell took the core of the storm at full strength, and twenty-one people died there. For a town that size, that is more than one in every hundred residents. The survey teams that came afterward documented things that are hard to write in an official report. A twenty-five foot section of asphalt road, torn out of the ground and thrown a third of a mile. Home foundations swept so clean that surveyors struggled to tell where houses had stood. This is what engineers mean by EF five. It is not a bigger version of an ordinary tornado. It is a different kind of event, one where brick, concrete, and pavement stop mattering. The tornado ran on through Lawrence County, where fourteen more people died, then past Mount Hope, Tanner, and Harvest. It did not leave the ground for one hundred and thirty-two miles. Seventy-two people were killed along that single track, making it the deadliest American tornado since nineteen fifty-five. It would hold that terrible record for exactly twenty-five days, until a city in Missouri called Joplin.

### A4-S3 — graphic with VO
> On radar, this storm was a textbook nightmare. The archived scans show a hook curling around a ball of lofted debris, the signature of a tornado grinding through structures, and we have replotted the original radar data so you can watch it form. But this storm left one more mark that almost nobody talks about. Near the end of its run it cut the transmission lines feeding Browns Ferry, one of the largest nuclear power plants in America. At five oh one in the evening, with the tornado passing about three miles away, all three reactors scrammed, shutting themselves down as the grid around them died. Seven of the plant's eight emergency diesel generators picked up the cooling load. And they had to, because outside power was not fully restored for five days. The operators declared an official emergency notification to federal regulators. Alabama came one bad afternoon away from a very different kind of disaster story. But to follow the day in order, we have to step back forty minutes. Because while this giant was still on the ground, the third EF five of the day had already come and gone in Mississippi.

### A5-S1 — narration
> At three forty-two in the afternoon, a tornado formed just southwest of Smithville, Mississippi, a town of well under a thousand people. Within minutes it was producing damage at the very top of the scale. Winds reached two hundred and five miles per hour, and the funnel grew to three quarters of a mile wide, wider than the town it was aimed at. Smithville had a water tower, a city hall, a police station, and four churches. In about one minute it lost nearly all of them. One hundred and seventeen buildings were destroyed. The ground itself was scoured a foot deep in places. And what this tornado did to heavy objects is still cited in engineering circles. A Ford Explorer was lifted off the ground, thrown half a mile, and slammed into the top of the water tower, then thrown another quarter mile beyond it. In the cemetery, granite tombstones were rotated in place to face the opposite direction. They were left standing, but turned, as if the whole graveyard had looked away. Twenty-three people died along the path, seventeen of them in Smithville itself. No tornado this violent had struck Mississippi since nineteen sixty-six. The storm faded after about thirty-five miles, leaving a question that surveyors still use it to teach. What do you call a storm that picks up a car and throws it at a water tower? The scale says EF five. Smithville says the town that existed at three forty-one was gone by four o'clock. And the worst hour of the day had still not started.

### A6-S1 — narration
> At four forty-three in the afternoon, a new supercell dropped a tornado in rural Greene County, southwest of Tuscaloosa. Ahead of it sat a city of ninety thousand people, a third of them students at the University of Alabama. At four fifty, a meteorologist turned videographer named John Oldshue was streaming live when the funnel condensed in front of him. His stream gave broadcasters visual confirmation while the storm was still half an hour from the city, and it has been credited with saving lives ever since. The National Weather Service declared a tornado emergency, the rarest and most severe wording it has. And an entire state watched live on television as a wedge tornado, growing toward a mile wide, walked toward its fifth largest city in broad daylight. On the air, meteorologist James Spann said the words Alabama still remembers. This will be a day that will go down in state history. All you can do is pray for those people.

### A6-S2 — narration
> Just after five o'clock the tornado entered the city. It crossed Thirty-fifth Street, then McFarland Boulevard, then the crowded neighborhoods along Fifteenth Street, moving at nearly fifty miles per hour. It gutted apartment complexes full of students. It destroyed the county's own emergency management headquarters, the building responsible for coordinating the response to it. In roughly six minutes, whole business districts ceased to exist. The mayor, Walt Maddox, would later say that about twelve percent of Tuscaloosa was simply gone, and that seven thousand people lost their jobs in those six minutes. Debris rode the storm's updraft and fell out of the sky over Birmingham, more than forty miles away. People there found photographs, checks, and report cards from Tuscaloosa lying in their yards. And still it kept moving, out through Holt and the lake communities east of the city, grinding along the ground at a pace that outran anyone trying to flee by car. Sixty-five people died along this tornado's path, six of them students at the university, and more than fifteen hundred were injured. Before this story ends, we will come back to one of them.

### A6-S3 — graphic with VO
> The Tuscaloosa tornado is rated EF four, and that rating makes people angry to this day. So it is worth explaining how ratings actually work. Nobody measures a tornado's winds directly. Instead, survey engineers walk the path afterward with a catalog of twenty-eight damage indicators, from mobile homes to hospitals, each with defined degrees of damage. The final rating comes from the worst damage the evidence can firmly support. Tuscaloosa showed flashes of top end violence, including homes swept from their slabs. But the surrounding indicators did not corroborate that level consistently, so the official rating stayed at EF four, one hundred and ninety mile per hour winds. The scale measures what a storm did to whatever it happened to hit, not what it was capable of. Which means an EF four through a city can kill far more people than an EF five through open farmland. This one did.

### A6-S4 — narration
> And it was not finished. The tornado left Tuscaloosa, wobbled briefly, and then re-intensified as it bore down on the western suburbs of Birmingham. In Concord, Pleasant Grove, and McDonald Chapel, it killed twenty more people as a high end EF four. The cameras that had watched it hit Tuscaloosa now watched it march across an entire metro area, live, for an hour and a half. Along a path of nearly eighty-one miles, it destroyed two point four billion dollars of homes, businesses, and infrastructure. That made it the costliest tornado in American history at that moment. And like the death toll record set one hour earlier, that record would stand for less than a month. But the day still was not over. As this storm finally faded northeast of Birmingham, the far corner of Alabama was already producing the fourth EF five of the day. One afternoon. Four of the strongest tornadoes on earth.

### A7-S1 — narration
> At six nineteen in the evening, the last of the four giants touched down in DeKalb County, in the hill country of northeast Alabama. It moved through Fife and Shiloh and then into Rainsville, producing winds over two hundred miles per hour. The damage there had a signature all its own. An eight hundred pound safe was ripped out of a home's anchoring, carried six hundred feet, and found with its steel door torn off. A school bus was thrown across a highway. The local high school took millions of dollars in damage. Twenty-five people died along a thirty-four mile path before the storm crossed toward Georgia and lifted, just before seven in the evening. Even then, the outbreak refused to end. The same supercell dropped a new EF four on Ringgold, Georgia, where seven people died, and the storm kept killing as it ran on toward Tennessee. Around eight o'clock, another EF four tore through lakeside cabins at Lake Martin, back in Alabama, and seven more people were gone. Through the night the line pushed east, spinning out weaker tornadoes across Tennessee, Georgia, and Virginia. In the first minutes of April twenty-eighth, a tornado erased the tiny community of Vaughn, Georgia, in under a minute, killing two people. And then, gradually, mercifully, the atmosphere spent itself. Sunrise found seven states counting their dead. And it found Alabama silent. For the first time in nearly a full day, there was not a single tornado warning anywhere in the state. A broadcaster said it out loud, almost in disbelief. That silence is where the story of the storm ends. It is where the harder story begins.

### A8-S1 — narration
> Here are the numbers the government's own records settled on. Over four days, more than three hundred and sixty tornadoes struck the South, the largest outbreak ever recorded. Three hundred and twenty-four people were killed by the tornadoes themselves. On April twenty-seventh alone, three hundred and sixteen people died, two hundred and thirty-four of them in Alabama. The damage passed ten billion dollars. You will find different numbers in other tellings of this day, and they disagree with each other. These come from the National Weather Service's official assessment, written by the people whose job was to understand exactly what happened. And what happened is not that the warnings failed.

### A8-S2 — narration
> The average tornado warning that day arrived twenty-two minutes early, and nearly every violent tornado was warned. But in the region around Birmingham, in the years leading up to the outbreak, eighty-three percent of tornado warnings had been false alarms. People had learned to wait for proof before they moved. That morning, the first wave of storms had cut the power and silenced six weather radio transmitters, so for thousands of families, proof never arrived at all. And of one hundred and thirty-nine deaths the investigators studied closely, eighty-six happened in ordinary, well built, permanent homes. The kind of homes we are told are safe. Above ground, against two hundred mile per hour winds, they are not. The system learned from this. Within weeks, the first dual polarization radar in the national network went operational, technology that can see airborne debris and confirm a tornado in the dark, without waiting for human eyes. By twenty thirteen the entire national fleet had it. The false alarm problem, and the shelter problem, are still being worked on today. The machines improved. The lesson about the last mile, the distance between a warning and a decision, was paid for in lives.

### A8-S3 — narration
> But the numbers were never the real story. Ashley Harrison was twenty-two years old, a senior at Alabama, weeks from graduation, her parents' only child. She died in Tuscaloosa sheltering in a closet, doing exactly what the warnings said to do, while her boyfriend survived beside her. Multiply that story by three hundred and twenty-four. In Tuscaloosa today there is a memorial to that afternoon, honoring the responders who were called by circumstance, burdened by tragedy, and who answered anyway. And in Hackleburg, the town that lost eighteen people and three quarters of its buildings, the Wrangler plant was rebuilt. It reopened in twenty thirteen, with more jobs than it had before the storm. The town chose to exist. That is the other thing April twenty-seventh proved. And it is the thing worth carrying out of this story.

### A9-S1 — narration
> Disasters don't ask permission. But they do send warnings. April twenty-seventh sent them twenty-two minutes early, and the people who knew what those warnings meant, and where they would go, lived to tell it. Learn what yours sounds like, and pick your safe place now, so when it comes, you already know what to do. This was The Warning Hour.
