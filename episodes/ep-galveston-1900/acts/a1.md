## A1 · An absurd delusion
Chapter card: Purpose · show the belief was handed to Galveston by its own weather expert, in print, nine years before the storm.
Chapter card: Question · why would a weatherman write that a storm seriously damaging his city was an absurd delusion?
Chapter card: Reveal · his reasoning: the land to the north, lower than the island, would spread the water.
Chapter card: Visual event · the 16 July 1891 Galveston Daily News page, push to the column; the island elevation map with water spreading north, highest point about 8.7 ft.
Chapter card: Exit hook · one man's opinion has become the ground the city built on, and the Gulf is coming to test it.

> Cline was answering a fear some people in town held, and he did it in one long sentence. The opinion that Galveston will at some time be seriously damaged by some disturbance, he wrote, is simply an absurd delusion. He went further. A fear like that, he said, could only come from the imagination, and not from reasoning.

### A1-S4 — narration
**Picture:** hold the Indianola pin, then cut back to the 1891 column, the clause "as there is too large a territory to the north" lit; then the self-made island elevation graphic, camera tilts north over the bay to the mainland; an animated storm wave rolls in from the Gulf, rises against the island, then spills north and flattens out over the low ground; chip "TOO LARGE A TERRITORY TO THE NORTH". Source line: Isaac Cline, Galveston Daily News, 16 July 1891. [RS] [VE] [PAY] [loop L-intro closes]
> Cline gave his reason in that same sentence, and it was the land behind the island. There was, he wrote, too large a territory to the north, lower than the island, over which the water may spread. In his picture, a storm wave pushing in from the Gulf would run off across that low country and thin out. So, he wrote, it would be impossible for any cyclone, his word for a storm like a hurricane, to build a wave that would badly hurt the city.

Rows: Commons transcription ("as there is too large a territory to the north which is lower than the island, over which the water may spread, it would be impossible for any cyclone to create a storm wave which would materially injure the city"); sources-cline.md §1 ("large, lower-lying territory to the north"); factcheck/a1-r1.md rows 17 to 20; factcheck-E.md H1-S4 row. "Run off ... and thin out" is the writer's plain gloss of "spread" (row 20). "Badly hurt" for "materially injure": same gloss the hook uses ("badly damage", accepted in factcheck-D.md H1-S2). [verify: "cyclone" in 1891 Weather Bureau usage covered hurricanes, so "his word for a storm like a hurricane" is a fair gloss · https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/aboutgloss.shtml]
