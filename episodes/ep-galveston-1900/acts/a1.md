## A1 · An absurd delusion
Chapter card: Purpose · show the belief was handed to Galveston by its own weather expert, in print, nine years before the storm.
Chapter card: Question · why would a weatherman write that a storm seriously damaging his city was an absurd delusion?
Chapter card: Reveal · his reasoning: the land to the north lower than the island would spread the water (and, in later histories, the gently sloping Gulf floor).
Chapter card: Visual event · the 16 July 1891 Galveston Daily News page, push to the column; the island elevation map with water spreading north, highest point about 8.7 ft.
Chapter card: Exit hook · nine years later, the same man ran the office whose job was to warn the city.

### A1-S2 — narration
**Picture:** the column, Cline's sentence lit line by line as it is read; chip "ABSURD DELUSION" on the last phrase; chip "IMAGINATION, NOT REASONING". Source line as A1-S1. On-screen quote held until one human reads the scan (Portal is bot-gated). [VE] [Q] (who were "some"?)
> Cline put it in one long sentence. The opinions held by some who are unacquainted with the actual conditions of things, that Galveston will at some time be seriously damaged by some disturbance, is simply an absurd delusion. And he went further. He wrote that a fear like that can only come from the imagination, and not from reasoning.

### A1-S4 — narration
**Picture:** hold the Indianola pin, then cut back to the 1891 column, the clause "as there is too large a territory to the north" lit. [VE] [Q] (what reason?)
> When I read the whole sentence, the word that stopped me was reasoning. Cline was telling those worried readers that their side was imagination and his side was science. And he didn't leave it at a feeling. Right there in the same sentence, he gave them his reason.

Rows: same Commons transcription ("can only have it's origin in the imagination and not from reasoning"; the sentence continues "as there is too large a territory to the north which is lower than the island, over which the water may spread"). "Science" is the writer's gloss of "reasoning".

### A1-S5 — narration
**Picture:** self-made island elevation graphic, camera tilts north over the bay to the mainland; an animated storm wave rolls in from the Gulf, rises against the island, then spills north and flattens out over the low ground; chip "TOO LARGE A TERRITORY TO THE NORTH". Source line: Isaac Cline, Galveston Daily News, 16 July 1891. [RS] [VE] [PAY] [loop L-intro closes]
> His reason was the land behind the island. He wrote that there was too large a territory to the north, lower than the island, over which the water may spread. In his picture, a storm wave pushing in from the Gulf would run off across that low country and thin out, so it would be impossible for any cyclone to build a wave that would materially injure the city.

Rows: Commons transcription ("as there is too large a territory to the north which is lower than the island, over which the water may spread, it would be impossible for any cyclone to create a storm wave which would materially injure the city"); sources-cline.md §1 ("large, lower-lying territory to the north"); factcheck-E.md H1-S4 row. "Run off ... and thin out" is the writer's plain gloss of "spread".

### A1-S7 — narration
**Picture:** back to the 1891 page, the lower-third "ISAAC CLINE · WEATHER BUREAU" returns; then the island elevation map from the hook tail, a sea-level line and three chips stacking: "HIGHEST POINT ABOUT 8.7 FT", "MUCH OF THE CITY ABOUT 4 FT", "CLINE'S HOUSE: 5.2 FT". > And this came from the one man in town whose job was the weather, with the instruments, the records and the government title. Now set his reasoning next to the island he lived on. Its highest point was about eight point seven feet above the sea, and much of the city sat around four. The ground under Cline's own house stood five point two feet.
