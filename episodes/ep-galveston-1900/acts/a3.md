## A3 · The turn that never came
Chapter card: Purpose · show why Washington's recurve call was a reasonable bet that failed, and that the warnings worked at sea but met a calm, confident island.
Chapter card: Question · if the Bureau warned every day and ships stayed in port, why did Galveston still sit tight on Friday night?
Chapter card: Reveal · nothing to the north reached down to turn the storm (the Bureau's own September maps), and Friday on the island showed none of the signs Cline was watching for, only a swell and a tide rising against the wind.
Chapter card: Visual event · self-made steering graphic (Atlantic high, westerlies, the hook that never came); Captain Halsey's barometer falling on screen; the swell rolling in on a calm-sky Galveston beach.
Chapter card: Exit hook · Paul (P4): at half past three on Saturday, Isaac Cline sent Washington a telegram (cta-hinge).

### A3-S1 — narration
**Picture:** self-made steering graphic: a broad dome of high pressure over the Atlantic drawn as a soft orange ridge, a storm dot riding its southern and western edge; a dip in the westerly winds swings down from the north, catches the dot and flings it northeast; lower-third "NATIONAL HURRICANE CENTER"; chip "RECURVE". Source line: National Hurricane Center. [VE] [RS] [loop L-A3 open] (if storms usually turn, what kept this one going west?)
> Washington's call had a real reason behind it. A hurricane doesn't steer itself. It drifts around the edge of a big dome of high pressure out over the Atlantic, and once it gets far enough north, a dip in the westerly winds usually catches it and swings it off to the northeast. The National Hurricane Center still calls that recurving.

Rows: Bureau expected recurve "as many storms did near this latitude": sources-cuba.md §3 (NOAA AOML). **Picture:** Galveston beach, period photograph or stock of a flat Gulf beach under a broken, high-cloud sky; chip "NO BRICK-DUST SKY"; then a sky graphic, thin cirrus streaming in from the southeast, swapping to grey layers moving from the northeast by noon. > Friday on the island didn't look like hurricane weather. Cline watched that evening for the brick-dust sky he'd seen ahead of other storms on this coast, and it never showed. Thin high clouds drifted in from the southeast that morning, and by noon the clouds were coming from the northeast instead.

Rows: MWR Sept 1900, Cline's report (OCR lines ~210-222): "The brick-dust sky was not in evidence in the smallest degree.
