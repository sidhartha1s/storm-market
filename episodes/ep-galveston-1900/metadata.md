Nine years before the storm, Galveston's own newspaper told its readers no hurricane could badly damage their city. The man behind that line was Isaac Cline, the city's Weather Bureau chief; he called the fear "an absurd delusion". In September 1900 the Jesuit forecasters at Belen Observatory in Havana said the storm was heading northwest, into the Gulf of Mexico. The U.S. Weather Bureau in Washington expected it to turn up the Atlantic coast, and had ruled that only its own forecasts would travel over the telegraph. Galveston got Washington's warning, the wrong one. At least six thousand people lost their lives, Cline's wife among them. This film follows who told Galveston it was safe, who read the storm right, and why a warned city stayed.
