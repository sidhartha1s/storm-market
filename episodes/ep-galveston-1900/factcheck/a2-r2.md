# Fact-check a2 round 2 (A2-S1 to A2-S9), 26 Oct 2026

Sources used: acts/a2.md (round 2); research/sources-cuba.md; factcheck/a2-r1.md (accepted); Econlib on Larson (fetched); Roker/HistoryNet "Blown Away" (fetched, SECONDARY); Rubiera, OnCuba (fetched, SECONDARY); MWR Sep 1900 OCR (archive.org, grepped); Wikipedia 1900 Galveston hurricane and Benito Viñes (fetched); OnCuba "El Padre Huracán" (fetched). Focus: the 10 open [verify] markers and the new wording; r1 rows are not re-checked.
Flag-words: no hard or soft word in any spoken line. No digits or abbreviations in spoken lines. Heteronym "read" (past tense, "red") appears in S1, S4-S8 lines ("read this storm right", "He read the sky"): probe before voicing. Probe "Viñes" and "Gangoiti". All spoken sentences under 24 words.

| # | segment | claim (first words) | verdict | evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A2-S2 | "He also wrote his rules down in a book on West Indian hurricanes, so ship captains could read the signs" | CORRECTED | Book exists: Wikipedia "In 1885 he wrote a book entitled Practical Hints in Regard to the West Indian Hurricanes" (US Navy translated, Hydrographic Office issued); OnCuba: "Apuntes relativos a los huracanes de las Antillas en 1875 y 1876", Havana 1877, "Este libro contribuyó a su reconocimiento internacional". Neither page says who it was for; no mention of mariners or captains. "Rules" overstates a study of the 1875 and 1876 storms. Cut the audience clause, say "findings". |
| 2 | A2-S3 | "Belen kept up the old man's storm forecasting" | CORRECTED | Wikipedia: born 19 Sep 1837, died 23 Jul 1893, so fifty-five at his death (the marker's "fifty-six" is wrong). "The old man" is a characterisation no source gives. AOML: Belen "carried on the work of Fr. Benito Viñes in forecasting storms in the vicinity of Cuba". Use "Viñes's". |
| 3 | A2-S5 | "José Rubiera ... writes that in eighteen ninety-eight, once the Americans took charge, Belen was barred" | CONFIRMED | SECONDARY, attributed. OnCuba: "En 1898 durante el gobierno interventor de los Estados Unidos en Cuba ... se le prohíbe dar a conocer públicamente sus pronósticos". "Gobierno americano" is only a section heading; the body says "gobierno interventor de los Estados Unidos". "Once the Americans took charge" is a fair gloss. |
| 4 | A2-S5 | "That was the country's only weather institution." | CORRECTED | Rubiera's words: "al Colegio de Belén, única institución meteorológica existente en ese entonces en el país". It is his claim, not a checked fact; as a bare narrator sentence it drops the attribution and the "at that time". Fold into his attribution. |
| 5 | A2-S5 | "The author Erik Larson quotes a letter Willis Moore sent the head of Western Union." | CONFIRMED | Econlib quotes Larson (Isaac's Storm): Moore "wrote to Gen. Thomas T. Eckert, president of Western Union". Larson is the author of Isaac's Storm. |
| 6 | A2-S5 | "Moore said the Bureau in Cuba had been greatly annoyed by independent observatories." | CONFIRMED | Econlib: "The United States Weather Bureau in Cuba has been greatly annoyed by independent observatories securing a few scattered reports". |
| 7 | A2-S5 | "Larson dates it to late August, nineteen hundred, less than two weeks before the storm." | CONFIRMED | Econlib: letter dated "August 28", year 1900, and Econlib itself says "only 13 days before the hurricane hit Galveston". 28 Aug to evening of 8 Sep is eleven days, so "less than two weeks" holds on either count. "Late August" = 28 Aug. Do not say Larson himself counts the days; the line does not. |
| 8 | A2-S5 | chip/card "AUG 28, 1900", "SEPT 20, 1900", "twelve days after Galveston" | CONFIRMED | r1 rows 31, 32, 33; Econlib re-fetched for 28 Aug. |
| 9 | A2-S6 | "On the island itself, the government telegraph lines were run by the American War Department." | CONFIRMED | Roker: "the U.S. War Department controlled all of Cuba's government-owned telegraph lines" (r1 row 36). |
| 10 | A2-S6 | "At the Bureau's request, it banned weather messages from them." | CONFIRMED | Roker: War Department "responded quickly to the Weather Bureau's request to formally ban from those lines all messages referring to weather" (r1 row 37). SECONDARY, one source, no primary order found. Source line names Roker. |
| 11 | A2-S6 | "Moore also kept three words out of official weather reports: tornado, cyclone and hurricane." | UNVERIFIED | Roker (SECONDARY, only source): "For fear of panicking local populations, Moore banned certain words from all official weather reports", listed among reforms after Moore took over in 1895, no date and no document. Did it hold in September 1900? Evidence against a blanket ban: the Bureau's own MWR for September 1900 uses "hurricane" 35 times, is titled "West Indian Hurricane", and the Bureau reprints "The weather office sent out its hurricane warnings" (Boston Herald, MWR p.376, press voice). Wikipedia says only that the central office "discouraged" "hurricane" and "tornado". A search summary of Larson says only "tornado" was avoided and "hurricane" needed the chief's approval. Econlib says nothing of a word ban. Not settled, contradicted in part: cut the sentence. |
| 12 | A2-S6 | "And he stopped his own Havana office from wiring New Orleans directly." | CONFIRMED | Roker: Moore "banned direct communication between the U.S. Weather Bureau's office in Havana and the office in New Orleans" (r1 row 38). Econlib (Larson) agrees: Moore "halted all direct transmission of West Indies storm reports" and stopped reports from the Havana office to New Orleans. Two secondary lines. |
| 13 | A2-S6 | "Havana's reports went to Washington first, so Washington alone decided what New Orleans was told." | CORRECTED | Roker: "Havana would report directly to Washington, and Washington would decide what information to give New Orleans". "First" and "decided" are sourced; "alone" is added emphasis and rests on one secondary line. Drop "alone". |
| 14 | A2-S7 | "On Monday the third of September, he picked up a new storm east of Cuba and started tracking it." | CORRECTED | Roker: "On Monday, September 3, Father Lorenzo Gangoite ... observed a new storm" and gives no position. "East of Cuba" and "started tracking" are the writer's. MWR: the storm "moved westward over the Caribbean Sea during the first three days of September", so the position is at best the Caribbean, not "east of Cuba". Wikipedia (SECONDARY) dates Gangoite's formative-stage note 1 Sep, a conflicting date from Roker's 3 Sep; keep Roker's, which is the attributed one, and claim only "observed". 3 Sep 1900 was a Monday. |
| 15 | A2-S7 | "He read the sky and the sea the way Viñes had." | CORRECTED | Roker (SECONDARY): on 5 Sep Gangoite saw cirrus clouds moving from the west by north, and "following Father Viñes' model" concluded the storm was headed for the Texas coast. Sky and Viñes's model are supported; "the sea" is not. AOML supports only "carried on the work of Viñes". |
| 16 | A2-S7 | "He'd been writing to Willis Moore since late eighteen ninety-eight, and the Bureau was still writing to him that August." | CONFIRMED | r1 rows 23, 25 (Pietruska fn 53, 56, 73); contents unseen, line claims none. |
| 17 | A2-S7 | "So the Americans could hear him. Galveston couldn't." | CONFIRMED | Letters (row 16) and Rubiera "comunica a los meteorólogos norteamericanos". OnCuba does not say how he communicated. The spoken line does not say "face to face", so that marker text is not in the line; it can go. |
| 18 | A2-S9 | "Stockman, the Bureau's own man in Havana, found it not severe in the West Indies." | CORRECTED | MWR p.? "The storm was not severe in the West Indies although the rains were, in places, torrential. ... W. B. Stockman, Forecast Official." The words are exact (chip is exact). But it is an after-the-fact summary printed in the September issue; "found it" reads as his judgment on 4 Sep. Also Santiago got 24.34 inches over the period. Say he "later reported". |
| 19 | A2-S9 | chip "STOCKMAN, MWR: 'NOT SEVERE IN THE WEST INDIES'" | CONFIRMED | Exact substring of the MWR sentence. |
| 20 | A2-S1 to A2-S9 | all other claims | NOT RE-CHECKED | Covered by a2-r1 (accepted); unchanged wording. |

New evidence for research:
- https://www.econlib.org/lessons-from-isaacs-storm/ : Moore "wrote to Gen. Thomas T. Eckert, president of Western Union"; "annoyed by independent observatories securing a few scattered reports"; dated August 28, 1900, "only 13 days before the hurricane hit Galveston"; Moore "instituted the ban on Cuban weather telegrams and halted all direct transmission of West Indies storm reports". No word ban on that page.
- https://historynet.com/blown-away/ : "For fear of panicking local populations, Moore banned certain words from all official weather reports" (undated, among changes after 1895); Gangoite 5 Sep cirrus "following Father Viñes' model".
- MWR Sep 1900 (archive.org URL in sources-cuba.md): "hurricane" appears 35 times, including the Bureau's own title "West Indian Hurricane" and "The weather office sent out its hurricane warnings both for the Atlantic and Gulf coasts" (Boston Herald, reprinted).
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Vi%C3%B1es : born 19 September 1837, died 23 July 1893, Havana; 1885 "Practical Hints in Regard to the West Indian Hurricanes".
- https://oncubanews.com/cuba/el-padre-huracan : book "Apuntes relativos a los huracanes de las Antillas en 1875 y 1876", Havana 1877; "muchos lo llamaban el Padre Huracán".
- https://oncubanews.com/?p=56292 : "En 1898 durante el gobierno interventor de los Estados Unidos en Cuba"; "gobierno americano" is a heading only.
Could not settle: whether the word ban applied in 1900; whether Gangoiti first noted the storm on 1 or 3 September; the date of the War Department order; Viñes book audience. Note: Roker gives no clear year for the telegraph bans in the text read; do not date them aloud.
