# El Reno, Oklahoma Tornado — May 31, 2013: Verified Research

Sources used: NWS Norman event page; NWS Service Assessment "The Historic Tornado Outbreaks of May 2013" (13oklahoma_tornadoes.pdf, published March 2014); Marshall, Burgess, Garfield, Smith, Speheger, Snyder & Bluestein, "13.1 Ground-Based Damage Survey and Radar Analysis of the El Reno, OK Tornado" (AMS 27th Conf. on Severe Local Storms manuscript); AMS *Weather, Climate, and Society* 2022 evacuation study (abstract); ABC News, News9/KFOR.com, Mike Smith Enterprises blog (industry-expert commentary, cross-checked against NWS Norman's own tweets). YouTube-only claims were excluded; anything not confirmed in the sources below is marked UNVERIFIED.

---

## 1. Verified Timeline

- **Tornado formation:** ~5:03 PM CST (6:03 PM CDT) near Reuter and Heaston/Radio roads, about 10 km west of El Reno. Source: [NWS Norman event page](https://www.weather.gov/oun/events-20130531); confirmed independently by the Marshall et al. survey paper ("The tornado developed around 2303 UTC in a sparsely populated area about 10 km west of El Reno").
- **Warnings:** Tornado warning issued 6:08 PM CDT, with follow-on warnings at 6:28 PM and 6:57 PM as the storm produced multiple vortices. Source: NWS Norman event page.
- **Early motion:** Initially moved southeast at 20–25 mph, causing only EF0/EF1 damage to rural homes (Marshall et al.).
- **The turn:** Around 2309 UTC (~5:09 PM CDT) the tornado turned east crossing Chiles Road. During the period of rapid intensification, per the **NWS Service Assessment** (primary, direct quote): *"the forward motion of the circulation increased from approximately 35 mph to more than 50 mph. This speed increase, combined with a sharp left turn and intense inflow winds, made driving away from the tornado difficult."* This is the sudden-left-turn-plus-acceleration event that trapped chasers and motorists near Hwy 81.
- **Maximum width:** 4.2 km (2.6 miles), reached about 6 km south of El Reno at approximately 2319 UTC (~7:19 PM CDT/6:19 PM CST) — Marshall et al., using the RaXPol 30 m/s isodop as the tornado-edge boundary. This is stated as **"a new record for tornado width,"** surpassing the Hallam, Nebraska F4 of May 22, 2004.
- **Path length:** 26.2 km (16.3 miles) total path, per Marshall et al. ground survey (257 standard damage indicators (DIs) + 344 non-standard DIs, including 112 oil-tank batteries).
- **Rating:** EF3 — maximum damage found by ground survey teams could not support a higher rating, despite instantaneous RaXPol velocities in excess of 130 m/s (~291 mph) close to the ground and >120 m/s (~268 mph) within subvortices east of Hwy 81. Marshall et al. state directly: *"Despite the intense wind velocities, surveyors could not find damage that would support a rating higher than EF3."* RaXPol-measured velocities were found to run **10–30% higher than the maximum EF-scale-implied wind speeds** at the same locations.
  - Note on the commonly cited "296 mph" figure: the primary AMS ground-survey paper documents 130 m/s (≈291 mph) instantaneous near-surface winds and >120 m/s (≈268 mph) in subvortices — the "296 mph" number widely repeated online is close to but not an exact match to the peer-reviewed figures we could directly confirm from the text extracted here. Treat any single "296 mph" citation as **UNVERIFIED** pending the full Bluestein et al. (2015, 2018) RaXPol papers (paywalled/not directly extractable in this pass); the order of magnitude (280–300+ mph in subvortices) is solidly verified.
- **Multi-vortex behavior:** RaXPol and separately the Doppler on Wheels (DOW) radars (Wurman et al. 2014) both documented a persistent subvortex plus intermittent subvortices, a **satellite tornado** moving southbound into the northwest flank of the main circulation, and an **anticyclonic tornado** located east-southeast of the main tornado — with some of the anticyclonic-family subvortices themselves spawning still-smaller vortices. Source: Marshall et al., Section 6/Fig. 28.

## 2. The Human Story

### TWISTEX deaths — Tim Samaras, Paul Samaras, Carl Young
- All three died when their white Chevrolet Cobalt was overtaken near Hwy 81/Reuter Road. Per the **NWS Service Assessment**: *"All eight of the direct tornado fatalities on May 31, 2013, were in vehicles, four of whom were involved in storm chasing... None of the individuals were functioning under a NOAA-sponsored research project at the time."* (Note: this is a materially under-reported nuance — at the moment of death, TWISTEX was not operating as an official NOAA-funded research mission.)
- Per ABC News: Tim Samaras (55) was found inside the vehicle with his seatbelt still fastened. Paul Samaras (24) and Carl Young (45) were ejected/pulled from the car by the tornado; one was found roughly half a mile from the vehicle.
- Fatality table in the NWS Service Assessment appendix independently lists three vehicle/storm-chasing deaths aged 55, 24, and 45 — ages that match Samaras/Samaras/Young exactly, corroborating the ABC News account from an independent federal source.
- **Ages of the TWISTEX crew are a strong net-info-gain fact**: most retellings state names but rarely cite the government fatality table that independently corroborates ages/circumstances.

### Dan Robinson's near-miss and the "chaser traffic was a non-factor" finding
- Direct quote from the **NWS Service Assessment**, citing chaser Dan Robinson's own post-storm account: *"At this point at 6:19:25 p.m., the white Chevy Cobalt occupied by the TWISTEX crew was immediately behind me. Other than the TWISTEX Cobalt and a red pickup truck parked along Highway 81, there were no other vehicles in our immediate vicinity. In other words, chaser traffic was a non-factor at our location."*
- This directly **refutes** any narrative blaming "chaser convergence"/traffic congestion among storm chasers for the TWISTEX deaths — the Service Assessment states explicitly: *"It does not appear that traffic was a factor in the deaths of the TWISTEX storm chasers."* This is a strong net-info-gain correction to common YouTube narratives that blame chaser gridlock for the deaths.

### Richard Henderson (amateur chaser)
- A truck driver and hobbyist storm chaser who drove from his home near El Reno/Hinton area to see the tornado. Per News9/KFOR.com (2014 retrospective): he texted a photo of the approaching tornado to a friend at 6:05 PM; roughly 10 minutes later he reported hearing debris hit his pickup before his phone went dead. His family believes "the tornado sucked him out of his pickup."
- He left behind two sons (Robbie, 11, and Wyatt, 8, at time of the 2014 article), ex-wife Amanda Brimage, and mother Dianna Henderson.
- The **NWS Service Assessment** independently confirms: *"an amateur storm chaser that left his nearby home was killed"* — matching Henderson's story of driving from a nearby residence.
- His age (widely cited elsewhere as 35) was **not independently confirmed** in the primary sources pulled here — flag as **UNVERIFIED** pending a document that states it directly (the NWS fatality table lists a 35-year-old "Vehicle, storm chasing" death consistent with this, but does not name him).

### Total death toll: 8 tornado deaths + 13 flash-flood deaths
- **8 direct tornado fatalities, all in vehicles** — confirmed by both the NWS Norman event page and the NWS Service Assessment appendix fatality table, which lists: M67 (driving El Reno→Chickasha), M35 (vehicle, storm chasing), F26 (vehicle, parked along I-40), M-infant/17 days (vehicle, parked along I-40 — same location as F26, implying mother and infant), M32 (vehicle), M55/M24/M45 (vehicle, storm chasing — the TWISTEX crew).
- **13 additional deaths from the flash flooding** overnight into June 1, mostly in Oklahoma City — 12 of the 13 were in Oklahoma City proper. Direct quote, NWS Service Assessment: *"The flash flooding that occurred in Oklahoma County, OK, during the evening of May 31 and early morning of June 1 resulted in 13 fatalities, including 12 people in Oklahoma City. This was the deadliest flooding event ever for Oklahoma City... also the deadliest flash flood in the WFO Norman forecast area since April 3–4, 1934, when 17 people were killed... along the Washita River near Hammon, OK."*
- Several flood victims died **sheltering in drainage ditches** (per the fatality table) — people fled to ditches/underpasses seeking shelter from the tornado threat and were caught by rapidly rising floodwater, a genuinely under-told compounding-disaster angle.

### Mike Morgan "drive south" broadcast controversy
- KFOR-TV chief meteorologist Mike Morgan twice told viewers in south Oklahoma City to flee their homes and "drive south" as the tornado approached during evening rush hour. Sources: HuffPost, Change.org petition record, Mike Smith Enterprises blog (industry commentary), cross-referenced against NWS Norman's own tweets.
- **Important nuance verified from NWS Norman's own tweet record (quoted directly in the Service Assessment):** at 1:00 PM that day — *before* the tornado even formed — WFO Norman itself tweeted: *"TORNADO SAFETY: If you choose to flee in your car, plan for and anticipate traffic jams and blocked roads, hail, heavy rain and flooding."* This means the NWS's own messaging that day also included conditional "flee by car" language, which the Mike Smith Enterprises commentary uses to argue Morgan was made a scapegoat for language NWS itself had used. This is a **strong net-info-gain nuance**: the controversy is not simply "TV meteorologist gave bad advice" — it's a genuine dispute over whether NWS's own "flee if you can't shelter" messaging set the precedent Morgan followed.
- NOAA meteorologist Harold Brooks called blanket "get underground or you won't survive" messaging "irresponsible at best, and dangerous at worst" — illustrating that even NWS scientists disagreed internally about evacuation-vs-shelter messaging that week.
- The Service Assessment's own **Finding 6**: *"Interviews with the public indicated the broadcast media focused on the tornadoes and not the flooding during the May 31 event,"* and **Recommendation 6** calls for NWS to give broadcast media better guidance on managing multiple simultaneous hazard messages — an institutional acknowledgment that messaging coordination, not one meteorologist's error, was the systemic problem.

### The I-40/US-81 traffic jam — largest mass vehicle-flight in OKC history
- The Service Assessment states plainly: *"the May 31 storms occurred on a Friday around rush hour when many residents were already in vehicles. People followed atypical media advice to immediately drive south as storms were bearing down on Oklahoma City."*
- Independent peer-reviewed confirmation: a 2022 AMS *Weather, Climate, and Society* study, "A Spatiotemporal Perspective on the 31 May 2013 Tornado Evacuation in the Oklahoma City Metropolitan Area" ([journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/wcas/14/3/WCAS-D-21-0106.1.xml](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/wcas/14/3/WCAS-D-21-0106.1.xml)), found: *"No other event in six metropolitan areas studied during 2011–18 could compare to the magnitude of this evacuation,"* calling it unique in modern OKC evacuation history. This is a strong, precisely citable net-info-gain fact — a peer-reviewed academic study, not a chaser anecdote, establishing the record-scale of the flight.
- Context the Service Assessment supplies for *why* people were primed to flee: this was the third major severe-weather event of the month (after the May 19–20 tornadoes, including the deadly EF5 Moore tornado on May 20, just 11 days earlier), and the report explicitly draws the analogy to the 2005 Hurricane Rita panic-evacuation following Katrina — arguing recent trauma, not the day's messaging alone, drove abnormal “everyone get in a car” behavior. Direct quote: *"The public felt the fear induced by the May 20 event led people to take actions on May 31 they would not normally take... this advice to evacuate overrode their typical emergency plans and advice about sheltering in place."*
- The tornado itself crossed Interstate 40 (Marshall et al. document EF-rated damage concentrated near I-40, including a cluster of manufactured homes just south of the interstate with the highest damage-indicator density on the entire path — 69 DIs per square mile there vs. an average of under 10/sq mi elsewhere on the path).

## 3. Meteorology Explainer Material

- **Why mobile radar saw what damage surveys couldn't:** The tornado spent most of its 26.2 km path over sparsely populated farmland with few structures to be damaged. RaXPol could sample wind velocity directly in the air, independent of whether anything was there to be damaged. Ground crews, by contrast, can only infer wind speed from what it did to buildings/vegetation/vehicles it actually struck (the EF scale's Damage Indicator/Degree-of-Damage method) — and here the most violent part of the tornado (measured 130 m/s / subvortices >120 m/s by radar) passed almost entirely over open ground, so no EF4/EF5-grade Damage Indicator ever existed to survey. Direct Marshall et al. quote: *"the highest velocities occurred in areas notably absent of DIs."*
- **The EF scale rates damage, not wind, by design:** the Enhanced Fujita scale assigns a rating from the *worst damage found to type-catalogued structures/vegetation* (Damage Indicators + Degree of Damage), not from any independently measured wind speed. Marshall et al. explicitly frame their whole paper around comparing RaXPol's directly-measured radial velocities against the wind speeds *implied* by the EF damage ratings, finding radar values ran 10–30% higher — i.e., the EF process is conservative by construction when violent winds miss engineered structures.
- **Anticyclonic satellite tornado:** Most tornadoes rotate cyclonically (counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere). El Reno's radar data documented a genuine anticyclonic (clockwise) tornado co-existing east-southeast of the main cyclonic tornado, plus a separate "satellite" tornado orbiting into the main circulation's northwest flank — a rare, radar-confirmed multiple-tornado-family structure within one supercell. Source: Marshall et al., Section 6, Fig. 28 caption: *"The anticyclonic tornado is located in the lower right corner of the image."*
- **Storm motion vs. sub-vortex velocity:** the tornado's own translation speed (~35→50+ mph per the Service Assessment) is the speed the entire circulation moved across the ground. This is distinct from the rotational/translational speed of individual subvortices embedded within the broader circulation, which RaXPol measured at far higher instantaneous velocities (120–130+ m/s, ~268–291 mph) — i.e., a person or vehicle could be standing still relative to the ground yet be hit by localized subvortex winds several times faster than the parent tornado's forward travel speed. The specific "~175 mph sub-vortex translation speed" figure requested could **not be independently verified** in the sources retrieved this pass (the Bluestein et al. 2015/2018 full RaXPol subvortex papers were not directly extractable — paywalled/blocked) — flag as **UNVERIFIED**, though it is consistent in order of magnitude with the documented "35→50+ mph parent tornado + up to 291 mph subvortex wind" combination.

## 4. NET-INFO-GAIN Candidates (facts a typical YouTube retelling omits)

1. **TWISTEX was not on an active NOAA-funded mission when the crew died**, per the NWS Service Assessment: *"None of the individuals were functioning under a NOAA-sponsored research project at the time."* Source: [13oklahoma_tornadoes.pdf](https://www.weather.gov/media/publications/assessments/13oklahoma_tornadoes.pdf), Section 3.9.
2. **Chaser traffic was explicitly ruled out as a factor in the TWISTEX deaths**, based on the very next chaser's (Dan Robinson's) direct on-scene account, quoted verbatim in the federal Service Assessment. Same source.
3. **NWS Norman's own 1:00 PM tweet told the public to "flee in your car" hours before the tornado formed** — undercutting the simple "Mike Morgan alone caused the traffic jam" narrative; the Service Assessment quotes this tweet directly. Same source, Section 3.3.
4. **A peer-reviewed 2022 AMS journal study found this was the largest tornado evacuation of any of six U.S. metro areas studied 2011–2018** — a precise, academically-sourced record claim, not a chaser-forum anecdote. Source: [Weather, Climate, and Society, Vol. 14, Issue 3 (2022), WCAS-D-21-0106.1](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/wcas/14/3/WCAS-D-21-0106.1.xml).
5. **RaXPol-measured wind speeds ran 10–30% higher than the EF-scale-implied winds at the same locations** — the precise, quantified relationship between the "296 mph vs EF3" discrepancy that most retellings state only qualitatively. Source: Marshall et al. AMS manuscript, Abstract/Section 6.
6. **The flash flood death toll (13) tied the 1984 Tulsa flood as Oklahoma's second-deadliest flash flood on record and was the deadliest ever specifically for Oklahoma City** — with an exact comparison chain back to an April 3–4, 1934 flood near Hammon, OK (17 dead) that virtually no popular retelling cites. Source: NWS Service Assessment, Section 3.3.
7. **Damage-indicator density on the ground survey peaked at 69 DIs/sq mi in a manufactured-home cluster just south of I-40** vs. an average under 10/sq mi elsewhere on the path — a hard data point tying the interstate corridor specifically to the tornado's most severe surveyed damage concentration. Source: Marshall et al. manuscript, Section 3.
