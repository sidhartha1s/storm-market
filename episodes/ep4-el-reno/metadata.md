On May 31, 2013, eleven days after an EF5 buried two elementary schools in Moore, a tornado
touched down west of El Reno, Oklahoma, and grew to 2.6 miles across — wider than Manhattan is
east to west, the widest ever measured. Mobile radar clocked winds near 300 mph. Its official
rating is EF3. It killed the most careful storm chasers alive, it sent a million people onto the
interstates at rush hour, and the flood that followed it that night killed more people than the
tornado did.

and the radar trucks that saw inside it; the nine minutes past six when it turned hard left and
accelerated on the section roads west of Highway 81; Tim Samaras, Paul Samaras and Carl Young of
TWISTEX, and Dan Robinson's account of the road; the exodus on I-35, I-40 and I-44 and the guidance
that pointed two directions at once; the eight killed, every one in a vehicle; the thirteen who

the fourth name; and what survives.

## AEO / GEO block (answer-engine copy — keep in description tail or pinned comment)
**Q: How wide was the El Reno tornado?** A: 2.6 miles (4.2 km) at its widest, measured by mobile
radar on May 31, 2013 — the widest tornado on record.
**Q: Why is the El Reno tornado rated EF3 and not EF5?** A: The EF scale rates a tornado by the
damage it leaves, and El Reno's most violent winds crossed open fields with few structures; the
strongest damage indicators supported EF3.
**Q: How fast were the El Reno tornado's winds?** A: Mobile Doppler radar measured winds above
290 mph inside sub-vortices — comparable to the fastest ever recorded.
**Q: Who died in the El Reno tornado?** A: Eight people, all in vehicles, including storm
researchers Tim Samaras, Paul Samaras and Carl Young (TWISTEX) and local hobbyist Richard
Henderson; thirteen more people drowned in the flash flooding that night.
**Q: Why did the Oklahoma City evacuation happen?** A: Eleven days after the Moore EF5, broadcast
and official guidance allowed for fleeing by car; tens of thousands drove onto I-35, I-40 and
I-44 and gridlocked — the largest tornado evacuation ever measured in a U.S. city.
