## Time zones (read before using any clock time)

- STUDY: "All times in the present report refer to Eastern Standard time unless noted otherwise" (footnote, printed p. 7). It adds that SELS forecast times "refer to the time the forecast first entered the system via local Weather Bureaus, and not the time that the general public became aware of the threat". Indiana clock times in this sheet are EST.
- SURVEY Annex 2 logs: marked "EST" on every entry read; two South Bend entries add the sender's own "CDT" times (6:32 PM CDT and 6:40 PM CDT) inside an entry stamped 6:35 and 6:42 PM EST.
- IWX tornado table: UTC. Conversion to EST (UTC minus 5) done by script in this run: 2245 UTC = 5:45 PM, 2315 = 6:15 PM, 0010 = 7:10 PM, 0020 = 7:20 PM, 0110 = 8:10 PM, 0230 = 9:30 PM, 0405 = 11:05 PM.
- IWX narrative: the zone is not stated for most times. The first forecast is "1045am" there and 11:45 AM EST in STUDY, so the early narrative times read as CST. Later narrative times are given as "PM EST" in the quoted warnings. Treat every unlabelled IWX narrative time as unconfirmed.

## C. Where the chain stopped (one sentence each, with its quote)

All STUDY quotes were read from OCR text and still need the page-image check the angle file asks for before any line is voiced. Counties are named only where the study names them; the study names "County A", "County B", "County C" and gives no key (see section F).

1. County A, civil defense. The civil defense director heard about the tornado from a policeman who came to his house after it had hit. "County CD was not notified of the tornado warning until twenty minutes after it hit." (STUDY printed p. 21)
2. County A, city police. The largest city's police had heard the State Police message and did nothing until the tornado arrived. "Police headquarters did not notify other members of their own organization nor any other organization until after the first tornado hit the county." (STUDY printed pp. 20-21)
3. County A, the plan that came too late. "County CD perceived one of its own functions as a back-up warning system to radio and TV. On Palm Sunday the plan was put into effect too late as the first tornado had already passed through the area." (STUDY printed p. 21)
4. County A, the second storm. "Even though almost everyone was alerted after the first tornado went through, many were killed by the second one due to the convergence of people at disaster sites." (STUDY printed p. 21)
5. County B, the police. The civil defense director radioed the city police with the tornado's location and asked them to tell the radio station. "The information died with the police." The chief gave his reason: "I don't like to panic them." (STUDY printed pp. 22-23)
6. County B, the sheriff. "The (first) time that I knew that we (were) even going to have one was when it was going over. We didn't have any warning at all ... And the television didn't even list here (County B) as being in the danger area." (STUDY printed p. 23)
7. County B, the clock. "The tornado hit the county seat at 8:07 p.m., approximately thirty-five minutes after the CD director first notified the City Police of the tornado." (STUDY printed p. 23)
8. A radio station never told. "We understood later that the local police had been notified earlier, that a tornado was headed toward (us) but (we) were not notified by the local police so we couldn't prepare our listeners." Same page: "our immediate vicinity was not included until 7:00 p.m. and our transmitters were hit at 7:45." (STUDY printed p. 26)
9. Nobody saw it as their job. "No member of any organization interviewed saw it as their responsibility to supplement or 'back up' radio or TV alerting activities." (STUDY printed p. 10)
10. The police chief's reason. "I never felt that it was our primary duty to inform the public because usually the radio has the information even before we get it." (STUDY printed p. 10)
11. The study's verdict on the chain. "In most cases, the WB sent the warning messages but the system tended to break down on the receiving end. Crucial organizations and the general public often did not receive the warnings in time or at all." (STUDY printed pp. 35-36)
12. Radio as the last desk. "there was the general assumption that providing relevant information to the radio and TV stations was sufficient to warn the public." (STUDY printed p. 36)
13. Stations short-staffed and slow. "Most radio and TV stations were understaffed during the Palm Sunday weekend and, therefore, in many cases the public issuing of the tornado forecast was delayed (A few stations never passed on the forecast)." (STUDY printed p. 11)
14. A station's own view of the age of the warnings. "Tornado warnings were generally too old-i.e. 15-30 minutes-to be truly worthwhile as a warning." (STUDY printed p. 25)
15. Late alerts to places in the path. "the alerts which did reach some of the organizations in potential impact areas were received only one, ten, or thirty minutes before impact." (STUDY printed p. 22)
16. Warning for a tornado already gone: Marion. The Fort Wayne 9:20 PM statement (A22) and IWX: "The tornadoes Fort Wayne was warning for had actually hit Marion a full hour and twenty minutes earlier." Curran (former Fort Wayne observer, in IWX): "the receipt of a phone call of a tornado in Marion resulted in a tornado warning being issued for a tornado that had passed through an hour earlier."
17. Warning for Berne as the tornado crossed it. Curran: "I believe the tornado had already crossed into Ohio by the time the warning was transmitted on the Weatherwire." Compare SURVEY: tornado at Linn Grove 8:40 PM, warning stamped 8:40 PM, Ohio line near 8:52 PM. IWX: "In reality, the tornado skimmed the north side of Berne about six minutes after that statement was sent out."
18. Toledo. "The Toledo tornado was never warned for. The only mention the storm got was from the Columbus weather office ten minutes before the twister struck." (IWX narrative; Ohio, outside the STUDY)
19. Seven counties in the clear, one not. "Seven of the eight counties studied were included in WB advisories prior to the time that tornadoes actually touched down in the county. Only one county was given no forewarning that it might be in the path of tornadoes." (STUDY printed p. 16)
20. Forecasts met with inaction. "if there was any pattern to the responses of such organizations ... it was that of inaction insofar as further dissemination and transmission of the tornado forecasts was concerned." (STUDY printed pp. 9-10)
21. The public. "Many simply were not listening to or watching radio and TV on this the first, nice Spring day of the year." (STUDY printed p. 26). NWS Chicago: "Palm Sunday 1965 marked the first truly warm day of spring for residents in the Midwest." (LOT)
22. Nothing to confirm it. "TV stations interrupted usual programming to issue warnings, but they resumed their normal schedules ... Other individuals looked outside for adverse weather conditions ... but found none. There was not a cloud in the sky." (STUDY printed p. 37)

## G. What changed afterwards (only where a primary source says it)

Primary, SURVEY Section II, printed p. 2 (PDF p. 8), read on the page image. The ten recommendations. Ones that bear on the angle:

- Rec. 1: "Hold preparedness meetings in collaboration with the appropriate Federal, State, county and local government officials and news disseminators in order to develop emergency plans for alerting all segments of communities whenever emergency warnings are issued. Urge news outlets to install positive alerting devices (remoted to the announcer's position where necessary) on weather teletypewriter machines installed on their premises."
- Rec. 2: "Saturate the public before and during the season with explanatory material on tornadoes, tornado forecasts, tornado warnings and the appropriate action to be taken in each instance with the assistance of press, radio and TV, schools, and community groups."
- Rec. 3: "Strengthen severe storm warning reporting networks in local communities and insure that they remain alert and active."
- Rec. 5: "Provide remote narrow bandwidth radar repeater scope (or scopes) to each Weather Bureau office in tornado-prone areas where such office is dependent for protection on a radar in another locality."
- Rec. 6: "Complete the radar coverage with modern WSR-57 type radar for all areas east of the Rocky Mountains as soon as practicable"
- Rec. 7: "Provide back-up emergency radio communications between adjacent Weather Bureau offices and between Weather Bureau and appropriate Civil Defense, state, county and local offices with emergency responsibility."
- Rec. 8: "Provide adequate emergency power for all radar installations and all communications equipment (including teletypewriters) at all Weather Bureau offices."
- Recs. 4, 9 and 10 (statewide teletypewriter networks, extending RAWARC, behavioral science surveys) were on the same page; read, not quoted here.

## H. Meteorology layer, minimum (so the explainer has a source)
