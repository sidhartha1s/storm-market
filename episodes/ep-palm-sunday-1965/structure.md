# Palm Sunday 1965: structure (shown to the owner 11 Oct 26, not yet signed off)

Claim (copied from `angle.md`, never rewritten): On Palm Sunday 1965 the forecast did not fail and the warnings were sent. They stopped one desk short of the people.

The viewer keeps watching to find out: Where exactly did the warning stop, and who was holding it?

Architecture: a detective story on a clock. One message is followed desk to desk; each act moves the clock on and changes what the viewer understood.
Planned narration: 22 min. Book ad at the seam after Act 3, at 11:30 of 22:00 = 0.52 of the runtime (window 0.40 to 0.60 in `config.yaml`).
Fact rows (A1, C5 and so on) are the rows of `research/facts.md`.

## Hook and intro (11 Apr 1965; starts 0:00, 1.5 min)
- Changes: The whole WHAT in the first minute and a half: the day, the six states, the toll, and the twist that the forecast and the warnings both went out.
- Opens: If the warnings were sent, where did they stop?
- Fact (hook contract): Paul on camera speaks one sentence that pays the claim. Its wording comes after your title pick.
- Fact (A1, A6): A forecast reaching into north central Indiana was on the Weather Bureau teletype at 11:45 AM. The first tornado warning for northern Indiana went out at 5:43 PM.
- Fact (C19): "Seven of the eight counties studied were included in WB advisories prior to the time that tornadoes actually touched down in the county."
- Fact (F): Toll said once, with its source. The sources give 258 to 271 lives lost; four Weather Service pages use 271.
- On screen: Paul full frame, then damage film or photographs of the day, date and place label inside the first second.
- Hands on: A warning in 1965 was one message. So follow that one message.

## Act 1. They saw it coming (11:45 AM to 5:20 PM; starts 1:30, 3 min)
- Changes: The belief that nobody knew falls. A forecast reaching into north central Indiana was out at 11:45 AM, and at 5:20 PM a second covered most of the northern half of the state.
- Opens: If the forecast was right, who was it written for?
- Fact (A5): Severe Weather Forecast No. 69, 5:20 PM: "PORTIONS OF NORTHERN INDIANA ... A TORNADO OR TWO".
- Fact (A1, G): The word that day was "tornado forecast". A forecast went to offices by teletype, not to people.
- Fact (C13): "Most radio and TV stations were understaffed during the Palm Sunday weekend"; "A few stations never passed on the forecast".
- Fact (C21): Small beat from candidate 4: "Many simply were not listening to or watching radio and TV on this the first, nice Spring day of the year."
- Fact (H): Weather explainer: one strong low, storms that lived "up to 4.5 hours".
- On screen: The forecast text as teletype cards (government work). The forecast area drawn on our own map. One weather diagram.
- Person: The Kansas City forecasters, unnamed in our sources.
- Hands on: At 5:43 PM the forecast becomes a warning. One message, one telephone list.

## Act 2. One message, one telephone list (5:43 to 6:46 PM; starts 4:30, 3.5 min)
- Changes: A warning was not sent to people. It was read down a telephone list: State Police, sheriffs, city police, the electric company, the Red Cross, named stations.
- Opens: How long does a warning take to cross that list?
- Fact (A6): "Generally there was a delay of at least ten minutes from the time that the warning was issued by the WB to the time it was broadcast over radio and T.V."
- Fact (H): "The South Bend office had no radar, nor a remote radar feed." It worked from telephone calls and sightings.
- Fact (C14): A station on the age of the warnings: "too old-i.e. 15-30 minutes-to be truly worthwhile as a warning."
- Fact (C1, C2): First sign of the gap, one county: "County CD was not notified of the tornado warning until twenty minutes after it hit." Police headquarters had heard it and told no one.
- On screen: The chain drawn desk to desk with a running clock (our graphic, the spine of the video). The 5:43 PM warning as a teletype card.
- Person: Paul Huffman of the Elkhart Truth, "sifting through the warnings" before he took his camera out. His twin funnel photograph needs your rights call (below).
- Hands on: At 6:50 PM the South Bend desk types a message no warning office wants to send.

## Act 3. The wires (6:50 to 7:45 PM; starts 8:00, 3.5 min)
- Changes: Candidate 2. The telephone list ran on telephone and power lines, and the first tornadoes were taking those down.
- Opens: What happens to a telephone list when the telephones go?
- Fact (A10): 6:50 PM: "it is impossible to keep track of them ... telephones being out in many areas and it is impossible to notify many people."
- Fact (A11, A12): 7:10 PM: "Bremen cannot be contacted by phone." 7:13 PM: "numerous poor joke calls and they tie up the lines."
- Fact (D): "eleven breaks in its major lines in Indiana with a total of 54 steel towers downed".
- Fact (D): "unless they possessed battery-powered transistor radios they were unable to receive future warnings."
- On screen: The three South Bend messages as cards in a row. The chain graphic with links going dark. Our map of the broken power lines.
- Person: Ron Przybylinski, 11, in South Bend, hearing the 6:50 PM message read out during "Lassie" (his 2014 recollection).
- Hands on: One county still had working radios, and at about 7:30 PM it had the warning in its hands. [The book ad sits here.]

## Act 4. The desk where it stopped (7:15 to 8:07 PM; starts 11:30, 4 min)
- Changes: Candidate 1 pays. Where the lines held, the warning was stopped by people who thought telling the public was someone else's job.
- Opens: Who had it, and what did they do with it?
- Fact (A13): The civil defense director waited fifteen minutes "until he saw the tornado 'with his own eyes'", then radioed the city police and asked them to tell the radio station.
- Fact (C5): "The information died with the police." The chief: "I don't like to panic them."
- Fact (C7): "The tornado hit the county seat at 8:07 p.m., approximately thirty-five minutes after the CD director first notified the City Police of the tornado."
- Fact (C8): The radio station: "we were not notified by the local police so we couldn't prepare our listeners."
- Fact (C9): "No member of any organization interviewed saw it as their responsibility to supplement or 'back up' radio or TV alerting activities."
- Fact (C22): Second beat from candidate 4, the living room: stations "resumed their normal schedules"; people looked out and "There was not a cloud in the sky."
- On screen: The chain graphic stops on one desk and holds. The 35 minutes shown as a clock. Quotes as attributed text, never a voice.
- Person: The unnamed civil defense director and the unnamed police chief. The study hides the county; we say "one Indiana county" unless a newspaper names it.
- Hands on: And at the next office east, warnings were still being typed for storms that were already gone.

## Act 5. Warnings for storms already gone (7:40 to 10:30 PM; starts 15:30, 3.5 min)
- Changes: Candidate 3 as the small arc. By late evening the warnings described the past, and then the office that sent them went dark.
- Opens: How late can a warning be and still be sent?
- Fact (A20): Fort Wayne, 8:40 PM: Berne should take precautions "for the next 30 to 60 minutes". The survey puts that tornado at Linn Grove at 8:40 PM and in Ohio near 8:52 PM.
- Fact (A22, C16): Fort Wayne, 9:20 PM, on Marion. "The tornadoes Fort Wayne was warning for had actually hit Marion a full hour and twenty minutes earlier." The hospital clock "stopped at 8:10 PM".
- Fact (A23): "No issuances between 9:30 PM EST and 10:30 PM EST due to power failure." I read this line on the page image of the survey myself.
- Fact (C18): "The Toledo tornado was never warned for." It began at 9:30 PM. Times laid side by side, no cause claimed.
- On screen: The Fort Wayne log as cards against a map of where each tornado already was. The office in the dark: no picture exists, so a drawn scene or Paul.
- Person: John Curran, observer, back at midnight to teletype machines running on an extension cord from the FAA Flight Service Station.
- Hands on: The next morning the Weather Bureau sent a team to find out what had happened to its warnings.

## Act 6. What they fixed, and the close (12 Apr to 2 May 1965, then today; starts 19:00, 3 min)
- Changes: The Bureau's own answer matches the claim: its first recommendation is about the last desk.
- Opens: Did anyone fix the last desk?
- Fact (G): Recommendation 1: "positive alerting devices (remoted to the announcer's position where necessary)" on the stations' weather teletypes.
- Fact (G): Recommendations 7 and 8: back-up radio between offices, and emergency power for every office.
- Fact (F): Three months on, the study found "in the majority of communities, no sirens sounded".
- Fact (G): The Weather Service says "tornado forecast" became "tornado watch" after this day. No 1965 document for the renaming has been read yet, so it is said as their statement.
- On screen: The recommendation page as a document card. Then one present-day picture of how a warning arrives now (to be sourced).
- Person: P. H. Kutschenreuter, who chaired the survey team and signed its memo on 2 May 1965.
- Hands on: The dominant question is answered in the last 30 to 60 seconds, then the book once.

## Still unverified, and how the structure treats it
- Which county each passage of the study is about. The study gives no key. A Lebanon, Indiana newspaper piece in its footnote 16 is the lead to read.
- Every line from the 1966 study was read from scan text with broken letters. Each line that is voiced gets read on the page image first.
- The lives lost figure. 271 is the Weather Service pages' figure; the survey page that gives it has not been read on its image yet.
- That "watch" and "warning" as words date from this day: a Weather Service page says it, no 1965 document read.
- How a warning reaches people today: one sourced sentence is needed for the close.
- How much of Indiana the 11:45 AM forecast covered: the study says it showed no immediate tornado threat to any part of Indiana, while South Bend reissued it naming three Indiana counties. The script states both.

## Production risks
- Film of this day is thin. The inventory has no free picture of a Weather Bureau office, a police radio room, a civil defense office, a 1965 studio or a siren. This video leans on our own graphics (the chain, the clock, the log cards), Paul, and the free survey charts and teletype photographs.
- Free items are government work plus Wikimedia Commons files whose public-domain claim is the uploader's argument.
