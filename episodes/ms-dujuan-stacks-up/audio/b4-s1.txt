Line up every typhoon that has actually hurt Japan since 2016, and a pattern appears. These are peak numbers, measured over open water before landfall, not the weaker numbers each storm carried when it actually came ashore. Typhoon Lionrock made landfall on Japan's northern Pacific coast in 2016. It was the first typhoon on record to hit that stretch of coastline since Japan started keeping typhoon records in 1951. At its peak, central pressure fell to nine hundred forty hectopascals, sustained winds one hundred five miles per hour. Nine people drowned inside a single nursing home in one coastal town. Twenty two people died across Japan. Typhoon Lan came ashore on the central Japan coast in 2017, far weaker than its peak. At its strongest, over open water, it reached nine hundred fifteen hectopascals, sustained winds one hundred fifteen miles per hour. Three hundred eighty one thousand people evacuated. Seventeen died. Two typhoons hit Japan within a month of each other in 2018. Each one peaked at nine hundred fifteen hectopascals, sustained winds one hundred twenty miles per hour, before weakening toward landfall. Jebby made landfall near Kobe on September fourth, the strongest typhoon to hit Japan since 1993. It broke wind records at one hundred weather stations, flooded a major Osaka airport, and set a new storm surge record in Osaka. Fourteen people died. Trami followed on September thirtieth, cancelling more than a thousand flights and evacuating over three hundred eighty thousand people. Four died. Faxai hit the Tokyo region about a month before Hagibis that same year. At its peak, central pressure fell to nine hundred fifty five hectopascals, sustained winds one hundred miles per hour. It left nine hundred thirty four thousand households without power. Three people died. Hagibis followed, peaking at the deepest pressure of that pair, nine hundred fifteen hectopascals, one hundred twenty miles per hour. It killed one hundred thirty nine people and caused seventeen point three billion dollars in damage, the costliest typhoon in Japanese history. Four of those eight storms peaked at the exact same central pressure, nine hundred fifteen hectopascals, across three separate years. Nanmadol struck southern Japan's Kyushu island in 2022. At its peak, central pressure fell to nine hundred ten hectopascals, sustained winds one hundred twenty miles per hour. That is the deepest peak pressure on this entire list, tied with three other storms for the strongest peak wind. Four people died there, far fewer than Hagibis or Lionrock, because it crossed a less crowded stretch of coastline. Ampil never made landfall at all in 2024. Its own peak, offshore, reached nine hundred fifty hectopascals, one hundred miles per hour, and its closest approach to Tokyo stayed offshore. Four people were injured across the entire Tokyo region. Typhoon No. 25's own longest forecast right now has its peak at nine hundred seventy five hectopascals, sixty seven miles per hour. Every one of those eight storms peaked harder than that, at a lower pressure and a higher wind speed. Even so, the damage each one caused varied wildly.
