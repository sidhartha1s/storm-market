| 1 | S3 | "The faster a low deepens, the harder the wind blows around it." | CORRECTED | NWS Aberdeen, https://www.weather.gov/abr/HighWindsNovember18and19 : "Basically the stronger the pressure systems are, the more the air wants to move/blow from high to low." (PRIMARY; the rule is about the pressure difference, so a deeper low blows harder.) NESDIS, https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/news/bomb-cyclone-delivers-powerful-punch-the-northeast : lower pressure corresponds to a more intense storm (the page says "because of how quickly the storm intensified"). No NWS page found that says the SPEED of the fall sets the wind; the AFD itself lists "MSLP and strength of both the low level and upper level jets" as extreme but gives no causal sentence. "Faster" overstates; "deeper" is supported. |
| 2 | S3 | "twenty-four millibars in twenty-four hours" as "the textbook bar" | CONFIRMED (nuance, no change) | AFD "definition of bombogenesis (24mb/24hrs)". NOAA Ocean Service, https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/bombogenesis.html : "At 60 degrees latitude, it is a drop of at least 24 millibars (24 hectopascals) over 24 hours." The bar scales down with latitude (NOAA: about 17.8 mb at New York City's latitude), so Buffalo's own bar is lower and 32 mb clears either. The line matches the AFD; kept. |
| 3 | S2 | "a few thousand feet above the ground" | CORRECTED | The AFD gives no height for the jet: no 850, 900 or 925 mb level and no altitude (re-read of FXUS61 KBUF 200951); it says only "Normally these winds would be aloft abv a pronounced inversion". NWS has no single height: glossary https://forecast.weather.gov/glossary.php?word=low%20level%20jet "A region of relatively strong winds in the lower part of the atmosphere." SECONDARY: AOPA "usually 1,500 to 3,000 feet above the ground"; NAV CANADA 300 to 3000 feet, at times 7000. The number is a generic range, not this storm's jet, so cut the figure and keep what the AFD supports: up above the ground. The Picture's "a few thousand feet up" has the same problem and goes too. |
