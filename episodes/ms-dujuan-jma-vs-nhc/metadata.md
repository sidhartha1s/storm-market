## Description
https://paulraymond.online
📘 The book mentioned at 8:14 — The Storm-Ready Home

Right now forecasters in Tokyo and Miami are tracking the same tropical system near the Mariana Islands, and they
do not agree on its strength. Neither one is wrong. Japan's weather agency measures wind as a ten-minute average.
America's National Hurricane Center measures the strongest one-minute average inside the storm. That single
difference in the clock is why a "strong typhoon" out of Japan and a hurricane category out of the United States
can describe the same wind field with two different numbers.

This explainer covers: today's live numbers for the unnamed tropical depression near the Marianas (998 hPa, 33 mph
sustained, gusts to 52), the three agencies that track Pacific storms (JMA, the National Hurricane Center, and the
Navy's Joint Typhoon Warning Center), Japan's full ten-minute wind scale (tropical storm through violent typhoon),
the American Saffir-Simpson scale side by side with it, why the Joint Typhoon Warning Center exists at all, today's
actual forecast gap in real numbers (JMA's 67 mph versus JTWC's 75 mph for the same storm), why typhoon and
hurricane are the same weather phenomenon under different regional names, and the stakes behind the gap: Typhoon
Hagibis (2019), 139 dead, $17.3 billion in damage, the costliest typhoon on record in Japan.

Sources: Japan Meteorological Agency tropical cyclone intensity scale (via Wikipedia "Tropical cyclone scales"),
JMA's live TC2630 advisory (16 Sep 2026), the National Hurricane Center's published Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind
Scale, the Joint Typhoon Warning Center's warning bulletin for Tropical Depression 24W, and Wikipedia's "Typhoon
Hagibis" casualty and damage figures.
