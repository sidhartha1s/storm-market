### A2-S1 — narration
**Picture:** Self-made map from the NWS Norman damage-path KML: the track draws from Amber to Bridge Creek, clock chip "ABOUT 6:50 PM", then the A9 photo near Bridge Creek (Erin Maxwell, NWS, NWS Norman event page). Labels "WILLOW LAKE ADDITION · SOUTHERN HILLS ADDITION · BRIDGE CREEK ESTATES" pop on the map. Source line: "Speheger et al. 2002 (6:47 PM); FEMA 342 (6:55 PM); NWS Storm Data, May 1999; Doswell and Brooks 2002". [loop L1 closes] [VE] [Q]
> About twenty-five minutes after it touched down near Amber, the tornado reached Bridge Creek. It was about six fifty in the evening. Bridge Creek wasn't a town at all. It was an unincorporated community out in Grady County, a spread of neighborhoods. In the three that took the worst of it, most of the homes were mobile homes. What does an F5 do to a place built like that?

*register: 9/10*

Chips one per sentence: "2 SLABS · SWEPT CLEAN", "ABOUT 12 CARS · 1/4 MILE", "1 INCH OF ASPHALT · PEELED". Source line: "NWS Storm Data, May 1999; NWS Norman event page". [RS] [VE] [PAY]
> In Willow Lake Addition, two houses were swept clean off their concrete slabs. About a dozen cars were carried a quarter of a mile. The wind peeled an inch of asphalt right off the road. Damage like that is part of what earned this tornado its F5. Bridge Creek is one of only a few places along the whole path where the survey teams found it.

*register: 9/10*

At six fifty-four, over Bridge Creek, it read about three hundred two miles an hour, give or take eleven. Joshua Wurman's team published that figure in 2007, a little under the three hundred eighteen announced in 1999.

### A2-S5 — narration
**Picture:** Self-made graphic: two rows of house icons. Top row "MOBILE HOMES HIT · MORE THAN 1 LIFE LOST PER 10 (ESTIMATE)"; bottom row "HOUSES HIT · UNDER 1 PER 200 (ESTIMATE)". Then the NWS Norman damage-path map, Bridge Creek lit, chip "THE ONLY CLUSTER OF MOBILE HOMES ON THE PATH". Source lines: "Brooks and Doswell 2002" and "Doswell and Brooks 2002". [RS] [VE] [PAY]
> Two researchers at the National Severe Storms Laboratory worked out the odds for that night. By their estimate, for every ten mobile homes the tornado hit, more than one person lost their life. In houses, it was under one in every two hundred. And Bridge Creek was the only place on the whole path with a real cluster of mobile homes.

*register: 8/10*

### A2-S6 — narration
**Picture:** FEMA 342 photo of a house reduced to its concrete slab (FEMA BPAT, check the credit line); chip "SLABS · BASEMENTS RARE". Then a self-made cutaway: a basement with a concrete ceiling, chip "SHELTER ONLY WITH A CONCRETE CEILING". Source lines: "FEMA 342, §3.3 and §6.2.1.1; Doswell and Brooks 2002". [Q] [PAY]
> Most of those families had no basement to run to. In the Oklahoma damage areas, homes sat on concrete slabs, and basements were rare. For most families out here, the safest room they had was still above ground. Researchers tie that to the soil types and the water table there. And FEMA adds a catch. A basement only counts as a shelter if it has a concrete ceiling over it.

*register: 9/10*

### A2-S7 — narration

> Some families in Grady County did have a place below ground. Darrell Turner heard the weather service warnings on a local TV station. He went down into his brother's storm cellar with his family and his neighbors, thirty-five people in all. The tornado pulled the cellar door open and destroyed their house. Every one of them came up alive.

*register: 9/10*
