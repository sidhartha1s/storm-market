> I went down the list of the thirty-six people the tornado took directly that night, and I sorted it by age. There's a hole in the middle of it. Not one of them was aged five through twenty-three. Harold Brooks and Charles Doswell, at the National Severe Storms Laboratory, worked out the odds of a gap like that by chance. It's under one in three thousand.

### A5-S2 — narration
**Picture:** NSSL May 3 page damage photo of a flattened neighbourhood (damage1.jpg, NSSL), slow push; chip "1,800 STUDENTS · HOMES HIT" then "NONE LOST". Source line: "Moore Public Schools, via Brooks and Doswell 2002". [PAY] [HUMAN] [Q]
> The kids came through better than anyone, and Brooks and Doswell think the safety lessons at school helped. Two weeks later, about seven in ten junior high students in one informal survey said they'd learned tornado safety in class. At one school, that lesson came the Friday before. In Moore, about eighteen hundred students lived in houses that were badly damaged or gone, and none of them lost their life. The two authors are careful to say they can't prove the lessons did it.

His later paper with Doswell called its own number crude, about five hundred forty, using the odds people faced in tornadoes before 1925. That one counts every safety effort of the day together.

He meant a reinforced safe room, or a place underground. Near Bridge Creek, on the edge of the F-five damage, somebody had built the first kind. Its concrete walls took a few hits from light debris, and that was all.

### A5-S6 — narration
**Picture:** NWS Norman Tornado Emergency graphic (tornadoemergency600.jpg, NWS Norman event page), then a self-made timeline "1999 · NORMAN, ONE NIGHT" to "2014 · 46 OFFICES TEST IT" to "2017 · EVERY LOWER-48 OFFICE"; source lines "NWS SCN 17-46; NWSI 10-511"; then chip "OKLAHOMA · FIRST SAFE-ROOM REBATE IN THE US", source line "Oklahoma Emergency Management"; then the NSSL aerial of the path (John Jarboe, NWS). [VE] [Q]
> Two things from that night outlasted it. The phrase forecasters in Norman used, tornado emergency, became the weather service's own. Forty-six offices tested it in 2014, and on May tenth, 2017, it went into warnings at every office in the lower forty-eight. And after the tornado, Oklahoma started the country's first program to help families pay for safe rooms of their own.
