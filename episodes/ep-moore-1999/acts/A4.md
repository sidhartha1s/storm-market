At Kelly Elementary, that hallway came down. School was already out for the day, so the corridor stood empty. FEMA's engineers wrote that, had it been in use, it would have hurt many children.

### A4-S4 — narration
**Picture:** NOAA Report, June 1999, photo of the Millhouse hallway (Joseph Schaefer, NOAA; US gov work), slow push on the standing walls. Chip "30 MIN WARNING · ALL SAFE". Source line: "NOAA Report, June 1999, p2 to p3". [HUMAN] [RS] [VE]
> Now a house in Moore. Captain John Millhouse worked at the office that supports the NEXRAD radars, and he had about thirty minutes of warning. He got his wife, their kids, aged six and two, the cat and the dog into the center hallway of their home. When it passed, the walls around that hallway were about all that was left. Every one of them was fine.

*register: 9/10*

What about the people in houses who didn't make it?

### A4-S8 — narration
**Picture:** Self-made bar chart from FEMA 342 section 6.2.1.2, three bars drawn one per sentence: "BATHROOM 81%", "CLOSET 75%", "KITCHEN ~16%", header "89 WALL CLUSTERS LEFT STANDING". Then a FEMA 342 BPAT core-remnant photo (check credit). Source line: "FEMA 342, pp. 6-20 to 6-22". [VE] [PAY] [Q]
> Were those the wrong rooms? FEMA sent a team down the center of the track to find out. Where the houses were gone, they studied eighty-nine standing clusters of inside walls. An inside bathroom was part of what stood eighty-one percent of the time. A closet, seventy-five percent. The kitchen, roughly sixteen. They counted rooms, though, not who was inside them.

*register: 8/10*

### A4-S9 — narration
**Picture:** NSSL damage photos of slabs and stripped lots (damage1.jpg, damage3.jpg, NSSL May 3 page), slow push; attributed text card: "Doswell and Brooks, 2002: by the Fujita scale's definition, F4 and F5 damage leaves no interior walls; there, living is a matter of luck without adequate shelter" (paraphrase, marked as such). Source line: "Doswell and Brooks, Weather and Forecasting, 2002, p615". [PAY] [VE]
> The bathroom was the right call, most of the time. Two researchers who studied this storm, Doswell and Brooks of the National Severe Storms Laboratory, wrote down the rest of it. By the Fujita scale's own definition, where damage reaches F4 or F5, no inside walls are left. There, they wrote, living is a matter of luck unless you can reach adequate shelter.

*register: 8/10*
