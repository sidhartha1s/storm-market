## B2 — WHERE THINGS STAND (0:40–1:50)

### B2-S1 — narration
> As of Monday evening, Hawaii time, here is where things stand. The island's utility cooperative holds restoration at eighty one percent. It brought about one thousand eight hundred fifty more customers back on Sunday. About five thousand accounts on the North Shore are still dark, all of them on one damaged transmission line. Scattered pockets remain across the east, the south and the west of the island. About one hundred outside line workers are on the ground, and the Air Force has flown in six utility trucks from California. The cooperative has around two hundred fifty of its own people on the lines. The first North Shore neighborhoods are expected back early this week. There is still no date for the whole stretch. Damage reports from residents have risen from about four hundred sixty to more than five hundred fifty. The death toll stays at three. A seventy four year old man killed by the surge on Kauai's south shore. A fifty nine year old man found on the North Shore. A homeless man on Oahu. No federal disaster declaration has been approved for Lowell or for Lala. [h1, h2, h3, h8]

## B5 — WHY ONE LINE DECIDES IT (4:50–6:45)

### B5-S1 — narration
> The North Shore is last because of how it is wired. One transmission line feeds that whole stretch of coast, and Lowell damaged it along its length. Crews have to rebuild that backbone first. Only then can the smaller distribution lines behind it carry power into homes. That is the sequence in Bissell's timeline, transmission by the end of next week, distribution after. The two closed schools sit on the same corridor, so their reopening depends on cleared roads and restored service, not staffing. Some scale for that. At the storm's peak, more than ninety one percent of the county's accounts were dark. Oahu lost about twenty one thousand on its own. The governor had declared a state of emergency five days before landfall. Getting from ninety one percent dark to eighty one percent lit in a week is the easy part of the curve. The last twenty percent is one road, one line, and the worst of the damage. [h1, h5, h2, h9]

Visual: the corridor map again, the transmission line pulsing red, distribution stubs grey; popfigs "1 LINE" · "~5,000 ACCOUNTS".

### B5-S2 — narration
> The harbors run on a different logic. Cargo and fuel come before passengers, so operators opened to freight first and are holding cruise calls until the coast settles. The federal money runs on a third track. A Major Disaster Declaration needs the President's signature and the federal emergency agency's sign off, not the state's request alone. The only damage figure for Lowell so far is the governor's own estimate. No federal team has validated a number for this storm. The smaller federal figure that does exist, sixty seven million dollars, belongs to Lala on another island. That gap is why the delegation escalated. Five disasters in six months, they wrote. Kona lows in March, a Kona earthquake in May, Lala in August, a tropical storm the same month, and Lowell this September. [h2, h3, h7]
