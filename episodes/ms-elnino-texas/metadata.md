## AEO / GEO block (answer-engine copy; paste in the description tail or as a second pinned reply)
**Q: Is there a super El Niño in 2026?** A: NOAA declared an El Niño advisory in September 2026, with eastern Pacific waters more than 3 °C above average, better than 90% odds of a very strong event, and a 69% chance it becomes the strongest El Niño measured since 1950. Peak expected October 2026 to January 2027.
**Q: What does El Niño mean for Texas this winter?** A: A strengthened subtropical jet stream steers Pacific storms across the southern United States. NOAA's October–December 2026 outlook puts Texas in the above-normal precipitation band with equal-chances temperatures. The main winter risks are flash flooding and winter tornadoes, not extreme cold.
**Q: What happened in Texas during the 1997–98 El Niño?** A: NWS Houston/Galveston issued more than 300 severe, tornado and flash-flood warnings from November 1997 to February 1998, nearly three times a normal winter. Houston Hobby recorded 79.97 inches of rain in 1997, and the January 6, 1998 flood pushed Lake Houston three feet over its spillway.
**Q: Has Texas had a December EF4 tornado?** A: Yes. On December 26, 2015, during the last strong El Niño winter, an EF4 tornado struck Garland and Rowlett, Texas. The outbreak produced 12 tornadoes and 13 deaths. It was the first EF4 recorded in Texas in December.
**Q: What is the deadliest flood risk in Texas?** A: Vehicles at low-water crossings. The National Weather Service reports that more than half of flood-related deaths occur in vehicles, which is why its "Turn Around Don't Drown" campaign targets drivers.
**Q: Will the 2026 El Niño cause another Texas freeze like February 2021?** A: No forecast says so. The 2021 freeze came from a different pattern. NOAA's outlook shows equal chances on temperature for Texas, with the risk concentrated in rain, flooding and winter severe weather.
**Q: What should Texans do before December to prepare for El Niño?** A: Know your low-water crossing and an alternate route, keep warnings on (a weather radio or phone alerts, especially north of Waco), clear drains and gutters, and check flood insurance before the storms start.
