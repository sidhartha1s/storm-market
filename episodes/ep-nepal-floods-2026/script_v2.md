The Kali Gandaki runs south out of the high Himalaya through one of the deepest gorges on Earth. The river sits

## A1 · Three seconds (1:52)
**Picture:** g-collapse-3s full: four stills pulled from the viral clip at the shot log's timecodes, each stamped on
the voice, then the clip replays once at full speed (reuse, `data-callback="frame-by-frame payoff"`). [RS] [VE]

The video that spread that weekend was filmed from across the river, near a village in Mustang district.

In the first second, a strip of green hillside above the water starts to slide as one piece. By the second, the face
beneath it breaks into brown dust and rock. By the third, the whole section is in the river, and muddy water surges
across the channel.

**Picture:** g-pore-pressure teaser: a slope cross-section, a thin blue line creeping upward inside it, a text card
"U.S. Geological Survey · Landslide Hazards Program".

Landslides like this build for days and finish in seconds. The U.S. Geological Survey describes the same pattern for
American hillsides. Rain soaks in slowly, and the water pressure inside the slope climbs. Then the last of the friction holding it gives way all at once. The three seconds on camera were the end of a failure that had been building since
the rain began.

**Picture:** g-map-kali-gandaki zoom: the gorge narrows, an arrow shows debris entering the channel, then two arrows,
one upstream labelled "blocked" and one downstream labelled "surge risk". [Q]

A gorge this narrow turns one slope failure into a problem for the whole river. Debris that lands in the channel can
pile up into a natural dam and hold back a lake behind it. When that dam gives way, the stored water runs downstream as
a surge of mud. By the twenty eighth, reports said the debris had blocked the Kali Gandaki for a while, before the
water cut through again.

**Picture:** Republic World flooded-road or debris shot, muted, graded (credit line reads "Republic World") ·
g-hydro-card: a dam pin downstream on the map, "144 MW", a US house icon grid filling to "about 120,000 homes".

Downstream, the river feeds a dam and power plant that was the largest in Nepal when it opened in two thousand two.
It makes enough power for roughly one hundred and twenty thousand American homes. With a mountainside of mud in the
water, the plant shut down until the river dropped.

**Picture:** a route line on g-map-kali-gandaki runs north to "Mustang"; a small trekker icon on the Annapurna trail ·
Khoj Samachar road-cut shot (second segment). [VE]

The same valley carries the main road north to Mustang, and to a temple that Hindu and Buddhist pilgrims both visit.
Late September also opens the autumn trekking season on the Annapurna trails above it, when Americans and Europeans
start to arrive. The rain that set all of this off came when Nepal's rainy season is supposed to be ending.

## A2 · The wrong end of the season (3:43)
**Picture:** g-monsoon-calendar: a June to October bar, "onset" mark in mid June, "normal withdrawal" mark in early
October, a red pin landing on "Sept 27" near the end. [VE]

Nepal gets most of its rain from the summer monsoon, which usually arrives in mid June and starts leaving in early
October. By late September, the hillsides have been soaking for more than three months. They go into the last storms
of the year holding more water than at any other time.

**Picture:** g-bay-of-bengal-track: a low pressure symbol forms over the Bay of Bengal, tracks north over India, a rain
shield spreads across the Nepal border · News18 Bihar river-in-spate shot, muted.

This storm began as a low pressure system over the Bay of Bengal, south of India, and moved north. Indian channels
called it Cyclone Arnab, but it never became an official cyclone. By the time it reached the mountains it had lost its winds. It still carried the moisture of the ocean it came from.

**Picture:** g-rain-bars: a small "northern India" label, two bars rising, "normal · 0.3 in" and "Sept 26–28 · 2.6 in",
a multiplier tag landing on "nine times". [RS]

Just across the border in northern India, Al Jazeera reported about two and a half inches of rain that weekend. The
normal for those days is under a third of an inch, so it was close to nine times as much.

**Picture:** g-pore-pressure full: slope cross-section, soil grains as circles, blue water filling the gaps, small
arrows pushing grains apart, a "friction" bar dropping under a "weight" bar until the slope slips.

A slope stays up because of friction between the grains of soil and rock inside it. Rain fills the tiny gaps between
those grains, and the water pushes them apart from the inside. Geologists call that pore pressure. When it climbs
high enough, the friction holding the slope drops below the weight pulling it down. A soaked slope doesn't need the
biggest storm of the year. It needs one heavy weekend on top of three wet months, and late September supplies it.
`[loop L2 closes]`

**Picture:** a cutaway of the Kali Gandaki valley wall: bedrock in grey, a loose tan layer of old glacial gravel
draped over it, labelled "glacial debris" · Lallantop slope or debris shot (second segment).

The Kali Gandaki's walls are among the steepest in the Himalaya, and much of them is covered in old glacial debris.
That's loose rock and gravel that ancient glaciers dragged downhill and dropped when they melted. It was never
cemented into solid rock, so water moves through it fast, and it gives way fast.

**Picture:** a Washington state map, a pin at Oso · popfig "March 22, 2014" · a USGS PD photo of the Oso slide scar
(credit checked) · text on the voice: "43 lives lost". [RS] [VE]

We've watched the same kind of ground fail at home. In March twenty fourteen, near Oso, Washington, a hillside of old
glacial sediment collapsed after weeks of heavy rain. Forty three people lost their lives. The Oso hillside and the Kali Gandaki wall share the same recipe, loose glacial material on a steep slope, full of water.

**Picture:** g-sep-2024 card: "Late Sept 2024 · Kathmandu Valley" with a count landing "more than 200 lives lost",
then a calendar showing both Septembers with the last days circled.

Nepal has seen this late September calendar before. Two years ago, in the last days of September, late rain flooded
the Kathmandu Valley. It set off landslides that took more than two hundred lives, on almost the same dates as this
storm. Higher up, the same kind of storm falls as snow. On Himlung Himal, that snow sat above a camp full of workers. `[loop L1 continues]`

## A3 · Base camp (6:09)
**Picture:** g-basecamp-diagram: Himlung massif side-on, base camp tents at the foot, a fixed-rope line climbing to
"Camp 1", an avalanche path drawn down across the tents · WION avalanche clip (third cut, rescue or helicopter). [VE]

Himlung Himal stands in Nepal's far north, close to the border with Tibet. Expedition companies sell it as a first big
Himalayan climb, a practice run before peaks like Everest.

Every autumn climb starts with the people who arrive before the clients. Guides carry up the tents and fix the ropes,
while cooks build the camp the team will live in for weeks. At Himlung that week, sixteen workers were at base camp,
two climbing guides and fourteen kitchen and support staff. They were getting it ready for a women's climbing school
from the United Arab Emirates, due in Kathmandu on October first.

**Picture:** text-only name card "Pasang Bhote · climbing guide · eastern Nepal" slides in over the base camp shot.
[HUMAN]

Then snow and rock came down onto the camp. One of the crew was Pasang Bhote, a climbing guide from the far east of
Nepal. `[loop L1 closes]` The crew had gone up to make the mountain ready for other people, and the storm arrived
before the climbers did.

**Picture:** g-rock-ice-mechanism: a hanging slab of rock and ice on a steep face, a crack line, the slab falls, ice
fragments turn to water, the mass spreads into a fast tongue that runs far past a plain rockfall line. [RS]

Langtang's collapse in August was heavier, a rock and ice avalanche. That's rock and glacier ice falling together,
fast enough to flow. As it falls, friction melts some of the ice. The water turns the mass into a slurry that runs much farther than dry rock. The Langtang slab was about two thousand feet wide, and it fell almost four thousand feet.
That's about three Empire State Buildings stacked up.

At Himlung, reports so far describe snow and rock. Whether glacier ice was part of it, and how far it ran below the
camp, hadn't been published by the twenty ninth. `[loop L3 open]`

**Picture:** g-insar-creep full: a Copernicus Sentinel radar strip over Langtang, a colour ramp, the counter finishing
its climb to "30 cm · about 12 in" across a "3 to 4 weeks" bracket · text card "ICIMOD · Kathmandu" · then the strip
returns empty over a Kali Gandaki outline with a "?" stamp. [RS] [VE]

In August, someone did measure the Langtang slope before it fell. ICIMOD is a mountain research center in Kathmandu. It said satellite radar showed the slope slid up to twelve inches in three to four weeks. For the Kali Gandaki wall and
the Himlung face, no agency had published radar from before September twenty seventh. `[loop L4 continues]` By the
twenty ninth, the reports couldn't even agree on how many people the storm had taken. `[loop L5 continues]`

## A4 · Settled, disputed, open (8:24)

Three things about this week are settled. The wall fell into the Kali Gandaki on September twenty seventh, after days
of rain. The video is from that day, filmed near the village of Lete in Mustang district. And the avalanche hit the
Himlung camp while the crew prepared it, and people lost their lives there.

The toll is the first thing the reports disagree on. On the twenty seventh, Nepal's disaster agency counted twelve lives lost and seven missing. Across the border in India, fifty six more were lost. That weekend, the Indian news agency
ANI put Nepal at twenty one, with five missing. The outlet NewsBytes reached twenty four, and the Indian channel CNBC
TV18 added both countries together to reach about seventy. The counts cover different places and different hours,
and the missing move into the toll as searches go on. `[loop L5 closes]`

**Picture:** a phone-frame mock of a social post, text blurred except "glacier collapse", a red "?" stamp · then the
g-rock-ice-mechanism slab beside a plain rain landslide cross-section, side by side. [RS]

The story online moved faster than the count. Posts sharing the video called it another glacier collapse, a repeat
of Langtang. Every account of the Kali Gandaki collapse describes days of rain and a soaked valley wall. None mentions glacier ice on that slope. Langtang began about seventeen thousand feet up, where rock and glacier ice broke
away together. The Kali Gandaki collapse matches the rain landslide Nepal sees every monsoon, only larger, and caught
on camera. The Himlung avalanche, coming off a high face with snow and rock, is the event closer to Langtang's type.
`[loop L3 closes]`

## A6 · After the rain (11:37)
**Picture:** News18 Bihar river gauge or swollen river shot · g-sensor-gap full: a river gauge line rises only after a
slope icon has already fallen in; a timeline marks "slope fails" then "gauge sees it". [PAY]

It measured rivers. `[loop L6 closes]` Nepal's flood warnings come from river and rain gauges, which report when
water rises and send alerts to the villages downstream. A slope that fails in three seconds gives a river gauge nothing
to read until the mud is already in the water. In August, the gauges nearest Langtang were swept away before they
could warn anyone below.

**Picture:** g-insar-creep returns small: the Langtang counter at "30 cm", then an empty strip labelled "Kali Gandaki ·
Sept".

The tool that did see Langtang moving was satellite radar. It can measure ground motion from orbit to a fraction of an inch. It only works when someone processes the images in time. Then someone has to warn the valley when a slope speeds up. `[loop L4 closes]` `[loop L0 closes]`

**Picture:** g-monsoon-calendar returns: the red pin moves from "Sept 27" to "today", the "normal withdrawal" mark still
ahead · a trekking-season bar opening across October.

Trekking permits for the Annapurna trails reopened after a three day pause for the rain. Those trails share the roads through the Kali Gandaki valley. The climbing season on peaks like Himlung runs through October, and crews are already on those
mountains. At Himlung, the tents had been up for days. `[loop L7 open]`
