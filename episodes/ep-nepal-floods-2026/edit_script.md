### A1-S6 — narration

## A2 · The wrong end of the season (3:43)

### A2-S1 — narration

Visual: g-monsoon-calendar: a June to October bar, "onset" mark in mid June, "normal withdrawal" mark in early October, a red pin landing on "Sept 27" near the end.

> Nepal gets most of its rain from the summer monsoon, which usually arrives in mid June and starts leaving in early October. By late September, the hillsides have been soaking for more than three months. They go into the last storms of the year holding more water than at any other time.


### A2-S2 — narration

Visual: g-bay-of-bengal-track: a low pressure symbol forms over the Bay of Bengal, tracks north over India, a rain shield spreads across the Nepal border · News18 Bihar river-in-spate shot, muted.

> This storm began as a low pressure system over the Bay of Bengal, east of India, and moved north. Indian channels called it Cyclone Arnab, but it never became an official cyclone. By the time it reached the mountains it had lost its winds. It still carried the moisture of the ocean it came from.


### A2-S3 — narration

Visual: g-rain-bars: a small "northern India" label, two bars rising, "normal · 0.3 in" and "Sept 26–28 · 2.6 in", a multiplier tag landing on "nine times".

> Just across the border in northern India, Al Jazeera reported about two and a half inches of rain that weekend. The normal for those days is under a third of an inch, so it was close to nine times as much.


### A2-S4 — narration

Visual: g-pore-pressure full: slope cross-section, soil grains as circles, blue water filling the gaps, small arrows pushing grains apart, a "friction" bar dropping under a "weight" bar until the slope slips.

> A slope stays up because of friction between the grains of soil and rock inside it. Rain fills the tiny gaps between those grains, and the water pushes them apart from the inside. Geologists call that pore pressure. When it climbs high enough, the friction holding the slope drops below the weight pulling it down.


### A2-S5 — narration

> A soaked slope doesn't need the biggest storm of the year. It needs one heavy weekend on top of three wet months, and late September supplies it.


### A2-S6 — narration

Visual: a cutaway of the Kali Gandaki valley wall: bedrock in grey, a loose tan layer of old glacial gravel draped over it, labelled "glacial debris" · Lallantop slope or debris shot (second segment).

> The Kali Gandaki's walls are among the steepest in the Himalaya, and much of them is covered in old glacial debris. That's loose rock and gravel that ancient glaciers dragged downhill and dropped when they melted. It was never cemented into solid rock, so water moves through it fast, and it gives way fast.


### A2-S7 — narration

Visual: a Washington state map, a pin at Oso · popfig "March 22, 2014" · a USGS PD photo of the Oso slide scar (credit checked) · text on the voice: "43 lives lost".

> We've watched the same kind of ground fail at home. In March twenty fourteen, near Oso, Washington, a hillside of old glacial sediment collapsed after weeks of heavy rain. Forty three people lost their lives. The Oso hillside and the Kali Gandaki wall share the same recipe, loose glacial material on a steep slope, full of water.


### A2-S8 — narration

Visual: g-sep-2024 card: "Late Sept 2024 · Kathmandu Valley" with a count landing "more than 200 lives lost", then a calendar showing both Septembers with the last days circled.

> Nepal has seen this late September calendar before. Two years ago, in the last days of September, late rain flooded the Kathmandu Valley. It set off landslides that took more than two hundred lives, on almost the same dates as this storm. Higher up, the same kind of storm falls as snow.


### A2-S9 — narration

> On Himlung Himal, that snow sat above a camp full of workers.

> Langtang's collapse in August was heavier, a rock and ice avalanche. That's rock and glacier ice falling together, fast enough to flow. As it falls, friction melts some of the ice. The water turns the mass into a slurry that runs much farther than dry rock.

It said satellite radar showed the slope slid up to twelve inches in three to four weeks. ## A6 · After the rain (11:37)

### A6-S1 — narration

Visual: News18 Bihar river gauge or swollen river shot · g-sensor-gap full: a river gauge line rises only after a slope icon has already fallen in; a timeline marks "slope fails" then "gauge sees it".

> It measured rivers. Before this storm, Nepal's disaster agency put forty nine of its seventy seven districts on flash flood alert. Nepal's flood warnings come from river and rain gauges, which report when water rises and send alerts to the villages downstream.


### A6-S2 — narration

> A slope that fails in three seconds gives a river gauge nothing to read until the mud is already in the water. In August, the gauges nearest Langtang were swept away before they could warn anyone below.


### A6-S3 — narration

Visual: g-insar-creep returns small: the Langtang counter at "30 cm", then an empty strip labelled "Kali Gandaki · Sept".

> The tool that did see Langtang moving was satellite radar. It can measure ground motion from orbit to a fraction of an inch. It only works when someone processes the images in time. Then someone has to warn the valley when a slope speeds up.


### A6-S4 — narration

Visual: g-monsoon-calendar returns: the red pin moves from "Sept 27" to "today", the "normal withdrawal" mark still ahead · a trekking-season bar opening across October.

> Trekking permits for the Annapurna trails reopened after a three day pause for the rain. Those trails share the roads through the Kali Gandaki valley. The climbing season on peaks like Himlung runs through October, and crews are already on those mountains. At Himlung, the tents had been up for days.
