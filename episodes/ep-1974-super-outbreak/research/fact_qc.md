## The specific questions asked, answered in one line each

- **First Tanner F5: an hour or ninety minutes?** Neither can be sourced. NWS Huntsville (primary) gives 6:30 PM CDT touchdown near Newburg, entry into Limestone County about 7:05 PM, and 85 continuous miles in Alabama; Wikipedia gives 6:15–7:28 PM CDT (73 minutes); the "over 90 minutes / 52 miles" pairing is a TV retelling only. Drop the duration.
- **Guin 1970 population and the hour it was hit:** 2,220 people; the tornado moved through Guin about **9:04 PM CDT** (NWS Birmingham), roughly one hour fifty minutes after a 7:14 PM CDT sunset.
- **Brandenburg 1970 population:** **1,637**.
- **Cincinnati / Hamilton County death toll from the Sayler Park tornado:** **four**, with 199 injuries and $50,000,000 damage, per the WSO Cincinnati preliminary report of 9 April 1974. The office's whole warning area came to 7 dead, 331 injured, $82,390,000.
- **Fifteen tornadoes on the ground at once:** not found in any NWS or NOAA source. Wikipedia and secondary retellings only. Attribute or cut.
- **Average US tornado warning lead time today:** **thirteen minutes** (NOAA, Tornadoes 101).
- **Indiana time in April 1974:** Eastern **Standard** Time, UTC−5. Most of Indiana, White County included, sat out the federal year-round DST of 1974. Monticello was hit at 5:17 PM EST, which was 6:17 PM EDT in Louisville and Ohio.
- **Microburst and airliner crashes:** yes. Fujita's microburst came out of the Eastern Air Lines Flight 66 crash at JFK on 24 June 1975; acceptance followed Delta 191 at Dallas–Fort Worth on 2 August 1985, and Doppler radar went into airports as a result.
- **Do Wireless Emergency Alerts wake a phone?** No official source says so. NWS confirms tornado warnings trigger a WEA with "a special tone and vibration, both repeated twice"; overriding silent mode is device-dependent and is not claimed by NWS, FEMA or Ready.gov.
- **The Weather Bureau's post-outbreak public-awareness finding:** it belongs to 1965, not 1974. After 1974 the surveys found "most everyone was aware of the potential severe weather that day" and that "most people took the correct action."
- **Seven F5s in one day, unmatched?** Yes. 1974 holds the record at seven F5s; the 2011 Super Outbreak, the only larger outbreak by tornado count, produced four EF5s on 27 April 2011.
