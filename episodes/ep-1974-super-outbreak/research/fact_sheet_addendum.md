## 1. Tanner/Harvest corridor hit again, 27 April 2011

- The 2011 tornado that re-struck the Tanner/Harvest corridor was the **Hackleburg–Phil Campbell tornado**, an EF5 (initially rated EF3, upgraded 30 April 2011) with a 132-mile path across five Alabama counties — Marion, Franklin, Lawrence, Limestone, and Madison — making it the longest-tracked tornado of the 27 April 2011 outbreak and the deadliest single tornado in Alabama history at 72 deaths statewide. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Hackleburg%E2%80%93Phil_Campbell_tornado
- The tornado's path through Limestone and Madison counties directly overlapped the 1974 Tanner corridor: it destroyed "several homes, a mobile home park, and a church that were destroyed by the 1974 Tanner tornadoes and later rebuilt," then crossed into Madison County "tearing through the suburban communities of Harvest and Toney." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2011_Hackleburg%E2%80%93Phil_Campbell_tornado
- Limestone County recorded 4 deaths from the 2011 tornado, versus 16 deaths in Limestone County from the two 1974 Tanner-corridor F5s combined. https://enewscourier.com/2011/12/28/nws-tornado-paths-for-1974-2011-match/
- NWS meteorologist Tim Troutman and University of North Alabama geography professor Greg Gaston conducted a joint 2008–2011 track-comparison study; after the 2011 event, their analysis found "the two paths match almost exactly" — reported by the local Athens newspaper, which had itself noted the resemblance before the official study confirmed it. This is the closest available sourced NWS-linked statement comparing 1974 and 2011 on the same ground; no direct verbatim NWS Huntsville press-release quote was located. [unverified — paraphrase of a meteorologist's finding via local newspaper, not a verbatim NWS statement] https://enewscourier.com/2011/12/28/nws-tornado-paths-for-1974-2011-match/
- Researchers were "not surprised at the scope of the destruction because more homes and businesses were located in the storm's path than 37 years before," but attributed fewer 2011 fatalities relative to damage extent "most likely due to better warnings." https://enewscourier.com/2011/12/28/nws-tornado-paths-for-1974-2011-match/
- Limestone County population: 41,699 in 1970 → 103,570 in 2020 (2.5x growth). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limestone_County,_Alabama
- Madison County population: 186,540 in 1970 → 388,153 in 2020 (2.1x growth). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madison_County,_Alabama

## 2. Monticello, Indiana — the Otterbein-to-Wolcottville F4 (121 miles)

- The tornado struck Monticello (White County seat) at 5:17 p.m. EDT/local, having formed near Otterbein in Benton County and eventually crossing into Noble County near Fort Wayne — the outbreak's longest single tornado track at roughly 121 miles, on the ground over 100 miles by some tellings. https://wibc.com/67423/super-outbreak-74-the-monticello-f4-tornado/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tornadoes_in_the_1974_Super_Outbreak
- **Downtown damage:** the White County Courthouse was destroyed — a safe fell from the third floor clear through to the first floor. A railroad bridge over the Tippecanoe River, about a quarter mile downstream of downtown, was lifted and knocked out of alignment. At the Washington Street bridge, a church van/mini-bus carrying six girls from a Fort Wayne church group was swept roughly 50 feet off the bridge into the Tippecanoe River below. https://wibc.com/67423/super-outbreak-74-the-monticello-f4-tornado/
- **Death toll conflict:** WIBC's retrospective states "ten people died in White County; at least seven more perished before the tornado dissipated," totaling 17+; Monticello's own Tornado Memorial Park materials state "eight people perished" (likely a Monticello-city-only count distinct from the White-County/full-track total); the main fact sheet's Wikipedia-sourced figure of 18 sits within this range. Treat 18 (full track, all counties) as the number to lead with, and flag the 8/10/17 sub-counts as [unverified] city-vs-county splits. https://wibc.com/67423/super-outbreak-74-the-monticello-f4-tornado/ ; https://www.monticelloin.gov/tornado-memorial-park
- **Named survivor:** Rod Poole, later head of Monticello's water department, witnessed the tornado and recalled: "The greenish, browning color, I know everybody says they sound like a train. Well, they do. It was just a real deep sound and an ugly color." https://wibc.com/67423/super-outbreak-74-the-monticello-f4-tornado/

## 3. The forecast itself, 3 April 1974

- No primary NSSFC document giving specific tornado watch numbers, exact issue times, or watch-area polygons for 3 April 1974 could be located in this pass — NWS/NCDC historical watch logs for this date were not found openly indexed. [unverified — could not confirm exact watch numbers/times from a primary source] https://www.spc.noaa.gov/history/timeline.html
- What is confirmed: NSSFC director Allen Pearson recognized the outbreak potential on 2 April 1974 (the day before) and called every NWS office in the eastern US to service radars, add staff, and prime satellites for severe weather mode. Independently, a 2004 Risk Management Solutions technical retrospective states NOAA "issued preliminary severe weather alerts on the morning of April 2" for the central and southern plains — consistent with, but not identical to, Pearson's office-by-office calls; treat as corroborating context, not the same action. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Pearson ; https://www.weather.gov/media/hun/stormsurveys/1974-04-03/documents/1974supertornadoreport.pdf [RMS 2004 report, hosted on NWS Huntsville's site — a secondary technical report, not an NWS-authored primary document]
- **What "tornado watch" meant to the public in 1974:** following the outbreak, the Weather Bureau/NWS found "the public did not know about and appreciate the Weather Bureau's capability to forecast tornadoes and did not understand the tornado hazard," which drove the term's later refinement (from "tornado forecast") and an aggressive public-education push. No specific percentage of public awareness in 1974 was located in this pass. [unverified — no numeric survey figure found] https://weather.com/safety/tornado/news/2020-04-03-april-1974-super-outbreak-tornadoes
- SPC's own institutional timeline confirms the watch/forecast terminology lineage: first "watch" (then called "forecast") issued 17 March 1952; first successful one 21 March 1952; the office renamed the National Severe Storms Forecast Center (NSSFC) in 1966, when SELS' tornado and severe-thunderstorm forecasts were formally renamed "watches" to align with National Hurricane Center terminology; aviation and public watches were combined into one product line in 1973, the year before the Super Outbreak. https://www.spc.noaa.gov/history/timeline.html
- **First warning of the day, by office:** this addendum could not confirm which NWS office issued the single earliest tornado warning nationally on 3 April 1974. The earliest specific, timestamped warning activity found in a primary document is WSO Cincinnati's chronological log, which shows Special Weather Statements (non-tornado) as early as 10:50 a.m. EDT and its first Tornado Warning at 4:00 p.m. EDT for Butler and Warren counties, Ohio — this is a regional office's first warning of its own local event window, not necessarily the outbreak's first tornado warning nationally. [unverified as the national "first" — confirmed only as WSO Cincinnati's first of the day] https://www.weather.gov/media/iln/events/19740403/CVG_Report.pdf
- **1948 Tinker AFB lineage:** the modern watch/warning system traces directly to 25 March 1948, when Air Force officers Maj. Ernest Fawbush and Capt. Robert Miller issued the first operational tornado forecast, correctly anticipating a second tornado at Tinker Air Force Base, Oklahoma, five days after an unforecast tornado had struck the same base — the event credited with launching operational severe-weather forecasting and, by extension, the NSSFC/SPC watch system used in 1974. https://www.weather.gov/oun/events-19480325 ; https://www.spc.noaa.gov/history/timeline.html

## 4. Hanover College

- The tornado struck the Hanover College campus (Jefferson County, Indiana) at 3:51 p.m. EDT, part of the same squall line responsible for the DePauw/Madison-area tornadoes; nearly 1,000 students were on campus that afternoon, some in active class sessions. https://www.hanover.edu/about/news/2024/50-years-1974-tornado/
- Damage: more than 30 of the campus's roughly 33 buildings were damaged, two destroyed outright, for about $10 million in 1974 dollars (about $62 million in 2024 dollars). https://www.hanover.edu/about/news/2024/50-years-1974-tornado/
- **Casualties: zero deaths on campus** and only sporadic, minor injuries — Hanover's own 50th-anniversary retrospective calls this outcome "miraculous" given the number of people present and the scale of damage. https://www.hanover.edu/about/news/2024/50-years-1974-tornado/
- **Where people sheltered:** the college's own account is specific only about the *aftermath*, not the shelter locations during the tornado itself — it states then-president John Horner gathered the campus community on the patio of the J. Graham Brown Campus Center that evening, after the storm passed. No sourced quote describing where students/staff sheltered *during* the tornado (basements, interior halls, etc.) was found in the pages reviewed. [unverified — shelter-location detail not found in primary/college-archive sources reviewed] https://www.hanover.edu/about/news/2024/50-years-1974-tornado/
- Dr. Greg Forbes, a severe-weather expert connected to Hanover College, was featured in a 2024 commemoration event tied to the anniversary; no direct quote from him about the 1974 event itself was found in the page reviewed. [unverified — no quote located] https://www.hanover.edu/about/news/2024/forbes-1974-tornado-commemoration/

## 5. Xenia aftermath

- **Nixon's visit:** President Richard Nixon visited Xenia on 9 April 1974 — six days after the tornado — flying into Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Dayton, surveying the devastation by helicopter, then driving into town to meet residents. https://www.yahoo.com/news/ap-1974-tornado-xenia-ohio-040639251.html ; https://www.nixonlibrary.gov/research/almanac/april-9-1974
- **Ohio National Guard:** Governor John J. Gilligan ordered 250 Guardsmen into service in Xenia immediately; by midnight the same day, 1,445 Guard troops were on duty statewide, more than half of them in Xenia. https://www.yahoo.com/news/ap-1974-tornado-xenia-ohio-040639251.html
- **The seven schools:** secondary sources converge on the same core list. Destroyed: Xenia High School, Central Junior High School, McKinley Elementary, Simon Kenton Elementary, and Arrowood Elementary (five schools destroyed). Also damaged: Saint Brigid Catholic School (destroyed per one source) and Warner Junior High (damaged but repaired well enough to reopen for classes) — together accounting for the "seven schools" figure already in the main fact sheet. [unverified — no single primary NWS/district document listing all seven by name in one place was found; this is a synthesis of multiple secondary retrospectives] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenia_High_School ; https://www.ideastream.org/2024-04-02/xenia-tornado-april-1974-tvkiese
- **School-in-session detail:** the tornado hit central Xenia at approximately 4:40 p.m. EDT. No source in this pass gave a definitive statement of whether Xenia High School classes were still in session or had just been dismissed at that hour; the drama-club rehearsal detail already in the main fact sheet (students moved from the stage area to a hallway seconds before a school bus was thrown onto the stage) indicates after-school activities were underway, which argues against "school had just let out" as a clean description — students were still on campus for extracurriculars, not departed. [unverified as a direct confirmation/contradiction of a specific "school had just let out" line; inferred from the existing drama-rehearsal detail]

## 6. Louisville and Sayler Park

- Louisville, Kentucky population per the 1970 census: 361,472, the 39th-largest US city at the time. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_United_States_census
- **Crescent Hill:** Dick Gilbert, WHAS radio's helicopter traffic reporter (call sign "Skywatch 84"), grew up on Pennsylvania Avenue in the Crescent Hill neighborhood — a street directly in the tornado's path — and broadcast live as the storm heavily damaged the Louisville Water Company's Crescent Hill pumping station, warning residents in the tornado's path to take cover as he flew over it. https://www.lpm.org/remembering-1974-louisville-tornado-40-years-later/ ; https://www.lpm.org/news/2014-04-02/remembering-the-1974-louisville-tornado-40-years-later
- **Kentucky Fair and Exposition Center:** an F4 tornado touched down in Louisville at 5:37 p.m. EDT, one-quarter mile north of Standiford Field airport, and tore off part of the roof of Freedom Hall at the Kentucky Fair and Exposition Center (KFEC) while also destroying several horse barns on the fairgrounds; total KFEC damage exceeded $2 million. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kentucky_Exposition_Center
- **Sayler Park death toll — conflicting counts:** Wikipedia's "1974 Cincinnati tornado" article gives 6 total deaths for that tornado (5 direct, 1 indirect) region-wide, but states only 1 death occurred within Sayler Park proper; the main fact sheet's Rising Sun/Taylorsport/Sayler Park entry cites 3 deaths for the full multi-state tornado track. The WSO Cincinnati preliminary report (primary source, dated 9 April 1974) records 4 fatalities and 199 injuries for Hamilton County, Ohio specifically (which contains Sayler Park), against a $50,000,000 damage figure for that county — but this Hamilton County total may include deaths beyond the Sayler Park tornado's Cincinnati-side path. Treat the exact Sayler-Park-only death count as [unverified]; the primary CVG report's Hamilton County total (4) is the most authoritative single-county figure located. https://www.weather.gov/media/iln/events/19740403/CVG_Report.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Cincinnati_tornado
- **WSO Cincinnati warning wording (verbatim from the primary preliminary report, dated 9 April 1974):** at 4:00 p.m. EDT, "Tornado Warning.....valid until 5:00 pm for persons in Butler and Warren counties in Ohio.....based on hook echoes on WSO CVG Radar and reports of funnels in SE Indiana along the Ohio River heard over the Indiana NAWAS." At 4:05 p.m. EDT: "Tornado Warning.....valid until 5:15 pm EDT for Boone County, Kentucky and Hamilton County in Ohio....based again on hook echoes on CVG Radar and Indiana NAWAS reports of funnels." At 4:45 p.m. EDT: "Tornado Warning.....valid until 5:45 pm EDT for Switzerland, Ohio, and Dearborn counties in Indiana, Hamilton and Clermont counties in Ohio, and Boone County in Kentucky....based on hook echoes on CVG Radar." This means Hamilton County (containing Sayler Park) was under a named tornado warning from 4:05 p.m. EDT — roughly 27 minutes before the tornado's ~4:32 p.m. EDT arrival in the Sayler Park area per the main fact sheet's timeline — refining the main sheet's single-warning note (4:45 p.m.) to show an earlier, standing warning already in effect. https://www.weather.gov/media/iln/events/19740403/CVG_Report.pdf
- The same report's closing assessment: "the extremely critical period between 4:30 pm EDT and 5:45 pm EDT was well covered in advance by warnings which had alerted almost the entire warning area to the possibility of severe weather... the performance of the radar while power was available and the warnings issued saved many lives." A 5:40 p.m.–8:53 p.m. EDT total power outage at WSO Cincinnati (loss of teletype, radar, incoming phones, and Indiana/Kentucky NAWAS) followed, forcing the office to relay later warnings by phone through WSFO Cleveland. https://www.weather.gov/media/iln/events/19740403/CVG_Report.pdf
- WSO Cincinnati's full county-warning-area toll for the whole 3–4 April event (Ohio, Kentucky, and Indiana counties combined): 7 fatalities, 331 injuries, $82,390,000 damage. https://www.weather.gov/media/iln/events/19740403/CVG_Report.pdf

## 7. Damage indicators of the original Fujita Scale

Verbatim wording and wind ranges from the NWS Storm Prediction Center's legacy F-scale reference page (the 1971 Fujita scale used at the time of the outbreak, before the 2007 Enhanced Fujita transition already noted in the main fact sheet):

- **F2 (113–157 mph):** "Roofs torn off frame houses; mobile homes demolished; boxcars overturned; large trees snapped or uprooted; light-object missiles generated; cars lifted off ground." https://www.spc.noaa.gov/faq/tornado/f-scale.html
- **F3 (158–206 mph):** "Roofs and some walls torn off well-constructed houses; trains overturned; most trees in forest uprooted; heavy cars lifted off the ground and thrown." https://www.spc.noaa.gov/faq/tornado/f-scale.html
- **F4 (207–260 mph):** "Well-constructed houses leveled; structures with weak foundations blown away some distance; cars thrown and large missiles generated." https://www.spc.noaa.gov/faq/tornado/f-scale.html
- **F5 (261–318 mph):** "Strong frame houses leveled off foundations and swept away; automobile-sized missiles fly through the air in excess of 100 meters (109 yds); trees debarked; incredible phenomena will occur." https://www.spc.noaa.gov/faq/tornado/f-scale.html
- This gives a clean "how to read a rating from a house" ladder for a graphics card: F2 = roof gone, mobile home demolished; F3 = walls gone, heavy car thrown; F4 = whole well-built house leveled; F5 = house swept clean off its foundation, car-sized objects flying 100+ meters, trees stripped of bark.
