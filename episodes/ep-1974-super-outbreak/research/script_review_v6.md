## HOOK

**8 / 10.**

Sentence one is the title payoff in plain words with the number first ("Seven F5 tornadoes in one
day, and it has never happened again"), and the 63-word hook lands four escalating facts and the
episode's question without a single announced move, a metaphor, or a banned opener.

It loses two points for stacking three number-slabs before any human enters ("Three hundred and
thirty people dead." is a verbless fragment doing the job a person should do), and for a promise
that never clearly lands: "the worst of the night hadn't even formed yet" tells the viewer a worse
tornado is coming, but the deadliest tornado of the day (Xenia, 34 dead) was already on the ground
at the moment the five hooks were on the scope, and nothing later in the script beats it.

## STAKES CURVE

Tension rises cleanly H → A1 → A2 → A3, peaks across A3/A4 (five hooks, the office going dark, two
F5s through the same rescuers, the Guin tape), then drops through A5 and stays down through A6.

Three sags:

1. **I (0:25–1:55).** The act carries the day's biggest numbers and the one forecaster, but nobody
   is hurt in it and nothing is at risk. It is 246 words of setup between the hook and the first
   death, the longest stretch in the episode without a human consequence.
2. **A3-S5 (Georgia, ~11:15).** Forty words, no person, no visual event of its own, and a clock
   claim that does not survive checking. It interrupts a peak instead of extending it.
3. **A5 (15:00–18:30).** This is the designed detective act and the payoffs are real (F6 taken back,
   the microburst), but only one named person appears (Pennington, and he is imported from Guin),
   the death count stops moving, and the synoptic explainer is the longest sentence in the script.
   The recovery lever is already in the room: Fujita's ten months and 10,000 miles is a person doing
   something obsessive, and the script currently states it rather than staying with him.

A6 is not a sag in information, but it flattens emotionally: it is a comparison table read aloud
until Gus Allen returns in the last 130 words. The strongest close material is the last four
sentences, and they arrive after a column-by-column recitation.

## REGISTER

Lines that will not read as plain spoken American through pocket-TTS.

**Comma splices that TTS will run together as one breath:**

- A3-S3: "Dick Gilbert reported traffic for WHAS radio, he grew up on Pennsylvania Avenue in
  Crescent Hill, and the tornado was on his street." (splice between clauses one and two)
- A6-S2: "So the real question isn't whether it happens again, it's whether the warning reaches
  you." (splice, and see the banned shape below)
- A6-S1b: "More houses in the path and fewer dead, and the researchers, they said why in two words,
  most likely: better warnings." (splice plus a mid-narration colon — TTS reads straight through it,
  and "in two words" then delivers three)

**Banned sentence shapes (humanise rule 7):**

- A6-S2: "So the real question isn't whether it happens again, it's whether the warning reaches
  you." — the reveal-flip, explicitly named in the house additions.
- A5-S3: "That's an outbreak." — the summary tag ("That's the mechanism.") by another name.
- A3-S3: "One man, one helicopter and one radio station, and that was the warning." — the drama
  stack.
- A6-S3 re-asks the hook verbatim: "So if he knew, why did so many people die?" — the mirrored
  callback ending. The answer that follows it is good; the re-ask is the banned part.
- **Balanced-contrast engine used four times** (rule 7 allows one per script): A1-S2 "Fifteen
  minutes is enough to get below ground, but few homes in Brandenburg had a below ground to get
  to."; A2-S3 "Thirty minutes was enough time, but it wasn't enough shelter."; A6-S3 "A storm
  shelter or an inside room on the lowest floor is close enough to reach in that time."; A6-S3 "A
  few minutes is enough to get below ground and not enough to get out of town." Three of the four
  are the identical "enough X, not enough Y" frame, and two of them sit in the same segment.

**Anaphora runs (3+ sentences opening on the same word), both in A6-S2/S3:**

- "In 1974 the radar saw rain… / In 1974 the warning was a radio… / In 1974 a good warning was
  fifteen minutes…"
- "A storm shelter or an inside room… / A car trying to outrun… / A few minutes is enough…"

**Rhetorical questions in narration (humanise rule 8 bans them outright; script-os records the
owner's 2026-09-14 decision as "the open fact is stated, not asked"). Five in the body:**

- H: "So if he knew, why did so many people die?"
- A3-S2: "How do you warn a city when your radar can't see the thing you're warning about?"
- A6-S1: "So what happened the second time?"
- A6-S2: "Could April third happen again?"
- A6-S3: "So if he knew, why did so many people die?" (the repeat)

The hook's question is the one defensible case — it is the packaging promise — but the other four
are all seams that the rule says should be stamps or stated facts.

**Not plain American / other register defects:**

- I: "the charts for the third of April scared him" — Oxford date phrasing. American spoken: "the
  charts for April third". The script says "April third" everywhere else; this is the odd one out.
- A6-S1: "Put another map on top of the 1974 tracks." — an instruction to the viewer to picture
  something (humanise rule 2), and it narrates the graphic instead of the fact.
- A6-S3: "his sister, his brother and his small cousin" — "small cousin" is not how anyone says it;
  A1-S3 already gave the right words, "a two year old cousin".
- A4-S3: "By midnight there was nothing left to count but the dead" — decoration wrapped around two
  real numbers, and it is also factually loose (see FACT FLAGS).
- A5-S3: "You take warm, wet air off the Gulf of Mexico, put a layer of dry air over the top of it,
  run a jet stream above that to turn the whole column, and send a cold front through to set it
  off." — 42 words, the longest sentence in the script, and a second-person recipe.
- A6-S2: "because a warning is only as good as the radio it plays on and the basement you can
  reach" — aphorism.
- Z: "See you in the next one." — sign-off line, banned by voice rule 13.

**Trailer fragments under six words:** none that are true fragments. The short sentences that exist
are all complete ("He wrote down a six." 5w, "Xenia stayed an F5." 4w, "He stayed." 2w, "That's an
outbreak." 3w, "What changed is the warning." 5w) and all but "That's an outbreak." earn their
place as kickers.

**Clean:** no em dashes, no semicolons, no hedges (arguably / perhaps / very / really / truly), no
banned vocabulary, no "picture this", no personified storms, no invented quotes. Every quoted line
traces to a fact sheet entry.

**Do the palette rules hold? Yes, on every count.**

- None twice within three minutes of narration: the four devices sit at ~3:40, ~9:50, ~12:40 and
  ~19:55. The closest pair is 2h50m apart in narration time and they are different devices; the two
  repeated-subject uses are sixteen minutes apart.
- None in neighbouring chapters: A1 (repeated subject) → A2 (none) → A3 (repeated verb) → A4 (aside)
  → A5 (none) → A6 (repeated subject). A3 and A4 are neighbours but carry different devices, which
  the rule permits.
- At most three of each per 20 minutes: repeated subject ×2, repeated verb ×1, aside ×1, opener ×0,
  check-in ×0. All under cap.

**The failure here is the opposite of the one the palette was written for.** The palette asks for
"one noticeable device every two to three minutes" — that is seven to nine in a 20-minute script.
This script has four, and two of the four are in the last third. Minutes 0:00–3:40, 4:00–9:50 and
15:00–19:55 contain no spoken device at all, which is where the narration reads most like a
well-written document and least like a person. Add two or three, spread into I, A2 and A5, and vary
the type.

**"uh" / "um" / self-corrections / doubled negatives:** none. Scanned the full narration for "uh",
"um", "maybe", "or rather", hedge-then-correct constructions and the "not before, not since" rhythm
pattern. Zero hits. The one "just" in the script is inside Gus Allen's quote ("It was just a bad
day"), which is verbatim and stays.

One placement objection, not a rule break: the aside "honestly" lands on
"it went straight through the people doing the rescuing." That sentence is the emotional peak of the
episode and the aside asks the viewer to notice the narrator instead of the fact. Move the device to
a lower-stakes line and let this one land flat.

## FACT FLAGS

### Errors and contradictions (fix before TTS)

1. **A4-S1, internal contradiction on the first Tanner F5.** "a tornado formed near Moulton,
   Alabama, and stayed on the ground for ninety minutes and fifty two miles… and by 7:30 PM the
   neighbors were out in the wreckage." Ninety minutes from 6:20 PM runs to 7:50 PM, so the tornado
   is still on the ground while the narration puts rescuers in its wreckage. The fact sheet timeline
   gives 23:20–00:21 UTC = **6:20–7:21 PM CDT, about an hour**; section 1 says "about 90 minutes."
   The two fact-sheet figures disagree. Take the timeline (about an hour), which is the only one
   that makes 7:30 PM rescuers possible.
2. **A1-S1 attributes a full-path death toll to one town.** "a town with a warning, with fifteen
   minutes to get ready, would lose thirty one people." The fact sheet reads "31 deaths (28 in the
   Brandenburg area)". A1-S2 gets this right ("thirty one people were dead along its path"); A1-S1
   does not.
3. **A3-S5, "That was four states between five o'clock and seven."** The passage names three:
   Indiana (Monticello, 5:17 PM EDT), Kentucky (Louisville, 5:37 PM EDT), Georgia (Resaca,
   6:30–7:00 PM EDT). Either name the fourth or say three. Alabama's first Tanner F5 formed at
   6:20 PM CDT = 7:20 PM EDT, outside the stated window.
4. **A3-S5, "more than four hundred miles south, in Georgia."** No anchor is given for where the
   400 miles is measured from. Read in sequence it attaches to Louisville, which is about 300 miles
   from Resaca; Monticello to Resaca is about 450. The rule in CLAUDE.md is that a spatial claim
   gets a map — this one also needs a named origin in the words.
5. **A4-S3, "By midnight there was nothing left to count but the dead."** The fact sheet dates the
   outbreak "3–4 April" over "a roughly 16–24 hour period," and the Guin tornado's own dissipation
   is cited at 10:30–10:57 PM CDT with activity continuing past midnight. Midnight was not the end.
6. **A4-S3, "three hundred and thirty of them by the government's number."** Two government numbers
   exist and the fact sheet says to report both: NCEI 335, NOAA 330. "The government's number" is
   the one phrasing that cannot be defended. Name the agency.
7. **A3-S3, "It tore the roof off Freedom Hall."** The addendum says it "tore off part of the roof."
8. **I, "two thousand six hundred miles."** Sourced figure is 2,598 (NCEI). Humanise rule 11 wants
   the exact figure; "two thousand five hundred and ninety eight" is one syllable-run longer and
   sounds researched.
9. **I, "for the next eight hours every one that followed found a town."** Contradicted by the
   script's own next segment, in which the first F5 runs 62 miles "through six counties and never a
   town," and by the fact sheet's 148-tornado count, most of them rural. The eight hours is right
   (2:16 PM to 10:57 PM CDT); the "every one" is not.
10. **H, "the worst of the night hadn't even formed yet."** At the five-hooks moment (~4:45 PM EDT)
    the deadliest tornado of the outbreak, Xenia, was already on the ground. Defensible only if
    "the worst of the night" means Guin specifically, which the viewer has no way to know.

## TOP TEN FIXES

1. **Fix the Tanner timeline contradiction (A4-S1).** Replace "stayed on the ground for ninety
   minutes and fifty two miles" with "stayed on the ground for about an hour and fifty two miles",
   which matches the fact sheet's 6:20–7:21 PM CDT timeline and makes the 7:30 PM rescuers
   possible.
2. **Cut the four rhetorical questions in the body** (keep only the hook's). A3-S2: replace "How do
   you warn a city when your radar can't see the thing you're warning about?" with "So the office
   had to warn a city of half a million off a shape on the edge of a storm." A6-S1: replace "So what
   happened the second time?" with "The second time, the numbers went the other way." A6-S2:
   replace "Could April third happen again? Yes, it could, because…" with "April third can happen
   again, because…" A6-S3: replace the re-asked "So if he knew, why did so many people die?" with
   "Pearson knew a day early, and it bought nobody anything without a radio and a basement."
3. **Break the two anaphora runs in A6.** Rewrite the second "In 1974…" as "Back then the warning
   was a radio you had to have switched on, and today it's the phone in your pocket, and it will
   wake you up", and the third as "A good warning in 1974 was fifteen minutes and most places got
   less." In A6-S3, fold the first two "A…" sentences together: "A storm shelter or an inside room
   on the lowest floor you can reach in a few minutes; a car trying to outrun a mile-wide tornado
   is not."
4. **Retire two of the three "enough X, not enough Y" sentences.** Keep A1-S2 ("Fifteen minutes is
   enough to get below ground, but few homes in Brandenburg had a below ground to get to"), which
