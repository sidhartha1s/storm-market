# 1974 Super Outbreak — Fact Sheet
Sourced research for a 22-minute documentary on the 3–4 April 1974 Super Outbreak. Every fact line ends with its source URL. Anything not confirmed from a primary source is marked [unverified].

---

## 1. The day in numbers

- Total tornadoes confirmed: 148 in the US and Canada (Ontario) in a roughly 16–24 hour period, 3–4 April 1974. https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/april-3-4-1974-super-outbreak
- States/provinces affected: 13 US states plus Ontario, Canada. https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/april-3-4-1974-super-outbreak
- Total combined tornado path length: 2,598 miles. https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/april-3-4-1974-super-outbreak
- Violent tornadoes (F4/F5): 30 tornadoes rated F4 or F5 — the most violent outbreak on record by that measure. https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/april-3-4-1974-super-outbreak
- Peak simultaneous activity: as many as 15 separate tornadoes on the ground at once. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Super_Outbreak
- Total dead: NCEI states 335 direct fatalities; NOAA's 50th-anniversary release states 330 dead; Wikipedia's synthesis gives a range of 310–335 across sources — treat 315–335 as the contested band and report both NCEI's 335 and NOAA's 330. https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/april-3-4-1974-super-outbreak ; https://www.noaa.gov/news/50-years-later-remembering-1974-tornado-super-outbreak
- Total injured: NCEI/Fujita's tally says 5,484; NOAA's anniversary piece says "at least 5,484"; other secondary accounts round up to "over 6,000" — 5,484 is the Fujita-survey figure and the one to lead with, noting the higher rounded figures as [unverified] press estimates. https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/april-3-4-1974-super-outbreak
- Damage, 1974 dollars: $600 million cited by multiple secondary sources (NCEI gives a 2024-CPI-adjusted figure instead, see below); the original $600M figure is common in secondary retrospectives and should be flagged [unverified against a primary 1974 dollar figure — NCEI's own page does not restate the original nominal number, only inflation-adjusted]. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Super_Outbreak
- Damage, inflation-adjusted: NCEI states losses of approximately $5.3 billion in 2024 dollars. https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/april-3-4-1974-super-outbreak

### The seven F5 tornadoes
1. **DePauw–Martinsburg–Daisy Hill / Huffman–Underwood, Indiana** — 2:16–3:25 PM CDT, 62 miles, 6 deaths, ~76–95 injured. Touched down 3 miles south of Huffman, IN, crossed Perry, Crawford, Harrison, Washington, Clark and Scott counties, missing any large community. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tornadoes_in_the_1974_Super_Outbreak ; https://w2.weather.gov/ind/april3_1974tor [NWS Indianapolis page could not be retrieved directly — 404/DNS error; figures cross-checked against Wikipedia's outbreak list, which cites NCDC Storm Data] [unverified against the primary NWS Indianapolis source]
2. **Brandenburg, Kentucky / Mauckport / Laconia, Indiana** — 3:30–4:22 PM CDT (local: struck Brandenburg mid-afternoon), 34 miles, 31 deaths (28 in the Brandenburg area), roughly 270 injured, ~2,400-foot width, destroyed 128 homes and 30 businesses in Brandenburg. Warning issued about 15 minutes before the town was hit. https://www.weather.gov/lmk/april31974_in_the_path ; https://www.weather.gov/lmk/top10tornadoes
3. **Bellbrook–Xenia–Wilberforce, Ohio** — touched down near Lower Bellbrook Road ~4:33 PM EDT, hit central Xenia ~4:40 PM, lifted near South Charleston; ~32-mile path, roughly half a mile to a mile wide; 32–34 deaths in Xenia (a total of 34 died along the full path per NWS Wilmington); about 1,150–1,300 injured; ~1,400 buildings including 7 schools damaged or destroyed; damage estimated near $250 million (1974 dollars) for this single tornado. https://www.weather.gov/iln/19740403 ; https://www.noaa.gov/news/50-years-later-remembering-1974-tornado-super-outbreak
4. **Rising Sun, Indiana / Taylorsport, Kentucky / Sayler Park, Ohio** — 4:28–4:51 PM CDT/EDT, roughly 21 miles, 3 deaths, ~210 injured; WSO Cincinnati issued a warning at 4:45 PM as this tornado approached the Sayler Park area of Cincinnati. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tornadoes_in_the_1974_Super_Outbreak ; https://www.weather.gov/iln/19740403
5. **Moulton–Trinity–Tanner, Alabama (first Tanner F5)** — formed roughly 6:20–6:30 PM CDT in Lawrence County, on the ground about 90 minutes, ~51–52 miles, ended in Madison County; 28 deaths, over 250 injured. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_tornadoes_in_the_1974_Super_Outbreak ; https://www.waaytv.com/news/alabama/remembering-the-deadly-impact-of-the-1974-tornado-super-outbreak-in-north-alabama/article_e2fae1e8-f116-11ee-9158-2f139a26c420.html
6. **Tanner–Harvest–Hazel Green, Alabama / Flintville, Tennessee (second Tanner F5)** — formed about 7:30–7:35 PM CDT, roughly one hour and one mile west of the first Tanner tornado's track, traveled about 98 miles before lifting in Coffee County, Tennessee; 16 additional deaths cited by one secondary account (a combined Tanner-area toll of "over fifteen lives each, fifty-five combined" appears in another secondary source) — the exact per-tornado death split for the two Tanner F5s is inconsistent across secondary retrospectives and should be treated as [unverified] pending the NWS Huntsville primary page, which returned a 404 when fetched directly. https://tornadohistory.fandom.com/wiki/1974_Super_Outbreak ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Tanner_tornadoes
7. **Guin, Alabama** — formed 8:25 PM CDT near Caledonia, Lowndes County, MS, dissipated 10:30–10:57 PM CDT near the Tennessee River in Morgan County, AL (accounts differ on the exact end time by roughly 27 minutes); duration cited as either "2 hours 5 minutes" or "2 hours 32 minutes" depending on source — flagged [unverified] pending a single authoritative timestamp; path length 101–102.4 miles, maximum width 1,350 yards; 28–32 deaths (Tornado Talk's oral-history-informed count is 32, including two later deaths from complications; other sources say 28) — treat 28 as the NCDC Storm Data figure and 32 as Tornado Talk's revised count, both cited; 272–332 injured; $45 million damage (1974 dollars); 737 houses destroyed including 191 mobile homes. https://www.tornadotalk.com/guin-al-f5-tornado-april-3-1974/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Guin_tornado

## 3. Named people with a verifiable story

- **Dick Gilbert**, WHAS-AM helicopter traffic reporter, Louisville, KY — followed the Louisville F4 tornado by air on 3 April 1974, including as it damaged the Louisville Water Company's Crescent Hill pumping station, and gave live, real-time directional updates; many credit him with saving lives; he received a special commendation from President Nixon for the tracking, and then-Governor Wendell Ford commended the WHAS station staff. https://www.lpm.org/news/2014-04-02/remembering-the-1974-louisville-tornado-40-years-later ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Super_Outbreak
- **David Heath**, drama/English teacher, Xenia High School, Xenia, OH — was directing an after-school rehearsal of the play "The Boy Friend" when the Xenia F5 struck; students had moved to the main hallway seconds before a school bus was thrown by the wind onto the stage where they had just been standing. [Source is a secondary retrospective account; the students' names are not given in the sources reviewed] [unverified beyond secondary reporting] https://cedars.cedarville.edu/2024/04/50-years-after-the-tornado-don-and-pat-dunstans-xenia-survival-story/
- **Dr. Tetsuya Theodore "Ted" Fujita**, University of Chicago — spent roughly ten months conducting the most extensive aerial tornado damage survey then attempted (over 10,000 miles of aerial survey), personally mapping and hand-drawing all 148 tornado tracks by F-scale rating; coined the term "Super Outbreak"; initially rated the Xenia tornado as high as F6 in early analysis before concluding such an extreme rating was "inconceivable" and settling on F5. https://www.chicagomag.com/city-life/april-2011/ted-fujita-chicagos-mr-tornado/ ; https://www.climatecentral.org/news/innovation-in-tornado-technology-born-from-1974-tragedy-17258
- **Allen Pearson**, Director, National Severe Storms Forecast Center (NSSFC), Kansas City — on 2 April 1974, the day before the outbreak, telephoned every NWS office in the eastern half of the country to have radars serviced, request extra staffing, and put satellites into severe-weather mode in anticipation of a major event; received the Department of Commerce Gold Medal in 1974 for his forecasting of the outbreak. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Pearson
- **Mildred McGuire**, owner of McGuire Drugs, Guin, AL — survived the Guin F5 with a broken pelvis; later wrote the poem "The Spirit of Guin" about the town's resilience. https://www.tornadotalk.com/guin-al-f5-tornado-april-3-1974/
- **Allen Lindley**, Guin, AL — recorded roughly a minute of the tornado's approach and 18 seconds of the vortex passing overhead on audio tape through an open window: "I have the thing on tape." https://www.tornadotalk.com/guin-al-f5-tornado-april-3-1974/
- **Becky Todd**, Guin, AL — family car was overturned by the tornado; recalled telling her family "I told them that was it. George told us to grab the seats, to hold on"; her sons Mark and Paul died in the storm. https://www.tornadotalk.com/guin-al-f5-tornado-april-3-1974/
- **Woodrow Haney**, Guin, AL — sheltered in a storm cellar and described the sound: "We heard it coming like two or three freight trains plus maybe a jet bomber"; helped pull injured teenager Janet Brown from debris (Brown later died). https://www.tornadotalk.com/guin-al-f5-tornado-april-3-1974/
- **Robert Pennington**, Guin, AL, electric department worker — returned to find his home erased: "There was no sign of our house, its foundation, framing or anything"; his wife Juanita, 64, was found dead. https://www.tornadotalk.com/guin-al-f5-tornado-april-3-1974/
- **Robert Debruler**, Kentucky witness — sheltered under a garbage truck during the outbreak: "The wind had a roar that sounded like a freight train & it was a sound that we haven't heard before." https://www.weather.gov/lmk/april31974_in_the_path
- **Gus Allen**, Brandenburg, KY — lost his sister, a 2-year-old cousin, and his brother in the tornado; his mother was carried roughly a mile by the wind and critically injured; recalling the day, he said simply, "It was just a bad day." [Secondary news source; full context of the quote is limited] https://www.wave3.com/2024/04/04/i-will-never-forget-what-i-saw-survivors-remember-1974-brandenburg-tornado-50-years-later/
- **Tim Haley**, Brandenburg, KY — 15 years old at the time, helped carry 169 dead and injured out of the debris, including removing a dead infant from a mother's arms "so that I could get her to safety," which he called "pretty, pretty traumatic." [Secondary news source] https://www.army.mil/article/275125/former_soldier_meade_county_community_remember_devastation_of_1974_tornado

## 4. The science and warning-system angle

- Radar coverage on 3 April 1974 was WSR-57 (Weather Surveillance Radar–1957) units — reflectivity-only, no velocity/Doppler capability; forecasters read hook echoes off scope traces drawn onto paper maps. WSO Cincinnati's WSR-57 had been installed in 1960. https://www.weather.gov/iln/19740403
- At the peak of the outbreak, Cincinnati's radar (CVG) displayed five distinct hook echoes simultaneously — more concurrent hooks than meteorologists there had previously observed. https://www.weather.gov/iln/19740403
- Satellite support came from ATS-3 (Applications Technology Satellite-3). https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/april-3-4-1974-super-outbreak
- NSSFC director Allen Pearson recognized outbreak potential on 2 April 1974 and called every NWS office in the eastern US to service radars, add staff, and prime satellites for severe-weather mode ahead of the event. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Pearson
- A total of 161 tornado warnings were issued during the outbreak, per secondary retrospective reporting; NWS Dayton issued a tornado warning at 4:10 PM EDT for Montgomery and Greene counties, Ohio (ahead of the Xenia F5), and WSO Cincinnati issued one at 4:45 PM EDT ahead of the Sayler Park tornado. [The 161-warning figure is a secondary-source aggregate, not sourced to a specific NWS/NCDC document in the pages reviewed] [unverified primary count] https://www.weather.gov/iln/19740403
- Warning lead time in 1974 averaged under one minute in some tellings; by contrast, present-day US tornado warning lead time averages roughly 8–15 minutes, with some long-track supercell events giving up to 30 minutes — these comparative figures come from secondary retrospective coverage, not a single NWS statistical report, and should be treated as [unverified] pending a primary NWS/NOAA lead-time study citation. https://www.livescience.com/28404-1974-tornado-super-outbreak-today.html
- After the outbreak, Ted Fujita conducted a roughly ten-month, 10,000-plus-mile aerial damage survey, the most extensive tornado survey conducted up to that time, applying his newly developed F-scale (introduced 1971) across the full outbreak — the first time the scale had been used at this scale of an event. https://www.chicagomag.com/city-life/april-2011/ted-fujita-chicagos-mr-tornado/
- During the survey, Fujita identified a class of straight-line wind damage inconsistent with tornadic rotation, leading to his later identification and naming of the microburst — a discovery that grew directly out of the 1974 survey work. https://www.climatecentral.org/news/innovation-in-tornado-technology-born-from-1974-tragedy-17258
- Fujita and Robert Abbey published the detailed technical study "Tornadoes: The Tornado Outbreak of 3–4 April 1974" in 1981, the primary scientific reference for path/rating data still cited by researchers (e.g., Tornado Talk, Grazulis). https://www.tornadotalk.com/guin-al-f5-tornado-april-3-1974/
- The outbreak is credited as a major driver of NWS modernization: improved coordination between forecast offices and emergency management/media, backup power at NWS offices, and the long-run push toward Doppler radar (culminating decades later in the NEXRAD/WSR-88D network). https://www.weather.gov/iln/19740403 ; https://www.ohiohistory.org (see Section 5 note) [general "led to NEXRAD" causal claim is a common secondary framing; no primary source reviewed gives an exact date linking this outbreak to the NEXRAD program's founding, so the causal link should be stated as contributing context, not a direct cause] [unverified direct causal chain]
- The original Fujita F-scale used at the time of the outbreak was replaced nationally by the Enhanced Fujita (EF) Scale in 2007. https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/april-3-4-1974-super-outbreak

## 6. Present-day return

- Xenia, OH population: approximately 25,725 in 2023, essentially flat to slightly down from 2010 (population fell 0.84% from 2010–2020 before modest recent growth); overall 2000–2023 growth was about 6.56% — Xenia has not seen dramatic population growth since 1974, unlike the Alabama and Kentucky ends of the corridor. https://www.neilsberg.com/insights/xenia-oh-population-by-year/
- Huntsville, AL: 2025 estimated population 249,102, the largest city in Alabama and its fastest-growing major city over the preceding 15 years; Madison County (which includes Tanner/Harvest/Hazel Green) grew to roughly 433,516 by 2025 — a corridor that was rural farmland in 1974 is now suburban Huntsville. https://www.huntsvilleal.gov/business/city-of-huntsville/facts-figures-about-huntsville/
- A 2024 assessment states that Huntsville would likely sustain catastrophic damage today from a long-track F5 similar to the Tanner tornadoes, given the corridor's growth, and that the northern suburbs of Louisville and southern suburbs of Lexington now sit across parts of the 1974 path and were separately hit by the 2 March 2012 outbreak — indicating repeated vulnerability of the same corridor. [Secondary synthesis source] https://yaleclimateconnections.org/2024/04/how-might-the-next-super-tornado-outbreak-play-out-in-tomorrows-world/
- One estimate states a repeat of the 1974 outbreak today would produce insurance losses from wind alone exceeding $3.5 billion, surpassing the $3.13 billion insured-loss total of the May 2–11, 2003 multi-state tornado sequence. [Secondary source, exact methodology/date of the estimate not confirmed] [unverified] https://yaleclimateconnections.org/2024/04/how-might-the-next-super-tornado-outbreak-play-out-in-tomorrows-world/
- Warning lead time then vs. now: 1974 lead times were frequently near zero or under a minute for some strikes (e.g., Brandenburg received roughly 15 minutes' notice, described elsewhere as better than average for the day); current national average tornado warning lead time is cited as roughly 8–15 minutes, with some events reaching 30 minutes for long-lived supercells. [Comparative figures drawn from secondary retrospective reporting, not a single NWS lead-time study] [unverified precise national average] https://www.livescience.com/28404-1974-tornado-super-outbreak-today.html ; https://www.weather.gov/lmk/april31974_in_the_path

## 7. Ten candidate retention spikes

1. Ted Fujita rated the Xenia tornado as high as F6 before concluding an F6 rating was "inconceivable" and settling on F5 — the scale's own inventor nearly broke his own scale. https://www.chicagomag.com/city-life/april-2011/ted-fujita-chicagos-mr-tornado/
2. In Guin, Alabama, a young man taped the tornado's approach and passage on audio: "I have the thing on tape" — one of the only surviving audio recordings of an F5 vortex passing directly overhead in 1974. https://www.tornadotalk.com/guin-al-f5-tornado-april-3-1974/
3. Xenia High School drama students rehearsing "The Boy Friend" moved to a hallway seconds before a school bus was thrown onto the exact stage they had just been standing on. https://cedars.cedarville.edu/2024/04/50-years-after-the-tornado-don-and-pat-dunstans-xenia-survival-story/
4. A 15-year-old, Tim Haley, personally carried 169 dead and injured people out of the wreckage in Brandenburg, Kentucky, including removing a dead infant from its mother's arms to get her to safety. https://www.army.mil/article/275125/former_soldier_meade_county_community_remember_devastation_of_1974_tornado
5. At the peak of the outbreak, as many as 15 separate tornadoes were on the ground across multiple states at the same moment. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Super_Outbreak
6. The Guin, Alabama F5 stayed on the ground for over two hours and traveled more than 100 miles — one of the longest-duration violent tornadoes ever recorded. https://www.tornadotalk.com/guin-al-f5-tornado-april-3-1974/
7. Two separate F5 tornadoes hit the same small town of Tanner, Alabama, just about an hour apart, striking rescuers who were still pulling victims from the first tornado's wreckage. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1974_Tanner_tornadoes
8. A radio helicopter traffic reporter, Dick Gilbert, tracked the Louisville tornado live on air and was later personally commended by President Nixon for saving lives with his real-time reports. https://www.lpm.org/news/2014-04-02/remembering-the-1974-louisville-tornado-40-years-later
9. NSSFC director Allen Pearson called every National Weather Service office in the eastern United States the day before the outbreak, telling them to service their radars and add staff — a forecast made almost 24 hours out that proved exactly right. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Pearson
10. Cincinnati's radar screen showed five distinct tornado hook echoes on the scope at once — more simultaneous rotation signatures than the forecasters on duty had ever seen. https://www.weather.gov/iln/19740403

### Summary of what could not be confirmed from a primary source
1. The exact death split between the two Tanner, Alabama F5 tornadoes (roughly 28 vs. ~16–27) is inconsistent across secondary retrospectives; the primary NWS Huntsville page for the event returned a 404 when fetched directly.
2. The nominal 1974-dollar damage total ($600 million) appears only in secondary sources; NCEI's own page gives only a 2024-inflation-adjusted figure ($5.3 billion), not the original nominal figure.
3. The national "161 tornado warnings issued" count and the "under one minute vs. 8–15 minutes" lead-time comparison both trace to secondary retrospective journalism, not a single cited NWS/NCDC statistical report.
4. Guin tornado timing details conflict by source: duration is given as either 2h05m or 2h32m, and the death toll as either 28 or 32 (Tornado Talk's revised count including two later hospital deaths).
5. Licence/reuse terms for the Ohio History Connection/Ohio Memory photo collection and for the "declassified CIA satellite imagery" cited by Tornado Talk were not independently confirmed and need direct rights clearance before use.
