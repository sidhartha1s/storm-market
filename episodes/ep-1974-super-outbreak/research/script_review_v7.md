## STAKES CURVE

Tension rises correctly for two thirds of the run: H (scale) → I (a man who knew) → A1 (six dead in
farmhouses, then 31 in a warned town, then a fifteen-year-old carrying 169 people) → A2 (34 dead, a city,
Nixon) → A3 (five hooks, the weather office itself goes dark, a city of 360,000 warned by a helicopter)
→ A4 (night, the same town twice, a tape of an F5 overhead, two named dead sons). A4-S2 is the emotional
peak and it is in the right place.

Three sags:

1. **I, at roughly 1:15.** "After it was over, the Weather Bureau found that most people didn't know it
   could forecast a tornado at all." A post-event finding dropped into the setup act pulls the clock
   backwards before the clock has started. Move it to A6, where the warning comparison lives.
2. **A5-S3, roughly 17:00.** The one-air-mass card is the flattest 120 seconds in the script: no name, no
   place, no clock, a 42-word recipe sentence, and two sentences that restate each other ("Every storm
   that formed in that column had the same engine" / "One machine made all of them"). This is the only
   stretch where a viewer has no person to hold on to.
3. **A6-S2, roughly 20:30.** The then/now columns are three balanced contrasts in a row, one of which
   (lead time) argues against the act, and the act's own thesis line is an aphorism. Stakes go sideways
   right where the dominant question should be closing.

A5-S4 (combed trees, the microburst) recovers well, and A6-S1/S1b (the 2011 EF5 on the rebuilt church,
16 → 4 with 2.5× the people) is the strongest present-day return the script has. The problem is that the
strongest A6 material sits before the weakest.

**Do the palette rules hold? No — the opener does not hold on any of the three rules.**

- **At most three of each per 20 minutes:** openers appear **seven** times (items 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10).
  Over the cap by four. Every other device is inside its ration: aside ×1, repeated subject ×1,
  repeated verb ×1, check-in ×0.
- **Never twice inside three minutes of narration:** broken twice. Items 1 and 2 are about fifteen seconds
  apart across the H → I seam. Items 4 and 5 are in **consecutive sentences** in the same segment, which
  is also the palette's flat ban on "any device used in two sentences in a row".
- **Never the same one in neighbouring chapters:** broken three times — H/I (items 1, 2), A4/A5
  (items 8, 9), A5/A6 (items 9, 10). The opener appears in six of the nine chapters, which is exactly the
  "a device that appears in every act is a pattern" failure the palette names.

**Fix shape:** keep at most three openers and spread them — one in A3 (drop the second), one in A4, one in
A6 — and delete the H, I and A5 openers, which all start sentences that stand fine without them.

**Banned-list sweep:** no "uh", no "um", no self-corrections ("about an hour, maybe a little less" pattern
absent), no doubled negatives for rhythm ("not before, not since" pattern absent), no trailer fragments
under six words. The shortest sentences are "He wrote down a six." (5 words, A3-S1) and "That's when the
second one came." (6 words, A4-S1) — both complete sentences, both earned, neither a fragment.

One near-miss worth naming: A3-S4's "…and that everybody says a tornado sounds like a train, and they're
right" is doing the work of a check-in ("right?") in narrator voice. It reads as the narrator nodding at
the viewer. It is not on the census as a "right?" but it should go for the same reason.

## FACT FLAGS

### Errors and contradictions (narration vs the fact sheets, or vs the script's own other lines)

1. **A4-S1 — Tanner 1 duration.** "stayed on the ground for about an hour and fifty two miles." The fact
   sheet prose (section 1, item 5) says "on the ground about 90 minutes, ~51–52 miles". Only the timeline
   table's derived 6:20–7:21 supports "about an hour", and the table is not authoritative. Worse, the whole
   rescuers beat hangs on it: with 90 minutes the first tornado was still on the ground at 7:30 PM, when
   the script has neighbours already out in the wreckage.
2. **A4-S2 — misattributed quote.** "Becky Todd's family were in their car when it rolled, and **she** told
   them to hold on to the seats." The fact sheet quote is "I told them that was it. **George** told us to
   grab the seats, to hold on." The instruction belongs to George, not Becky.
3. **A1-S3 → A2 — chronology at the act seam.** "It wasn't yet four o'clock, and about a hundred and
   seventy miles to the northeast, in Xenia, Ohio, a drama teacher was walking his students onto a stage."
   No zone is named, and the arithmetic fails either way. Brandenburg's tornado ran 3:30–4:22 PM Central
   (20:30–21:22 UTC); Xenia's ran 4:32–5:09 PM Eastern (20:32–21:09 UTC). The two F5s touched down two
   minutes apart and Xenia was struck **while Brandenburg's tornado was still on the ground** — the script
   presents Xenia as the next thing that happened after Brandenburg was over.
4. **A6-S2 — the lead-time contrast argues the wrong way.** "A good warning in 1974 was fifteen minutes and
   most places got less, and today a warning gives you eight to fifteen." As heard, 1974's fifteen beats
   today's eight-to-fifteen. The fact sheet's 1974 baseline is "near zero or under a minute for some
   strikes", with Brandenburg's fifteen minutes called better than average for the day. The line as written
   destroys the act's argument and the episode's answer.
5. **A6-S3 — contradicts the script's own reporting.** "Pearson knew a day early, and it bought nobody
   anything without a radio and a basement." A3-S2b already quoted the Cincinnati office's own report
   saying the warnings "saved many lives", A1-S2 credited Brandenburg's fifteen minutes, and A3-S3 had a
   President commending Gilbert for saving lives. "Bought nobody anything" is a harder claim than the
   episode earned and it undercuts three of its own beats.
6. **A2-S5 vs A3-S1 — the hinge states the opposite of its payoff.** "decided his own scale was too small"
   → "he wrote F6, then decided a six was a rating nobody could believe and took it back. Xenia stayed an
   F5." The scale was not too small; he concluded the opposite.
7. **A1-S2 — quote not in any source.** "He became a soldier later, and he said that was still the hardest
   thing he ever did." The sourced wording is "pretty, pretty traumatic" (army.mil). "The hardest thing he
   ever did" is an invented paraphrase of a living named person.
8. **A3-S3 — Louisville geography.** "an F4 came across the river into a city of three hundred and sixty
   thousand people." The addendum has the tornado touching down **in Louisville**, a quarter mile north of
   Standiford Field. Nothing in either sheet has it crossing the Ohio into the city.
9. **H — hooks are not tornadoes.** "Five tornadoes on one radar screen at once." The source says five
   distinct hook echoes on the Cincinnati scope. A3-S2 gets this right ("five hooks at once"), so the hook
   and the body disagree with each other as well as with the sheet.
10. **A2-S4 — superlative wider than the source.** "the afternoon was about to get worse than any radar
    operator alive had seen." The source says more concurrent hooks than the meteorologists **there** had
    previously observed.
11. **A2-S4 — distance conflicts with the episode's own register.** "fifty miles away in Cincinnati." The
    fact register in this same file says "Cincinnati ~40 mi from Xenia `[measure on the plate]`".
12. **A1-S1b — Hanover building count reads as exact and is low.** "Thirty of the thirty three buildings
    were damaged and two were destroyed." Sources give "more than 30 of roughly 33" (hanover.edu) and
    "32 of 33" (NWS Louisville). Say "all but three" or "more than thirty".
13. **A1-S2 — Brandenburg strike time.** "At 3:30 PM, Central time, in Brandenburg, Kentucky, they had
    fifteen minutes." 3:30 PM CDT is the tornado's touchdown at the start of a 34-mile track, not the
    moment it reached Brandenburg; the sheet says only "struck Brandenburg mid-afternoon". A1-S1's "an
    hour and a quarter later" inherits the same assumption.
14. **I — overstated bridge.** "for the next eight hours every one that followed found a town." False for
    148 tornadoes, and contradicted inside this script by the two Tanner F5s over 1974 farmland.
15. **A3-S4 — "In that same hour" depends on an unresolved zone.** Louisville is 5:37 PM EDT; Monticello is
    5:17 PM local. If Indiana was on EST that day (which the script's own `[verify]` suspects), Monticello
    is 6:17 PM EDT — the next hour, not the same one.
16. **A3-S2 — invented singular.** "the man on duty was looking at five hooks at once." No source names one
    operator; the CVG report describes an office. Say "the office".

## TOP TEN FIXES

**1. A6-S2 — the lead-time line currently says today is worse. Fix it or the episode's answer collapses.**
> "A good warning in 1974 was fifteen minutes and most places got less, and today a warning gives you eight to fifteen."

Replace with:
> "Brandenburg got fifteen minutes that day and most towns got nothing at all, and today the average warning runs eight to fifteen minutes everywhere."

**2. A4-S1 — Tanner 1 ran about ninety minutes, not about an hour, and the rescuers beat depends on it.**
> "a tornado formed near Moulton, Alabama, and stayed on the ground for about an hour and fifty two miles."

Replace with:
> "a tornado formed near Moulton, Alabama, and stayed on the ground for about an hour and a half and fifty two miles."

Then check the next sentence: with 90 minutes the first tornado lifts near 7:50 PM, so "by 7:30 PM the
neighbors were out in the wreckage" has to move back to "within the hour the neighbors were out in the
