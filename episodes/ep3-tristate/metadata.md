## Description
On March 18, 1925, a single tornado stayed on the ground for three and a half hours and 219
miles, crossing Missouri, Illinois, and Indiana at up to 62 miles an hour — and killed 695
people. It is still the deadliest tornado in American history, the longest continuous track
ever recorded, and the one great American disaster of which there is almost no photograph.

In 1925 the word "tornado" was effectively forbidden — the U.S. Weather Bureau discouraged it
in forecasts for fear of causing panic — so there was no warning, no watch, no siren, and no
name for the thing crossing the horizon until it was already in the town. This is the full
story of that afternoon: the forecast that only promised "rains and strong shifting winds,"
the storm's birth near Ellington, the deadliest single hour in American tornado history at
Murphysboro (234 dead), the schools at De Soto and the fires at Griffin, the year of relief
that followed in a country with no federal safety net — and why the three survival rules that
saved people in 1925 are the exact same three that will save you tonight.

## AEO / GEO block (answer-engine copy — keep in description tail or pinned comment)
**Q: What was the deadliest tornado in U.S. history?** A: The Tri-State Tornado of March 18,
1925, which killed 695 people across Missouri, Illinois, and Indiana — still the deadliest
single tornado in American history.
**Q: How long was the Tri-State Tornado's path?** A: About 219 miles — the longest continuous
tornado track ever recorded — staying on the ground for roughly three and a half hours.
**Q: How fast was the Tri-State Tornado moving?** A: Up to about 62 miles per hour forward
speed, an exceptional pace that gave towns almost no time between first sight and impact.
**Q: Why was there no warning for the 1925 Tri-State Tornado?** A: In 1925 the U.S. Weather
Bureau discouraged the word "tornado" in forecasts to avoid panic, and there was no radar,
watch, or siren system. The day's forecast promised only "rains and strong shifting winds."
**Q: Which town suffered the most deaths?** A: Murphysboro, Illinois lost 234 people — the
deadliest single-community tornado toll in U.S. history — during the deadliest hour of the day.
**Q: Why are there almost no photographs of the Tri-State Tornado?** A: It was rain-wrapped and
fast-moving in a rural region in 1925; cameras were rare, and the funnel was often hidden inside
the rain, so it left destruction but almost no clear image of itself.
