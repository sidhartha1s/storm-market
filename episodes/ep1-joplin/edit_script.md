## ACT 1 — A TOWN THAT STOPPED LISTENING (1:00–4:00)

### A1-S1 — narration — ~55s (~118 words)
> To understand what happened at five forty-one that evening, you have to understand what the
> sirens meant to Joplin. Because on paper, the system worked. Twenty-eight sirens covered the
> city, and they were loud, and they were tested, and people knew the sound. But the sirens had
> a habit. They cried for thunderstorms. They cried for hail. They cried, as one resident later
> told federal investigators, when all it meant was a little more water in the gutter. And every
> false alarm was a small withdrawal from a bank of trust that Joplin did not know it would need.
> On May twenty-second, that account came due.
Visuals: siren pole stills, small-town streets, radar-screen b-roll. Micro-loop: "that account
came due."

### A1-S2 — graphic — ~35s (VO continues, ~75 words) — REAL RADAR if agent delivered, else map
> At one thirty that afternoon, the Storm Prediction Center drew a tornado watch across the
> region. Watches are common in Missouri in May, and most of them pass without a scratch. But
> inside this one, two supercell thunderstorms were about to do something meteorologists dread.
> They were about to merge. And a merged storm does not just grow stronger. It grows wetter,
> and that single detail decided who would see this tornado coming, and who never could.
Visual: real KSGF radar frames or watch-box map graphic; text popup "TORNADO WATCH — 1:30 PM".
Open loop: the wet-storm detail pays off in Act 2.

### A1-S3 — narration — ~45s (~97 words)
> At five oh nine, the first tornado warning went out, and it was not for the storm that would
> kill Joplin. It was for a different cell, north of town, that would pass with little harm.
> Two minutes later the sirens sounded anyway, a three minute wail across the whole city. People
> looked up, and looked outside, and saw grey sky and rain, and went back to their dinners.
> Which, given everything the sirens had taught them, was a completely reasonable thing to do.
> It was also the worst possible rehearsal for what came next.
Visuals: dinner-table stock, window rain, siren pole. Hold 3s after "what came next" — music up
(dread-build starts here).

## ACT 2 — THE STORM THEY COULD NOT SEE (4:00–7:30)

### A2-S1 — narration — ~50s (~107 words)
> At five seventeen, the National Weather Service in Springfield issued a second warning, and
> this one was different. This one was for the merged supercell bearing down on Joplin itself,
> and it came seventeen minutes before the tornado touched the ground, and twenty-four minutes
> before it reached the heart of the city. Let that number sit for a moment. Twenty-four
> minutes. By every standard the weather service had, that is a good warning. That is time to
> get to a basement, time to leave a parking lot, time to gather your children. The meteorology
> did its job that evening. What failed was everything after.
Visuals: NWS office stock, warning-text graphic popup "TORNADO WARNING 5:17 PM — LEAD TIME 24
MIN". Micro-loop planted: "everything after."

### A2-S2 — narration — ~55s (~118 words)
> Here is where the wet storm matters. The Joplin supercell was what forecasters call rain
> wrapped, which means the tornado was buried inside curtains of falling water. A classic
> tornado announces itself. It hangs from the sky like a rope, and you can see it from miles
> away, and the sight of it sends you underground faster than any siren. Joplin's tornado
> offered no such courtesy. People stood on porches looking directly at it and saw only a grey
> wall of rain. And remember what the survey later found. The most common response to the first
> siren was to look for confirmation. Joplin looked, and the storm looked back, and it showed
> them nothing.
Visuals: rain-wall footage, storm wall clips, porch POV. This is the doomed-confirmation beat.

### A2-S3 — narration + graphic — ~45s (~97 words)
> Then, at five thirty-eight, the sirens sounded a second time, and this is the detail that
> broke the system. Joplin had no all clear signal. The same tone meant everything. So when the
> wail rose again, twenty-seven minutes after the first one had stopped, thousands of people
> made a perfectly logical mistake. They heard it as the end of the earlier threat, not the
> arrival of a new one. The second siren, the one that meant run, now, was heard by many as
> permission to relax.
Visual: waveform/siren graphic, two-tone timeline popup "5:11 SIREN ONE" / "5:38 SIREN TWO — 
SAME SOUND". Music: dread-build swells.

### A2-S4 — hold — 4s
Storm-wall clip sits. Ambience + music only.

## ACT 3 — THIRTY-EIGHT MINUTES (7:30–12:30) — the march

### A3-S1 — narration — ~40s (~86 words)
> At five thirty-four, on the western edge of the city, the funnel touched grass. It was small
> at first, the way they always are. Within four minutes it crossed Schifferdecker Avenue into
> the city limits, and somewhere over those neighborhoods it did something radar would only
> confirm after the fact. It became an E F five, the highest rating that exists, with winds
> beyond two hundred miles per hour. Faster than a Formula One car at full speed, and nearly a
> mile wide.
Visuals: touchdown-adjacent footage, street signs, dark wedge clips. Location-march begins —
lower-third location tags per beat ("SCHIFFERDECKER AVE — 5:38 PM").

### A3-S2 — narration — ~50s (~107 words) — ST. JOHN'S
> At five forty-one it reached Saint John's Regional Medical Center, nine stories of concrete
> and glass, with one hundred eighty-three patients inside. The tornado hit the building
> directly. It tore off the top two floors, blew out nearly every window, and pushed the entire
> hospital four inches off its foundation. Four inches does not sound like much, until you
> remember it is a hospital, and it weighs more than a naval ship. The main power failed, and
> then the backup generator was ripped out of the building itself, and five patients on
> ventilators died in the dark.
Visuals: St. John's damage stills (NOAA/FEMA), slow push. Lower-third "ST. JOHN'S MEDICAL
CENTER — 5:41 PM".

### A3-S3 — graphic (REAL RADAR) — ~40s (~86 words)
> And here is the fact that should stay with you. The radar signature that proves a tornado is
> destroying a city, the debris ball, a return not of rain but of houses, did not appear on the
> Springfield radar until five forty-three. Two minutes after the hospital. The radar sat
> fifty-five miles away, reading the storm thousands of feet above the ground. So even the
> meteorologists watching the screens learned the worst was happening after the worst had
> already happened.
Visual: REAL KSGF frames with debris ball annotated + couplet numbers popup ("188 KT COUPLET —
5:43 PM"). This is the episode's biggest net-info-gain beat.

### A3-S4 — narration — ~55s (~118 words) — RANGE LINE / HOME DEPOT
> The tornado crossed the city at a walking city's pace, six miles in twenty minutes, and near
> its widest it swallowed the Range Line Road retail corridor whole. At the Home Depot, the
> roof came off first, and that mattered, because the walls were made of tilt-up concrete
> panels, each one weighing one hundred thousand pounds, and tilt-up walls need their roof the
> way a card tower needs its top card. Sixty-three of the seventy-three panels came down. Where
> they fell inward, seven people died. In one corner of the store, the panels happened to fall
> outward instead, and the thirty people sheltering behind them lived.
Visuals: Home Depot aerial, structural diagram graphic (simple animated panel-fall), debris.
Lower-third "RANGE LINE ROAD — 5:45 PM".

### A3-S5 — narration — ~35s (~75 words) — PIZZA HUT
> A few blocks away, at a Pizza Hut, the manager on duty was a young father named Christopher
> Lucas. He put four employees and fifteen customers inside the walk-in cooler, and when the
> door would not stay closed against the pressure, he wrapped a bungee cord around his own arm
> and held it shut from the outside. Everyone inside that cooler survived. Christopher did not.
Visuals: cooler-door still (dark, symbolic), dim. Quiet beat. Music: pulls back to near-silence.

### A3-S7 — narration — ~30s (~64 words)
> Thirty-eight minutes after it touched down, the tornado lifted, east of a town called
> Diamond. Behind it lay a scar twenty-one miles long and a mile wide, cut through eight
> thousand buildings. Roughly a quarter to a third of Joplin was simply gone. And the cost,
> nearly three billion dollars then, four billion in today's money, made it the most expensive

Visuals: path-scar satellite/aerial, stat popups (ONE red rising counter reserved for Act 5).

### A3-S4b — narration — WALMART + the corridor
> Across the road, the Walmart came apart around two hundred people sheltering inside it, and
> three of them died there. Up and down Range Line, the pattern repeated in every direction.
> The big stores were engineered for straight winds of ninety miles per hour, which sounds
> sturdy, and is, right up until the sky brings double that. Nothing on that corridor was
> built for what arrived, because in fairness, almost nothing in America was.
Visuals: Walmart/Range Line aerials (noaa_aerial_rangeline_wide), corridor damage. Lower-third

### A3-S6b — narration — GREENBRIAR (before the hold)
> And on the south side of the path sat the Greenbriar Nursing Home, a single story of the
> most vulnerable people in Joplin. The building took the tornado's full width and was leveled
> to its slab. Twenty-one residents died there, more than at any other single building in the
> city. There was no basement, and there was no engineered shelter, and for people who cannot
> run, those two sentences decided everything.
Visuals: leveled-structure stills (FEMA), slow push, dim heavy. Music near-silent.

## ACT 4 — THE RECKONING (12:30–16:00)

### A4-S1 — narration — ~50s (~107 words)
> In the weeks that followed, the National Weather Service did something uncomfortable. It
> investigated itself. The assessment team went door to door among the survivors, and the
> answers they brought back changed American weather policy forever. Because the majority of
> people they surveyed admitted they did not take shelter when the first siren sounded. They
> waited. They checked a second source, they called a relative, they stepped outside to look at
> a storm that rain had made invisible. The warning system had delivered its message in time,
> and the message had bounced off a decade of false alarms.
Visuals: assessment-report cover, survey b-roll, door-to-door stock. Music: neutral-underscore.

### A4-S2 — narration — ~45s (~97 words)
> The investigators also found a mismatch hiding in plain sight. Weather service warnings are
> drawn as tight polygons around a storm's actual path. But Joplin's sirens fired city wide,
> all or nothing. So most residents had heard sirens, again and again, for storms that were
> never coming near their street. Every one of those blasts was technically correct, and every
> one of them taught the same quiet lesson. Sirens do not mean you. Until the evening when
> they did.
Visual: polygon-vs-city map graphic (self-made, animated). Popup "WARNING POLYGON vs SIREN ZONE".

### A4-S3 — narration — ~45s (~97 words)
> Out of one hundred sixty-one deaths came forty-seven findings and sixteen recommendations,
> and one change you have already heard, whether you know it or not. After Joplin, the weather
> service rewrote the language of fear itself. Warnings today are impact based. They no longer
> just name the storm, they name what it will do to you. Catastrophic. Mass devastation.
> Unsurvivable if you are above ground. That language was born in Joplin, paid for in Joplin,
> and it has been saving lives in every tornado warning since.
Visual: THE red rising counter 0 → 161 (owner-requested device) over dark, then IBW warning-text
graphic. Note: on-screen fine print "161 total; 158 directly attributed (NWS)" for integrity.

### A4-S3b — narration — the quiet fix nobody notices
> There was one more change, smaller and closer to home. Joplin rebuilt its siren policy from
> the ground up. Activations became zoned and specific, the criteria were tightened, and the
> practice of sounding the same tone for everything, everywhere, ended. It is the kind of fix
> that never makes the news, because its success is measured in sirens that do not sound. But
> the next time Joplin hears that wail, it will mean exactly one thing. And everyone will know
> what it is.
Visuals: modern siren still (siren-pole-sky, graded), city-hall/policy b-roll, quiet streets.

## ACT 5 — THE PEOPLE (16:00–19:00) — banked emotion

### A5-S1 — narration — ~40s (~86 words)
> The numbers are how a country remembers a disaster. But Joplin remembers people. It remembers
> fifty-three worshippers at Harmony Heights Baptist Church, who had no basement, and who
> folded into the interior hallways as the building came down over them. Fifty survived. Three
> women did not. One of them, Ramona Bridgeford, had come that evening for one reason. Her son
> was singing, and she wanted to hear him.
Visuals: flattened church photo (Baptist Press, verify license before render; else symbolic),

### A5-S2 — narration — ~35s (~75 words)
> It remembers the staff of Saint John's, nurses who covered patients with their own bodies as
> the windows came in, who squeezed air into failing lungs by hand down nine flights of dark
> stairs, and who emptied a nine story hospital in ninety minutes. Five patients were lost.
> One hundred and seventy-eight were carried out alive by people who refused to leave them.
Visuals: hospital evacuation photos (news/NIST), gentle push. Music: aftermath-somber.

### A5-S3 — narration — ~45s (~97 words) — WILL NORTON (real photo if license clears)
> And it remembers Will Norton, eighteen years old, four hours a high school graduate. He was
> driving home from the ceremony with his father when the tornado crossed the highway and took
> the car. His father survived. Will did not. He had already been accepted to film school in
> California, and his camera, and his easy laugh, had made him a small celebrity online long
> before the storm made him a name the whole country knew. He wanted to tell stories for a
> living. So the least we can do is tell his.
Visuals: real published Will Norton photo IF license/context clears morning review (news/memorial

## ACT 6 — CLOSE (19:00–20:30)

### A6-S1 — narration — ~40s (~86 words)
> Joplin was not a failure of forecasting. The watch was right, the warning was early, and the
> radar, for all its distance, found the storm in time. What failed was softer than science.
> It was the space between hearing a warning and believing it. Every siren you have ever
> ignored has made a small bet that this time is like last time. Joplin is what it looks like
> when that bet finally loses. And that is why the warnings changed, so the believing could
> come easier.
Visuals: rebuilt-Joplin footage (today), dawn tones. Music: hopeful-resolve enters.

### A6-S2 — narration — SIGNATURE OUTRO — ~20s (~43 words)
> Disasters do not ask permission. But they almost always send word ahead. So learn what your
> warning sounds like, and decide, on a quiet day, what you will do when it comes. That is the
> whole lesson. This was The Warning Hour.
Visual: title card "THE WARNING HOUR" over grain, warm grade, hard cut to black. No recap, no
subscribe beg (channel too young to burn trust; CTA experiments start ep 2).
