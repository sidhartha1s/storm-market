## B2 — WHERE THE NUMBERS STAND (0:42–1:55)

### B2-S1 — narration
> As of Tuesday, September sixteenth, the numbers come from the center's September tenth advisory, and they have not moved. The central Pacific index runs about one point eight degrees Celsius above average. Off the coast of South America the water runs three point four degrees above average. That is the warmest reading of any region the center tracks. The region between them runs two point five above. Out near the dateline the ocean barely moves, a tenth of a degree above normal. The heat is stacked in the east. The center puts the chance of a very strong event above ninety percent. It puts the chance of a historic one at three in four. Historic means a three month average of two and a half degrees or more this fall. That would pass every El Niño measured since nineteen fifty. The two strongest before this one were the winters of nineteen ninety seven and twenty fifteen. The advisory has stood since June. The next update comes on the eighth of October. [s1, s6, s3]

## B3 — WHERE IT SHOWS FIRST (1:55–3:35)

### B3-S1 — narration
> The clearest place to see it is the Atlantic, right at the peak of its own hurricane season. The National Hurricane Center's outlook this week shows no active storm anywhere in the Atlantic, the Caribbean, or the Gulf of Mexico. One disorganized area sits about a thousand miles east southeast of Bermuda, and conditions there could turn more favorable by midweek. For a basin at its busiest date of the year, the quiet is the story. NOAA's own blog puts Atlantic activity this season at less than ten percent of normal, and ties it to El Niño. The center gives that Bermuda system a forty percent chance of forming over the next week. The pattern works the other way in the Pacific. Hawaii has already taken two hurricanes this season, Lala in August and Lowell last week, and Kauai is still restoring power. Along the coast of South America the warmest water of the whole event sits offshore right now. That is where El Niño's effects have always arrived first, in the fishing grounds off Peru. For the United States the stake is the winter ahead. The center's seasonal maps lean milder across the north, from the Pacific Northwest to the Northeast. They lean wetter across the south, from Southern California through the Gulf Coast to Florida. [s4, s2]

## B5 — WHY IT KEEPS BUILDING (5:00–7:05)

### B5-S1 — narration
> The reason the surface numbers hold is below the surface. The World Meteorological Organization measured deep Pacific water running as much as eight degrees Celsius above average through the middle of summer. That reservoir sits under the surface and feeds warmth upward. That is why the readings stay very strong as the calendar turns toward winter. The heat surfaces first off South America and through the central basin, so those regions run hottest. It has not reached the far west near the dateline, so that region still reads close to normal. [s5, s1]

### B5-S2 — narration
> A second ocean is pushing the same way. The World Meteorological Organization also reports a positive pattern in the Indian Ocean this season, warmer in the west than in the east. Paired with El Niño it tends to reinforce the same shifts in wind and rain. Those shifts explain the week's other headlines. A strong El Niño raises wind shear over the tropical Atlantic, and shear tears storms apart before they can organize. That is the quiet Atlantic. On the far side of the Pacific the same shift dries out Indonesia during its dry season. None of this is new science. What is new is the timing. The heat below the surface and the Indian Ocean pattern are both still building as the Northern Hemisphere heads into fall. The last time the Pacific looked like this, in the winter of nineteen ninety seven, California took a season of floods and mudslides. Federal disaster photographs from that February show streets under water from the Bay Area to Los Angeles. The winter of twenty fifteen brought the same pattern, and the same pictures. [s5, s4, p1]
