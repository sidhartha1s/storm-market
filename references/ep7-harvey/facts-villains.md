## 1. THE DAM DECISION (Addicks & Barker dams)

- **Authorization & purpose**: Congress authorized the Buffalo Bayou and Tributaries, Texas Project (which includes Addicks & Barker) in 1938 — Pub. L. No. 75-685, 52 Stat. 802, 804 — after major flood damage/loss of life in Houston in 1929 and 1935. Purpose: reduce downstream flood risk to protect downtown Houston. — source: Federal Circuit opinion, *Ablan v. United States*, No. 2023-1363 (Fed. Cir. Dec. 22, 2025), slip op. at 5, citing *Liability Decision* at 229-30 — confidence: HIGH
- **Construction date — minor discrepancy, flag it**: the Federal Circuit opinion states "The Corps completed the dams in 1948" (slip op. at 5); Houston Chronicle (citing Corps history) states they were "constructed during World War II between 1942 and 1945." Both are consistent with phased construction/completion — use "built in the 1940s, WWII-era" rather than picking one exact year on air. — source: *Ablan v. U.S.* slip op. at 5; Houston Chronicle, "Judge backs Houston homeowners who sued feds..." (Apr. 23, 2026) https://www.houstonchronicle.com/news/houston-texas/environment/article/addicks-barker-downstream-decision-22222155.php — confidence: HIGH (both facts individually), MED on which single year to use on air
- **Design**: earthen dams forming reservoirs on Buffalo Bayou tributaries west of Houston; reservoirs are normally DRY and fill with water only during storms, to reduce/prevent downstream flooding of central Houston. — source: *Ablan v. U.S.* slip op. at 5 — confidence: HIGH
- **The 1940s design trade-off, verbatim from the court record**: during planning, the Corps considered buying all upstream land that would be inundated by "the design storm rainfall of 31.4 inches," but "considered it 'unnecessary to acquire lands to the pool elevation which would be produced by the design storm.'" Instead the Corps bought only the lower-lying land, reasoning "the savings in annual interest would be in excess of the probable damages" from the design storm or larger storms. The Corps itself recognized this decision "w[ould] eventually place the [government] in the position of having to flood the area within the reservoir with the accompanying damages in order to protect downstream improvements in the event of a severe future storm." — source: *Ablan v. U.S.* slip op. at 5-6, quoting *Liability Decision*, 146 Fed. Cl. 219 (2019), at 231-34, and J.A. 8864 — confidence: HIGH (verbatim court quotation of the administrative record)
- **Pre-Harvey risk rating**: in September 2009, USACE engineers raised the Dam Safety Action Classification (DSAC) for Addicks & Barker from Level II to Level I. Verbatim from a mid-August 2017 USACE report (obtained by the Texas Tribune via records request): *"Addicks and Barker Dams were designated (in 2009) as extremely high risk and classified as Dam Safety Action Classification I dams."* DSAC I "signifies that authorities should 'take immediate action to avoid failure.'" Also verbatim: *"The combination of life and economic consequences with likelihood of failure is very high."* A $74 million gate-replacement project began in 2015 and was still incomplete as of the Corps' mid-August 2017 report. — source: The Weather Channel, "Documents Show Army Corps Worried About Houston Dams in 2009, 8 Years Before Harvey Hit" (Sept. 29, 2017), quoting the USACE report obtained by the Texas Tribune https://weather.com/storms/hurricane/news/houston-dams-harvey-addicks-barker ; underlying Texas Tribune reporting https://www.texastribune.org/2017/09/28/could-houstons-dams-have-failed-during-harvey/ — confidence: HIGH
- **Pool levels during Harvey**: the flood pools reached record levels of **101.6 feet in the Barker Dam reservoir** and **109.1 feet in the Addicks Dam reservoir**. The 4-day average rainfall in the Addicks and Barker watersheds was approximately **31 inches** — almost exactly the 31.4-inch "design storm" the 1940s Corps had explicitly planned around and chosen not to fully buy out. — source: *Ablan v. U.S.* slip op. at 6, citing *Liability Decision* at 240-41 and J.A. 1045-46 ¶¶110-15, J.A. 9740, J.A. 9746 — confidence: HIGH
- **The controlled-release decision — date/time, VERIFIED AS APPROXIMATE, "1:40 AM" NOT CONFIRMED**: no primary source (court opinion, USACE record, or contemporaneous reporting) found in this research pass uses the specific figure "1:40 AM, August 28, 2017." The verifiable record instead shows: the Corps' original operational plan was to begin releases from Addicks around 2:00 AM and from Barker around 11:00 AM on August 28; because both reservoirs were "rising more than half a foot per hour" (per Col. Lars Zetterstrom, USACE Galveston District commander), the Corps and Harris County Flood Control District accelerated the timeline and controlled releases actually began sometime **between roughly midnight and 2:00 AM on August 28, 2017**. Recommend the episode say "in the middle of the night on August 28" rather than a specific minute. — source: zehllaw.com, "History and Timeline of Addicks and Barker Reservoir Releases" https://www.zehllaw.com/practice-areas/disputes/insurance-disputes/houston-addicks-barker-reservoir-release-damage-lawyers-flooding-timeline-before-and-after-hurricane-harvey/ ; corroborated by Houston Chronicle's later reporting that "Two days later [after Aug. 25 landfall], the Army Corps said it would open the reservoir gates. The next morning it did" (i.e., the morning of Aug. 28) https://www.houstonchronicle.com/news/houston-texas/environment/article/addicks-barker-downstream-decision-22222155.php — confidence: MED-HIGH on the ~midnight-2AM window; the "1:40 AM" figure specifically is UNVERIFIED — treat as unconfirmed/likely inaccurate until a primary USACE timestamp is found
- **Who decided / stated alternative**: the release decision was made by USACE's Galveston District in coordination with the Harris County Flood Control District. The government's later legal defense (rejected in the 2026 downstream ruling, see Section 2) was that releases were necessary to protect the "structural integrity" of the dams from an "imminent danger" of failure; Judge Loren A. Smith found in 2026 that operators opened the gates "before an actual emergency posed an imminent danger to the structural integrity of Addicks and Barker," citing an engineering report showing no significant structural threat at that time. — source: Houston Chronicle (as above); Houston Public Media, "Judge rules federal operator caused excessive damage to downstream homeowners after Hurricane Harvey" (Apr. 23, 2026) https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/harris-county/2026/04/23/549912/ — confidence: HIGH (Chronicle direct quotation of the 2026 opinion)
- **Upstream flooding (reservoir pool rising onto private land) — home count, MED confidence, no single official court/agency count found**: legal-advocacy and news estimates place roughly **10,000 upstream homes/structures** flooded when the swollen reservoirs spread beyond government-owned land onto private property during Harvey; a pre-Harvey (2016) ProPublica/Texas Tribune analysis had already found **approximately 14,000 homes located inside the reservoirs' flood pools**, of which more than one-third are reported to have flooded during Harvey. No USACE or Court of Federal Claims document in this research pass gives one authoritative upstream home count — the *Ablan* Federal Circuit/Court of Federal Claims record establishes LIABILITY via a small set of bellwether properties (13 for liability, 6 for damages — see Section 2), not a total count. — source: aggregate of legal/advocacy reporting (see Section 2 case-tracking sites) and ProPublica/Texas Tribune, "Boomtown, Flood Town" (Dec. 2016) https://www.propublica.org/article/boomtown-flood-town-text — confidence: MED
- **Downstream flooding (Buffalo Bayou overflow from the controlled releases) — home count, MED confidence**: reporting describes "thousands of homes and businesses" in the Buffalo Bayou watershed downstream of the dams — including the Fleetwood, Thornwood, Westchester, Nottingham Forest, and Energy Corridor neighborhoods — receiving floodwater specifically from the release, "flooding thousands of homes downstream that had never flooded before" from rainfall alone. No single verified official total was found distinguishing "flooded by releases specifically" from "flooded by rainfall" at the individual-home level; **at least two people died in their homes after the gates were opened**, per Houston Chronicle reporting on the 2026 ruling. A commonly repeated figure of "25,010 houses flooded in the entire Buffalo Bayou watershed" appears in secondary aggregation but its primary sourcing could not be independently pinned down in this pass — treat as UNVERIFIED/LOW confidence if used. — source: Houston Chronicle (Apr. 23, 2026, as above); zehllaw.com timeline (as above) — confidence: MED (thousands of homes, 2 deaths); LOW (25,010 combined figure — UNVERIFIED)
- **Mechanism distinction — do not conflate the two figures**: upstream flooding is water backing up onto privately-owned land AS the reservoir pools filled (a taking of a "flowage easement" over land the government chose not to buy in the 1940s). Downstream flooding is Buffalo Bayou overflowing its banks BECAUSE of the volume of water the Corps deliberately released from the reservoirs starting around Aug. 28. These are two different plaintiff classes, two different lawsuits, and two different legal outcomes (Section 2) — never present one home-count figure as if it covers both.

## 2. THE COURT RULINGS — precision-critical section

### Upstream case
- **Case & docket**: *In re Upstream Addicks & Barker (Texas) Flood-Control Reservoirs*, U.S. Court of Federal Claims, Master Docket No. 1:17-cv-9001, Upstream Sub-Master Docket. Presiding: Senior Judge **Charles F. Lettow**. — source: *Ablan v. United States*, Fed. Cir. No. 2023-1363 (consolidated with 2023-1365, 2023-1366, 2023-1412), decided Dec. 22, 2025, cover page — confidence: HIGH (direct PDF read of the Federal Circuit opinion, https://www.cafc.uscourts.gov/opinions-orders/23-1363.OPINION.12-22-2025_2622469.pdf)
- **Liability ruling — Dec. 17, 2019**: after a 10-day bellwether trial (13 representative upstream properties), the Court of Federal Claims held the government liable for a **Fifth Amendment taking of a flowage easement on all 13 properties**. *In re Upstream Addicks & Barker (Tex.) Flood-Control Reservoirs*, 146 Fed. Cl. 219, 264 (2019) ("Liability Decision"). The court held plaintiffs possessed a cognizable property interest, that the government took flowage easements on those properties, and that the government's police-power and necessity defenses did NOT absolve it of liability. — source: *Ablan v. U.S.* slip op. at 7, citing *Liability Decision* at 228, 248-49, 249-63, 263-64 — confidence: HIGH
- **Liability ≠ damages, in 2019**: the 2019 Liability Decision did NOT set any dollar damages. Class certification for liability purposes was later denied (2021) as untimely. Damages were determined only afterward, in a SEPARATE 2022 proceeding covering just 6 of the 13 bellwether properties: the court awarded amounts ranging from **$1,401.49 to $195,549.86 per property**, for a **combined total of $454,535.03 plus interest from the date of taking**. — source: *In re Upstream Addicks & Barker (Tex.) Flood-Control Reservoirs*, 157 Fed. Cl. 189, 193, 196-97 (2021) ("Class Certification Decision"); 162 Fed. Cl. 495, 502, 534 (2022) ("Damages Decision"); both as cited in *Ablan v. U.S.* slip op. at 7-8 — confidence: HIGH
- **2025 Federal Circuit appeal — current status**: on Dec. 22, 2025, the Federal Circuit (Circuit Judges Reyna, Taranto, Cunningham; opinion by Cunningham) **AFFIRMED the liability decision and the denial of class certification**. On damages, it affirmed the awards for leasehold advantage, damaged personal property, and the offset for FEMA emergency relief already received — but **VACATED and remanded** the awards for lost rent, displacement costs, and the valuation of one plaintiff's (Ms. Popovici's) flowage easement, holding those categories were improperly awarded as "consequential damages" (lost rental income, business profits, and temporary relocation costs are not compensable — the flowage-easement payment already compensates for the government's occupation of the property; those are indirect losses). The court reasoned the flooding is a **permanent taking** because Harvey-scale storms "would inevitably recur without identifiable end." — source: *Ablan v. United States*, Fed. Cir. No. 2023-1363 (Dec. 22, 2025), slip op. pp. 1, 4-8 (direct PDF text extraction) https://www.cafc.uscourts.gov/opinions-orders/23-1363.OPINION.12-22-2025_2622469.pdf — confidence: HIGH (verbatim reading of the opinion)
- **What's next / potential Supreme Court review**: as of this research, the Department of Justice had not yet decided whether to seek Supreme Court review; one legal-tracking source cites a certiorari-petition deadline of July 8, 2026. On remand, the Court of Federal Claims must resolve the vacated damages issues before lifting a litigation stay on the remaining (non-bellwether) upstream claims. Total potential liability across ALL upstream claimants has been informally estimated by reporting at "well over $1 billion," but this is NOT a court-set figure. — source: insideaddicksbarker.com, "Federal Circuit Affirms Government Liability for Upstream Hurricane Harvey Flooding" https://insideaddicksbarker.com/federal-circuit-affirms-government-liability-for-upstream-hurricane-harvey-flooding/ (cert deadline, MED confidence — single source, not independently corroborated); Houston Public Media (Nov. 2022 test-case coverage, referenced via search; $1B+ estimate) — confidence: MED

### Downstream case
- **Case & docket**: *In re Downstream Addicks and Barker (Texas) Flood-Control Reservoirs*, Court of Federal Claims, No. 1:17-cv-09002; appeal styled *Milton v. United States*, 36 F.4th 1154 (Fed. Cir. 2022). — source: cited within *Ablan v. U.S.* slip op. at 7 (referencing the Master Docket split into Upstream/Downstream Sub-Master Dockets) — confidence: HIGH
- **First dismissal — Feb. 19, 2020**: Judge **Loren A. Smith** dismissed the downstream claims outright. Verbatim: *"The damage was caused by Hurricane Harvey, and such a hurricane is an Act of God, which the government neither caused nor committed."* And: *"Of course, the water from the hurricane was not the government's water, unless the storm was also created by the government's wind and air and sun and sky... These were flood waters that no entity could entirely control."* His reasoning: the gates had been closed for the SOLE purpose of protecting those very downstream properties; that protective effort failed only because the impounded stormwater exceeded the reservoirs' controllable capacity; therefore Harvey itself — not the government's operation — was the sole and proximate cause of the downstream flooding. This is the "combined-cause"/causation-standard dismissal referenced in the research brief: downstream plaintiffs could not, in the court's 2020 view, separate "flooding from the storm" from "flooding from the release" as a matter of proximate cause. — source: Houston Chronicle, "Judge backs Houston homeowners..." (Apr. 23, 2026), quoting the 2020 opinion directly https://www.houstonchronicle.com/news/houston-texas/environment/article/addicks-barker-downstream-decision-22222155.php ; Texas Tribune, "Federal judge dismisses against Army Corps for post-Harvey flooding" (Feb. 19, 2020) https://www.texastribune.org/2020/02/19/federal-judge-dismisses-against-army-corps-post-harvey-flooding/ — confidence: HIGH
- **2022 Federal Circuit reversal**: the Federal Circuit reversed the 2020 dismissal (*Milton v. United States*, 36 F.4th 1154 (Fed. Cir. 2022)), holding the lower court erred in concluding plaintiffs failed to assert a cognizable property interest, rejecting the "Act of God" bar to a takings claim, and remanding for the trial court to address (1) whether a temporary taking occurred, (2) causation given the government's own actions, and (3) whether the "necessity doctrine" applies as a defense. — source: reduceflooding.com, "Appeals Court Revives Addicks-Barker Downstream Takings Cases" (June 2022) https://reduceflooding.com/2022/06/02/appeals-court-revives-addicks-barker-downstream-takings-cases/ — confidence: HIGH
- **2024-2026 test-case trial and NEW ruling — the most important recent update**: on remand, the Court of Federal Claims tried a bellwether/test case of **12 plaintiffs (11 homeowners + 1 business owner)** — testimony taken through Oct. 31, 2024 (including the Corps' former Galveston District commander and its district chief of engineering), closing arguments March 6, 2025. On **April 22, 2026**, Judge Loren A. Smith ruled **for the downstream plaintiffs**, reversing his own 2020 "Act of God" position: the government's actions in opening the gates amounted to a compensable **"taking" of private property**, because the plaintiffs proved they flooded MORE than they would have if the Corps had kept the gates shut, and the government's necessity/imminent-danger defense failed — operators opened the gates "before an actual emergency posed an imminent danger to the structural integrity of Addicks and Barker." **Damages for these 12 plaintiffs have NOT yet been determined** — that phase is still ahead. Plaintiffs' attorney Richard Mithoff (who alone represents 502 downstream clients) said there could be "many thousands" of eventual claims. — source: Houston Chronicle, "Judge backs Houston homeowners who sued feds..." (Apr. 23, 2026) https://www.houstonchronicle.com/news/houston-texas/environment/article/addicks-barker-downstream-decision-22222155.php ; Houston Public Media (Apr. 23, 2026) https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/harris-county/2026/04/23/549912/ — confidence: HIGH
- **STATUS FLAG for the script**: as of this research (Sept. 2026), the April 2026 downstream ruling is a Court of Federal Claims TRIAL-LEVEL decision on a 12-plaintiff test case — it is NOT yet a Federal Circuit appellate ruling, and the government may still appeal it, exactly as it did the 2019 upstream Liability Decision (which took until Dec. 2025 — six years — to be affirmed on appeal). Do not present the downstream outcome as being on the same final footing as the now Federal-Circuit-affirmed upstream liability finding.

### The hook line precision check — how far "a federal court ruled... it named the United States government" can go
- **What is fully court-confirmed and appellate-affirmed (safe to state as settled)**: the U.S. government is liable, under the Fifth Amendment's takings clause, for flooding upstream properties as a designed consequence of how it built and operated the Addicks and Barker dams to protect downtown Houston. This was affirmed by the Federal Circuit on Dec. 22, 2025, after the government's own appeal. The court's own language: the Corps "knew or should have known since the dams' construction that severe storms would cause the reservoirs to overflow and flood private properties," and chose in the 1940s not to buy all the land that its own 31.4-inch "design storm" study said would flood.
- **What is recent and directionally supportive but NOT yet final (state with a caveat)**: as of April 2026, a trial judge ALSO found the government liable to a downstream test-case cohort specifically for the additional flooding caused by the Aug. 28, 2017 gate-opening decision (beyond what the storm alone would have caused) — but this is a first-instance ruling that took the opposite result (dismissal, "Act of God") for two years (2020-2022) before being overturned on appeal, and it may be appealed again; damages have not been set.
- **What the rulings do NOT say, and the episode must not claim**: neither ruling holds that "the government caused Hurricane Harvey's flooding" broadly, or that the storm's record 60+ inches of rainfall is somehow the government's fault. The takings claims are narrowly about the INCREMENTAL, ADDITIONAL flooding caused by a specific 1940s design trade-off (upstream) and a specific 2017 operational decision (downstream) — not the hurricane's total damage. The most precise, defensible hook: *"A federal court ruled that when the Army Corps of Engineers built dams to protect downtown Houston, it designed them to flood other people's homes on purpose — and in 2025, an appeals court agreed: that's not disaster, that's a taking, and the government owes for it."* Do NOT say "a court ruled the government flooded Houston" as a blanket statement — narrow it explicitly to the upstream/downstream trade-off mechanism described in Section 1.

## 3. THE MAPS/DEVELOPMENT (REPORTING — journalism explicitly allowed here)

- **REPORTING: the core ProPublica/Texas Tribune finding**: the investigative series "Boomtown, Flood Town" (ProPublica + Texas Tribune, published Dec. 2016, BEFORE Harvey) established that Houston-area development had outpaced both drainage infrastructure and flood-risk understanding, and specifically predicted a Harvey-like disaster. — source: ProPublica/Texas Tribune, "Boomtown, Flood Town" full text https://www.propublica.org/article/boomtown-flood-town-text ; interactive graphics https://projects.propublica.org/houston-cypress/ — confidence: HIGH (this is the reporting itself, correctly labeled as reporting)
- **REPORTING: subdivisions inside/near the reservoir flood pools**: "Boomtown, Flood Town" documents **Bear Creek Village**, just south of a resident (Virginia Hammond)'s subdivision on "the northern edge of the reservoir" — flooded twice during the April 2016 Tax Day storm (once from rainfall overwhelming drainage, a second time when the Corps closed the Addicks dam gates and the reservoir backed up into neighborhood streets). Also documented: **Stable Gate** (Cypress area), built 2001, ~250 homes — was NOT in any FEMA floodplain when built, but new FEMA maps effective 2007 placed a large part of it in the 100-year floodplain (parts also fell into the 500-year floodplain); it first flooded in the 2016 Tax Day storm. — source: ProPublica/Texas Tribune, "Boomtown, Flood Town" (as above) — confidence: HIGH (as reporting)
- **REPORTING: were buyers told?** The article quotes homebuilder Frank Espinoza, who built 12 homes in the 100-year floodplain along Little Cypress Creek (3 of which flooded on Tax Day 2016): "even though he doesn't have to tell buyers, he said he will to avoid lawsuits" — implying there was no legal disclosure requirement. The article presents no evidence that flood risk was systematically disclosed to buyers in these areas. — source: ProPublica/Texas Tribune, "Boomtown, Flood Town" — confidence: HIGH (as reporting)
- **REPORTING: FEMA "low risk" designations that didn't hold**: a Houston-area homeowner (Louise Hansen, Memorial City) flooded three times (2009, 2015, 2016) despite living in an area "not in any floodplain identified by the Federal Emergency Management Agency." The article states "almost half of the buildings that flooded" during the 2001 Tropical Storm Allison "were outside floodplains designated by FEMA" — an early, pre-Harvey demonstration that FEMA's maps were systematically underestimating real flood exposure in Harris County. A 2018 Harris County analysis (post-Harvey) found **three-quarters of local homes that flooded during Harvey were outside the 100-year floodplain**, with about half of those outside ANY mapped flood zone. — source: ProPublica/Texas Tribune, "Boomtown, Flood Town" (Allison figure); Houston Chronicle / Kinder Institute coverage of the 2018 Harris County post-Harvey analysis, referenced via WebSearch aggregation (exact URL not independently re-fetched in this pass — RECOMMEND re-verifying the "three-quarters" figure against the Harris County or Kinder Institute source directly before quoting on air) — confidence: HIGH (Allison figure, direct from the article); MED (three-quarters Harvey figure — needs one more direct-source check)
- **REPORTING: who knew, and the Corps' own acknowledgment**: Richard K. Long, the USACE official who oversees day-to-day Addicks/Barker operations, told ProPublica/Texas Tribune: *"We're between a rock and a hard place,"* and acknowledged: *"In an ideal world, neighborhoods like [Virginia] Hammond's wouldn't exist."* He also said: *"Today if we were starting over again knowing what has happened, would our battle lines be a little different? Yeah, our battle lines would definitely be different."* Long said the Corps "has little to no control over development" and that its hands were tied by local politicians/zoning authority (Harris County has no zoning). Separately, a **2015 Harris County Flood Control District report** itself found that "the restoration of one acre of prairie" would offset the extra stormwater runoff created by two acres of new single-family homes or one acre of commercial development — i.e., HCFCD's own research quantified the flood-risk cost of the very development it was simultaneously permitting — but HCFCD leadership (then-director Mike Talbott, later Russ Poppe) publicly disputed that finding and maintained that regulations in place since the mid-1980s prevented any net increase in flooding from new development. — source: ProPublica/Texas Tribune, "Boomtown, Flood Town" — confidence: HIGH (as reporting; direct quotes)

## 4. THE PAVEMENT

- **REPORTING/context (pre-Harvey data, cited by ProPublica from Texas A&M research)**: the amount of **impervious surface in Harris County increased 25 percent from 1996 to 2011**. Research by Texas A&M scientist John Jacob found **almost 30 percent of Harris County's freshwater wetlands were lost between 1992 and 2010**. In the decade after the 2001 Tropical Storm Allison, roughly **167,000 acres were developed in Harris County (2001-2010)**. The Cypress Creek watershed (immediately upstream of Addicks) grew nearly 70% in population 2000-2010 (to 587,142), with developed land share jumping from 23% to 33% over the same decade. Since 2010, more than 7,000 new residential buildings and 1,600+ nonresidential buildings were built in areas Harris County itself has flagged as flood-risky. — source: ProPublica/Texas Tribune, "Boomtown, Flood Town" (citing Texas A&M/John Jacob research and Harris County data) https://www.propublica.org/article/boomtown-flood-town-text — confidence: MED-HIGH (figures are reported by ProPublica citing named academic/county sources, but this research pass did not independently re-verify the underlying Texas A&M study directly — RECOMMEND a follow-up pass to the primary John Jacob/Texas A&M publication if an on-air citation needs a non-journalism source)
- **Prairie loss (Katy Prairie)**: "The Katy Prairie northwest of Houston was once about 600,000 acres of flood-absorbing land; recent development has reduced it to a quarter of that capacity" (i.e., roughly 150,000 acres remaining functional flood-absorbing prairie, as of the article's 2016 reporting). — source: ProPublica/Texas Tribune, "Boomtown, Flood Town" — confidence: MED (REPORTING-sourced figure, no separate primary Katy Prairie Conservancy/USGS acreage study independently checked in this pass)
- **HCFCD's own design-storm framework (RECORD, primary)**: Harris County Flood Control District's own pre-Harvey public "media guide" states its major bayou-widening/channel capital projects (e.g., Brays Bayou, Sims Bayou, done jointly with USACE) are built to remove the **1-percent-annual-chance (100-year) floodplain** designation from the areas they protect — i.e., HCFCD's stated capital-improvement design target for its major bayous is the 100-year storm, not a lower-frequency storm. The same document notes the 2001 Tropical Storm Allison (28+ inches of rain in 12 hours, 35 inches over 5 days) "grossly exceeded the 1 percent (100-year) flood" standard, flooded 73,000 residences, and caused $5+ billion in damage — becoming, at the time, the costliest tropical storm in U.S. history. — source: Harris County Flood Control District, "Media Guide" (pre-Harvey edition, hosted at reduceflooding.com) https://reduceflooding.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/HCFCDmediaGuide2017-small.pdf (direct PDF text extraction) — confidence: HIGH (primary HCFCD document, though note: this specific guide predates Harvey — it documents the STANDARD, not a post-Harvey comparison)
- **What Harvey delivered vs. that standard**: per the official NWS Service Assessment (see Section 8), Harvey generated a 4-day average of ~31 inches in the Addicks/Barker watersheds and up to 60+ inches at the single-storm U.S. record-setting gauges near Beaumont — several multiples beyond a 100-year (1%-annual-chance) event for the region, and "nearly 57 percent of the Harris County Flood Control District gages measur[ed] record flooding" during the storm. — source: NWS Service Assessment, Hurricane Harvey (June 2018), p.7 — confidence: HIGH (see Section 8 for full citation)

## 5. STAY-PUT CALL

- **Mayor Sylvester Turner — exact quotes, Aug. 25, 2017**: at a 4:00 PM press conference on Friday, Aug. 25, 2017 (the day of Harvey's landfall near Rockport), Turner stressed Houston was NOT under a mandatory evacuation order. Verbatim: *"You literally cannot put 6.5 million people on the road."* And: *"If you think the situation right now is bad, you give an order to evacuate, you are creating a nightmare."* Turner also tweeted shortly after: *"Please think twice before trying to leave Houston en masse. No evacuation orders have been issued for the city."* Harris County Judge Ed Emmett, at the same joint press conference, said: *"At this time I can reemphasize there will be no mass evacuations called."* — source: Texas Tribune, "Ahead of Hurricane Harvey, officials send Texans mixed messages on evacuations" (Aug. 25, 2017) https://www.texastribune.org/2017/08/25/officials-send-texans-mixed-messages-hurricane-evacuations/ ; corroborated by CNN, "Why didn't Houston evacuate?" (Aug. 27, 2017) https://edition.cnn.com/2017/08/27/us/houston-evacuation-hurricane-harvey/index.html and NBC News https://www.nbcnews.com/storyline/hurricane-harvey/houston-officials-defend-not-ordering-hurricane-harvey-evacuation-n796561 — confidence: HIGH
- **Governor Greg Abbott — contrasting statement, same window**: Abbott said residents "between Corpus Christi and Houston" should "strongly consider" evacuating "before it's too late," even without a local mandatory order. Verbatim: *"Even if an evacuation order hasn't been issued by your local official, if you're in an area between Corpus Christi and Houston, you need to strongly consider evacuating. What you don't know, and what nobody else knows right now, is the magnitude of flooding that will be coming."* — source: Houston Chronicle/Chron.com, "Abbott: Hurricane Harvey threat is real, urges evacuations" https://www.chron.com/politics/texas/article/Hurricane-Harvey-threat-is-real-says-Abbott-11959332.php — confidence: HIGH
- **Hurricane Rita (2005) evacuation death toll — VERIFIED via peer-reviewed public-health study**: a study published in *CHEST* (October 2006), based on a systematic review of county medical examiner records, funeral homes, and news reports over a 7-day window spanning 3 days before/after landfall, found **111 total Rita-related deaths in Texas**: 3 were direct storm deaths (wind-blown trees); the large majority — reported as **90 deaths (83.3% of a 108-death evacuation-linked subset)** — were evacuation-related. ## 8. NET-INFO-GAIN FACTS (4 strong candidates found)

1. **★ BEST: "Nearly 57 percent of Harris County Flood Control District gages measured record flooding."** This is a specific, official, NWS-sourced statistic that quantifies just how unprecedented Harvey was at the LOCAL infrastructure-monitoring level — not just "record rainfall" in the abstract, but a majority of the county's own flood-gage network reading numbers it had never recorded before. 2. **★ STRONG: the exact U.S. rainfall records, by name and number.** Harvey set the all-time U.S. tropical-cyclone rainfall record: **60.58 inches at Nederland, TX**, and **60.54 inches near Groves, TX** (both Jefferson County, near Beaumont) — the highest totals recorded in the U.S. since reliable precipitation record-keeping began in the 1880s. Most competitor videos cite "60+ inches" generically; naming Nederland and Groves specifically, with the exact hundredths-of-an-inch figures, is a level of precision most retrospectives skip. — source: NWS Service Assessment, Hurricane Harvey (June 2018), p. 6 — confidence: HIGH
3. **STRONG: the NWS's own river-gage scorecard.** Of the 67 NWS river-forecast locations across southeast Texas: **90% (60/67) reached flood stage; 69% (46/67) reached MAJOR flood stage; 46% (31/67) set all-time flood records.** NWS issued more than 300 flood-related warnings during the event. This is a precise, official quantification of "how much of the region's river network broke" that most retrospectives simply narrate as "rivers everywhere flooded." — source: NWS Service Assessment, Hurricane Harvey (June 2018), p. 10 — confidence: HIGH
4. **GOOD: the highest storm-tide reading was in Houston itself, not on the open coast.** The highest water level recorded anywhere during Harvey — 10.35 feet above Mean Higher High Water — was measured at Manchester, inside the Houston Ship Channel, driven mainly by excessive runoff pouring out of Buffalo and Brays Bayous — HIGHER than any open-coast storm-surge gauge from Corpus Christi to Matagorda Bay (which ranged roughly 3-7 feet). This flips the intuitive image of "storm surge hits the coast" — Harvey's single highest water-level reading was actually generated by inland urban runoff draining into the Ship Channel, not by surge from the Gulf. — source: NWS Service Assessment, Hurricane Harvey (June 2018), p. 8 (Section 2.4, Storm Surge) — confidence: HIGH
