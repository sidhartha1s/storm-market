## 1. THE DAM DECISION (Addicks & Barker Dams)

- **Authorization & purpose**: the Buffalo Bayou and Tributaries, Texas project was authorized by Congress in the 1930s, after Houston's 1929 and 1935 floods, specifically to provide flood-damage reduction for the city and port of Houston along Buffalo Bayou — source: USACE Galveston District permitting record, "Buffalo Bayou and Tributaries, Texas Resiliency Study" https://www.permits.performance.gov/permitting-project/other-projects/buffalo-bayou-and-tributaries-texas-resiliency-study — confidence HIGH
- **Construction dates**: Barker Dam completed 1945, Addicks Dam completed 1948 — both built by USACE Galveston District in what was then undeveloped land in far west Harris/east Fort Bend counties — source: USACE/Army.mil "What are the Addicks and Barker Dams and Reservoirs?" https://www.army.mil/article/86814/What_are_the_Addicks_and_Barker_Dams_and_Reservoirs_/ — confidence HIGH
- **Design/operating concept**: the reservoirs behind the two 1940s earthen dams are normally kept empty specifically so they can impound rainfall/runoff that would otherwise flood central/downtown Houston; water is released through gates into Buffalo Bayou after a storm — source: Army.mil (as above); Houston Chronicle "How the Barker and Addicks dams work" https://www.houstonchronicle.com/local/gray-matters/article/How-the-Barker-and-Addicks-dams-work-12171719.php — confidence HIGH. Note: the original 1940s master plan also called for a third reservoir (a proposed "Cypress Creek"/northwest Houston structure) and additional levee/canal works that were never built — source: ProPublica "Boomtown, Flood Town" (reporting, used only as a pointer here) https://www.propublica.org/article/boomtown-flood-town-text — confidence MED-HIGH
- **Pre-Harvey USACE risk classification — RESOLVED, exact language and year**: USACE's 2009 Issue Evaluation Study recommended raising Addicks and Barker from Dam Safety Action Classification (DSAC) Level 2 to **DSAC I ("Urgent and Compelling"/"Unsafe")** — the Corps' own internal designation was: **"CRITICALLY NEAR FAILURE: Progression toward failure is confirmed to be taking place under normal operations."** This DSAC I classification, assigned in September 2009, was confirmed by USACE's Senior Oversight Group in 2011 and made both dams part of the small group informally described in Corps and press reporting as "extremely high risk"/among the riskiest dams in the U.S. inventory — source: The Weather Channel, "Documents Show Army Corps Worried About Houston Dams in 2009, 8 Years Before Harvey Hit" (reporting on USACE's own documents) https://weather.com/storms/hurricane/news/houston-dams-harvey-addicks-barker ; corroborating reporting: RenewableEnergyWorld/EnergyCentral wire summary of Corps designation https://www.renewableenergyworld.com/energy-business/energy-finance/corps-designates-addicks-and-barker-dams-extremely-high-risk/ — confidence HIGH on the DSAC I "critically near failure" language and 2009/2011 dating (drawn from USACE's own study/classification as reported); confidence MED on precisely which two structural deficiencies (outlet gate works and dam-end erosion) were cited, since this is relayed via secondary reporting of internal USACE documents, not a directly fetched primary PDF in this pass — see OPEN ITEMS.
- **Pool levels during Harvey vs. design capacity — exact figures**: Addicks Dam's normal design pool sits at elevation 108.3 ft (NAVD88); its Maximum Design Pool is 115 ft; maximum storage capacity is 204,500 acre-feet across 16,780 acres. Barker Dam's normal design pool is 101.7 ft; its Maximum Design Pool is 108 ft; maximum storage capacity is 207,000 acre-feet across ~17,225 acres, with a controlled emergency-spillway elevation of 103.7 ft. During Harvey, USGS provisional gauge data recorded a **record high pool of 109.08 ft at Addicks** and a **record high pool of 101.5 ft at Barker**, both set August 30, 2017 — source: Texas Water Development Board reservoir pages, Addicks https://www.twdb.texas.gov/surfacewater/rivers/reservoirs/addicks/index.asp and Barker https://www.twdb.texas.gov/surfacewater/rivers/reservoirs/barker/index.asp ; USGS "Hurricane Harvey flooding, Addicks Reservoir" before/after imagery citing the provisional record-high figures https://www.usgs.gov/media/before-after/hurricane-harvey-flooding-addicks-reservoir-houston-tx — confidence HIGH
- **The controlled-release decision — date/time, VERIFY OR REFUTE "1:40 a.m."**: multiple contemporaneous and retrospective sources (Houston Chronicle, Houstonia Magazine, and law-firm case timelines built from court filings) consistently describe the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (Galveston District), coordinating with the Harris County Flood Control District, beginning controlled releases from Addicks and Barker into Buffalo Bayou in the pre-dawn hours of **August 28, 2017**, variously described as "around midnight," "shortly before 2:00 a.m.," or within a "midnight-to-2 a.m. window" — source: Houstonia Magazine, "The Deliberate Flooding of West Houston" https://www.houstoniamag.com/news-and-city-life/2017/10/barker-addicks-reservoirs-release-west-houston-memorial-energy-corridor-hurricane-harvey ; Zehl Law timeline (litigation-adjacent, built from court record) https://www.zehllaw.com/practice-areas/disputes/insurance-disputes/houston-addicks-barker-reservoir-release-damage-lawyers-flooding-timeline-before-and-after-hurricane-harvey/ — confidence MED-HIGH on "shortly before/around 2:00 a.m., Aug 28, 2017" as the defensible on-air window. **The specific "1:40 a.m." figure could NOT be independently confirmed against a primary USACE press release or contemporaneous official record in this research pass** — the USACE Galveston District's own news-release pages for this event (swg.usace.army.mil, titles: "USACE Galveston District to make intermittent releases at Addicks and Barker Dams," "Corps releases at Addicks and Barker Dams to begin," "USACE makes unexpected releases from Addicks and Barker Dams") exist and are the correct primary source to pin the exact minute, but returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch in this pass. **Recommend the script use "in the early hours before dawn, around two in the morning, on August twenty-eighth" rather than the precise "one-forty A M" until someone manually opens the USACE press release page and confirms the exact minute** — confidence LOW on the specific minute; MED-HIGH on the ~2 a.m./Aug 28 window. Flag as PARTIALLY UNVERIFIED.
- **Who decided / who announced / stated alternative**: the release decision was made and announced by USACE's Galveston District in coordination with the Harris County Flood Control District (one HCFCD official later stated the District was not consulted before releases began — a separate internal-coordination dispute, source: Houston Chronicle/Chron.com, "Official: Harris County Flood Control District not consulted before reservoir releases began" https://www.chron.com/news/politics/houston/article/Official-Harris-County-Flood-Control-District-12867163.php — confidence MED-HIGH). The stated rationale across all reporting is consistent: without a controlled release, continued inflow risked the pools rising to the point of uncontrolled overtopping of the earthen dams, which USACE and outside engineers characterized as risking catastrophic, uncontrolled dam failure and a far larger flood of downtown/central Houston than the controlled release itself — source: Houston Chronicle investigative series "Developing Storm," "Officials patched and prayed while pressure built on Houston's dams" (Dec 2017, built from public records and Corps interviews) https://www.houstonchronicle.com/news/houston-texas/houston/article/Officials-patched-and-prayed-while-pressure-built-12425719.php — confidence HIGH on the concept (controlled release chosen over overtopping/failure risk); confidence MED on precise USACE phrasing (relayed via reporting, not a directly fetched primary statement in this pass).
- **Release rate escalation**: releases began at roughly 718,000 gallons/minute and were increased to nearly 7 million gallons/minute within under 48 hours — source: Zehl Law timeline (as above, built from court filings/public record) — confidence MED-HIGH (litigation-adjacent secondary compilation, not independently cross-checked against a raw USACE gauge record in this pass).
- **Upstream flooding — homes, DISTINCT mechanism (reservoir pool rising onto land)**: approximately **14,000 homes** stood within the government-owned Addicks/Barker flood-pool/easement footprint at the time Harvey made landfall; **more than one-third of those flooded** during Harvey (i.e., roughly 4,500–5,000+ homes by that framing) — source: Texas Tribune, Dec. 2025 coverage of the Federal Circuit ruling https://www.texastribune.org/2025/12/24/hurricane-harvey-lawsuit-houston-addicks-barker-reservoirs/ — confidence MED-HIGH. **Flag a real discrepancy**: plaintiffs'-side legal reporting elsewhere cites a rounder, larger figure of "**more than 10,000 upstream homes and businesses**" flooded — source: Zehl Law (litigation-facing, secondary) https://www.zehllaw.com/army-corps-of-engineers-had-decades-to-avoid-need-to-flood-upstream-and-downstream-properties-during-hurricane-harvey/ — confidence MED. These two figures are NOT reconcilable from the sources gathered in this pass (one is "homes+businesses flooded," the other is "of ~14,000 homes in the pool, one-third flooded") — script should either use the Texas Tribune framing (mechanism: ~14,000 homes/structures sit inside the flood-pool footprint; Harvey flooded a documented one-third-plus of them) and flag the "10,000+" figure as a competing plaintiffs'-side estimate, or simply say "thousands of homes" and avoid a precise disputed number on air.
- **Downstream flooding — homes, DISTINCT mechanism (Buffalo Bayou overflow from the controlled releases)**: figures again diverge by source. Zehl Law's litigation timeline cites **"over 4,000 homes and businesses"** downstream, between North Wilcrest Drive and Chimney Rock, "severely flooded and, in some cases, completely submerged," many of which had NOT already flooded from Harvey's rainfall before the releases began — source: https://www.zehllaw.com/practice-areas/disputes/insurance-disputes/houston-addicks-barker-reservoir-release-damage-lawyers-flooding-timeline-before-and-after-hurricane-harvey/ — confidence MED. A separate secondary compilation cites **"approximately 8,000 downstream property owners"** affected — confidence LOW-MED (source not independently pinned to a primary record in this pass; treat as a second, higher competing estimate). Affected communities named across sources: Fleetwood, Thornwood, Westchester, Nottingham Forest, and the Energy Corridor. **Script guidance: DO NOT combine the upstream and downstream home counts into one number** — they are different properties, flooded by different physical mechanisms (upstream = rising reservoir pool; downstream = bayou overflow from the controlled release), and are the subject of two separate lawsuits (Section 2).

## 2. THE COURT RULINGS — the episode's hook (maximum precision required)

- **Upstream case — identity**: *In re Upstream Addicks and Barker (Texas) Flood-Control Reservoirs v. United States*, No. 1:17-cv-09001-CFL, U.S. Court of Federal Claims — a consolidated "sub-master docket" covering hundreds of individual upstream property owners, tried via 13 bellwether/"test" properties — source: CourtListener docket https://www.courtlistener.com/docket/8108539/in-re-upstream-and-and-barker-texas-flood-control-reservoirs-v/ ; case filings compiled at Justia https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/federal-claims/cofce/1:2017cv09001/35865/260/ — confidence HIGH
- **Trial**: a 10-day liability trial on the 13 test properties was held in Houston beginning May 6, 2019, presided over by **Senior Judge Charles F. Lettow** — source: Justia case record (as above); Harvard Law School faculty summary of the case https://faculty.law.harvard.edu/joseph-singer/2019/11/18/regulatory-takings-claim-against-united-states-for-reservoir-design-that-foreseeably-flooded-upstream-properties-after-tropical-storm-harvey — confidence HIGH
- **Exact holding — RESOLVED**: in the Dec. 2019 opinion (published at 146 Fed. Cl. 219), Judge Lettow ruled that the United States is liable under the **Fifth Amendment Takings Clause** for the upstream flooding — finding that government officials **knowingly and intentionally used private property to store rising floodwaters** during Harvey, as a foreseeable consequence of a reservoir system that was, by design, built and operated to protect downtown/central Houston at the expense of the land immediately behind the dams — source: CourtListener opinion page https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/8436218/in-re-upstream-addicks-and-barker-texas-flood-control-reservoirs-v/ ; Harvard Law faculty case summary (as above) — confidence HIGH on the takings-liability holding and its "knowing/intentional use of private property to store floodwater" framing; confidence MED on the precise verbatim sentence(s) of the opinion, since this pass could not get clean extracted text from the primary PDF (biotech.law.lsu.edu/blog/In-re-Upstream-Addicks.pdf saved locally but returned only binary/compressed stream data to automated extraction) — a manual read of that PDF, or of the Fed. Cl. reporter citation 146 Fed. Cl. 219, is recommended before quoting the opinion verbatim on air.
- **Liability only, NOT damages, in 2019 — CONFIRMED**: the 2019 Lettow opinion decided liability only. Damages for the 13 bellwether/test properties were separately determined in a later ruling: a federal judge found the government owed roughly **$550,000** combined to the owners of the test properties for the upstream taking — source: Houston Chronicle, "Homeowners owed $550K for Harvey flooding upstream of Barker and Addicks, federal judge says" https://www.houstonchronicle.com/news/houston-texas/houston/article/Properties-upstream-of-Addicks-Barker-reservoirs-17554181.php — confidence MED-HIGH (headline/figure corroborated by search-engine indexing of the article; this pass could not re-fetch the full article body directly — see OPEN ITEMS for exact date/property count pinning).
- **2022–2025 appeal and current status — RESOLVED, and this is a LIVE/recent development as of this writing (September 2026)**: the government appealed liability to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the **Federal Circuit** (the correct appellate court for Court of Federal Claims cases). On **December 23, 2025**, a three-judge Federal Circuit panel **unanimously affirmed** that the government is liable for the upstream taking, holding that USACE **"knew or should have known since the dams' construction that severe storms would cause the reservoirs to overflow and flood private properties,"** and — significantly for how future damages are calculated — characterized the flooding easement as a **permanent taking**, since hurricane-level storms "would inevitably recur without identifiable end," rather than a one-time event. The same decision struck down certain categories of consequential damages (lost rental income, lost business profits, relocation costs) while upholding compensation for the permanent flowage easement itself and for destroyed personal property, and remanded the case to the Court of Federal Claims to work out the remaining damages questions under that framework — source: Texas Tribune, Dec. 24, 2025 https://www.texastribune.org/2025/12/24/hurricane-harvey-lawsuit-houston-addicks-barker-reservoirs/ ; case-tracking site "Inside Addicks/Barker," which cites the Federal Circuit decision directly https://insideaddicksbarker.com/federal-circuit-affirms-government-liability-for-upstream-hurricane-harvey-flooding/ — confidence HIGH on the affirmance, permanent-taking characterization, and Dec. 23, 2025 date; confidence MED on the exact formal case caption/citation number for this specific Federal Circuit opinion, which this pass did not independently pin to a court-published cite (e.g. a specific F.4th citation) — see OPEN ITEMS. As of the sources gathered, the government retains the option to seek en banc Federal Circuit review or petition the U.S. Supreme Court; no further appeal outcome was found in this pass.
- **Downstream case — identity**: *In re Downstream Addicks and Barker (Texas) Flood-Control Reservoirs v. United States*, No. 1:17-cv-09002, U.S. Court of Federal Claims — source: CourtListener https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4728040/in-re-downstream-addicks-and-barker-texas-flood-control-reservoirs-v/ ; Justia case docket https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/federal-claims/cofce/1:2017cv09002/35866/235/ — confidence HIGH
- **Dismissal and exact grounds — RESOLVED**: on **February 19, 2020**, Judge **Loren A. Smith** dismissed the downstream takings claims. The court's own causation reasoning, as reported: **"the downstream plaintiffs' theory of causation ignores the simple fact that the gates were initially closed for the sole purpose of protecting their properties from floodwaters, that such mitigation failed because the impounded storm waters exceeded the Reservoirs' controllable capacity, and that ... Harvey was the sole and proximate cause of the floodwaters."** In other words, the court held that downstream plaintiffs could not show the *release decision itself* — as opposed to the storm's rainfall — was the legal cause of their flooding, since the releases were an attempted mitigation that was overwhelmed by the storm, not an independent government act that caused flooding that would not otherwise have occurred. This is best described as a **proximate-cause / "Act of God" dismissal**, not strictly a "combined-cause" doctrine in the sources gathered — source: Texas Tribune, "Federal judge dismisses suit against Army Corps for post-Harvey flooding" (Feb. 19, 2020) https://www.texastribune.org/2020/02/19/federal-judge-dismisses-against-army-corps-post-harvey-flooding/ — confidence HIGH on the outcome and reasoning as quoted/reported; confidence MED on treating this precisely as the primary opinion's own words versus a close paraphrase in secondary reporting — recommend pulling the opinion text directly (Justia link above) before an on-air verbatim quote.
- **Appeal — RESOLVED**: downstream plaintiffs appealed to the **U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit**, which **reversed** the dismissal (decision reported in reduceflooding.com's tracking coverage dated June 2, 2022; the underlying Federal Circuit opinion date was not independently pinned in this pass — treat "2022" as correct, exact month/day as MED confidence only). The Federal Circuit held the Court of Federal Claims had erred in concluding downstream appellants failed to assert **a cognizable property interest**, and separately rejected the lower court's "Act of God"-style reasoning as a basis for dismissal at the pleading stage — remanding the case for further proceedings to determine (a) whether appellants can establish causation given the government's actions, and (b) whether the government's "necessity" defense applies — source: reduceflooding.com case-tracking summary, "Appeals Court Revives Addicks-Barker Downstream Takings Cases" https://reduceflooding.com/2022/06/02/appeals-court-revives-addicks-barker-downstream-takings-cases/ — confidence MED-HIGH on the substance of the reversal/remand; confidence LOW on the exact Federal Circuit case caption and precise decision date (this tracking source does not itself cite one, and this pass could not independently locate the formal opinion) — see OPEN ITEMS. **Status after remand is UNVERIFIED in this pass**: one search result headline ("Homeowners downstream of Addicks, Barker win Harvey-era flood case" — Houston Chronicle) suggests a subsequent trial-court ruling favorable to downstream plaintiffs occurred at some point after the 2022 remand, but this pass could not fetch that article's content (site error on WebFetch) — **flag explicitly as UNVERIFIED; do not state on air that downstream plaintiffs have won or been awarded damages without confirming this article or a primary docket entry first.**
- **THE HOOK LINE — precision check (read this before writing the script's thesis sentence)**: the evidence supports a court finding the U.S. government liable for a Fifth Amendment taking tied to the Addicks/Barker dams — but ONLY for the **upstream** flood-pool properties, and specifically because the reservoirs were *designed and operated* to protect downtown Houston by using upstream land as a floodwater-storage buffer. The downstream claim — arguably the more dramatic "the government's own decision flooded thousands of homes that hadn't flooded yet" story — was DISMISSED, then revived on appeal, and its final resolution is NOT cleanly confirmed as of this research pass. Given that:
  - **SAFE, fully supported phrasing**: *"A federal court found the United States government liable for taking private property — for flooding thousands of homes upstream of two Houston dams — because the dams were built decades earlier to protect downtown Houston by using that upstream land, quietly, as a floodwater reservoir."*
  - **OVERSTATEMENT to avoid**: *"A federal court ruled the government flooded Houston"* — stated broadly, this conflates a narrow, geographically-bounded upstream liability finding with the city as a whole, and ignores that the downstream claim (which covers a much larger, more central swath of Houston along Buffalo Bayou) was dismissed and remains legally unresolved in the sources gathered here. The precise, defensible claim is that the government was found liable for flooding **specific, government-owned/easement-bound land it had always intended to use this way** — not that a court found the government responsible for Houston's flooding generally.
  - Script may still use "the government flooded Houston" as a dramatic COLD OPEN hook if the narration walks it back to the precise upstream-taking finding within the same act — that is an editorial choice, not a factual one, but the writer must know the walk-back is required.

## 3. THE MAPS/DEVELOPMENT (REPORTING allowed and used here — every claim labeled REPORTING: or RECORD:)

- **REPORTING:** development inside the Addicks watershed grew from **28 percent developed in 2001 to 41 percent by 2010** — source: ProPublica, "Boomtown, Flood Town" (full text) https://www.propublica.org/article/boomtown-flood-town-text — confidence HIGH (direct figures stated in the piece)
- **REPORTING:** in the Cypress Creek area (also inside/near the reservoir watersheds), population grew roughly 70 percent between 2000 and 2010, while developed land there rose from 23 percent to 33 percent over the same period — source: ProPublica "Boomtown, Flood Town" (as above) — confidence HIGH
- **REPORTING — named subdivision, disclosure finding**: **Stable Gate**, a roughly 250-home upscale subdivision in Cypress, was built in 2001 when it was NOT inside any mapped floodplain; FEMA's 2007 map update subsequently placed a large swath of the subdivision in the 100-year floodplain and other parts in the 500-year floodplain. Stable Gate first flooded during the April 2016 "Tax Day" storm — source: ProPublica "Boomtown, Flood Town" (as above) — confidence HIGH
- **REPORTING — Memorial City, "not in any known floodplain"**: residents in the Memorial City area flooded repeatedly (2009, 2015, 2016) despite living in areas that ProPublica/Texas Tribune describe as "not in any known floodplain," illustrating that official flood maps understated real risk well before Harvey — source: ProPublica "Boomtown, Flood Town" (as above) — confidence HIGH

- **REPORTING — Corps' own retrospective acknowledgment**: a Corps official interviewed for the piece acknowledged that if 1940s planners had foreseen the scale of Houston's growth, "the Corps probably would've done things differently — bought up more land" — the piece frames this as the Corps admitting, after the fact, that it did not anticipate (and did not act to prevent) the scale of development inside/near the flood-pool land — source: ProPublica "Boomtown, Flood Town" (as above) — confidence MED-HIGH (quote paraphrase per the search-engine extraction of the piece; recommend re-reading the full article directly before an exact on-screen quote).
- **REPORTING — countywide floodplain building since 2010**: since 2010, more than 7,000 residential buildings and more than 1,600 nonresidential buildings were built inside Harris County's mapped 100-year floodplains — source: ProPublica/Texas Tribune, "Hell and High Water: Why Isn't Texas Ready for the Next Big Hurricane?" https://projects.propublica.org/houston/ — confidence HIGH

This is industrial/ship-channel exposure, not residential, but is a strong "nobody fixed this even after being warned in writing, before Harvey" beat, since "Hell and High Water" published more than a year before Harvey made landfall — source: ProPublica/Texas Tribune "Hell and High Water" (as above); Columbia Journalism Review retrospective on the series' pre-Harvey publication and predictive accuracy https://www.cjr.org/local_news/texas-tribune-hurricane-harvey.php — confidence HIGH

## 4. THE PAVEMENT

- **RECORD (academic)** — coastal prairie loss: more than **30 percent** of the Texas coastal prairie wetlands that existed in Harris County as of 1992 have been lost to development — source: Texas A&M University research, as reported by Houston Chronicle, "The fight for Texas' prairie wetlands" https://www.houstonchronicle.com/local/gray-matters/article/wetlands-6704939.php — confidence MED-HIGH (figure attributed to Texas A&M research via Chronicle reporting; a direct A&M primary-document citation was not independently pinned in this pass — treat the 30%-since-1992 figure as reporting-relayed-from-academic-research, not a directly fetched academic PDF).
- **RECORD (academic)** — sprawl/flood-loss link: Sam Brody, a Texas A&M University at Galveston marine sciences professor, has published research finding that low-intensity urban sprawl amplifies flood-damage losses, because wetlands and prairie serve as natural detention basins that absorb and hold stormwater that otherwise rushes through homes and streets when paved over — source: Houston Chronicle "The fight for Texas' prairie wetlands" (as above), reporting on Brody's published research — confidence MED-HIGH
- **RECORD — HCFCD's own rainfall/exceedance figures**: Harris County Flood Control District's own post-storm compilation states that roughly **1 trillion gallons of water** fell across Harris County during the four-day event — an amount equal to nearly **70 percent of the county's entire average ANNUAL rainfall falling in four days** — and that Harvey's rainfall severity **surpassed 17 of 18 Texas state historical storm rainfall records**; Tropical Storm Allison's (2001) previous record water-surface levels were exceeded by 2.0–3.0 feet along the entire channel in some reaches — source: Harris County Flood Control District, Hurricane Harvey final impact report (PDF) https://reduceflooding.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/Final-HCFCD-Harvey-Report.pdf ; corroborated in Kinder Institute (Rice University) summary of HCFCD's Harvey data https://kinder.rice.edu/urbanedge/hurricane-harvey-data — confidence HIGH on the "1 trillion gallons"/"70 percent of annual rainfall in 4 days"/"17 of 18 records" figures (multiple corroborating citations of the same HCFCD report); confidence MED on the exact page/figure inside the HCFCD PDF, since this pass's direct PDF fetch returned only unparsed binary stream data rather than clean extracted text — see OPEN ITEMS.
- **Design storm standard vs. what Harvey delivered**: Houston's historic drainage/bayou system was engineered to a materially lower rainfall-intensity standard than what climate reality later required — one contemporaneous account describes the system as designed to clear roughly 30 cm (about 12 inches) of rain per 24-hour period, a standard reporting describes as "no match" for combined explosive development and increasingly extreme storms — source: CBC News, "Houston Harvey drainage" (secondary/reporting) https://amp.cbc.ca/news/world/houston-harvey-drainage-1.4267585 — confidence MED (concept and rough figure corroborated across reporting; recommend citing HCFCD's own design-manual standard directly, e.g. a specific "designed for a 2-year/10-year storm" bayou-channel-capacity figure, before an on-air specific number — the HCFCD PDF report likely states this precisely but this pass could not extract clean text from it). **Post-Harvey benchmark, useful as contrast**: Harris County's flood-map update after Harvey recalibrated design rainfall upward — a 100-year storm is now modeled as 17 inches of rain in 24 hours (up from 13.2 inches pre-Harvey), and a 500-year storm is now modeled as 25 inches in 24 hours (up from 18.9 inches) — source: Houston Chronicle, "New Harris County flood maps will be first in the nation to show more accurate risks" https://www.houstonchronicle.com/politics/houston/article/harris-county-flood-maps-floodplain-risk-17762023.php — confidence HIGH
- **Scale of Harvey vs. a "1,000-year storm"**: for most of the Houston area, a 1,000-year storm (0.1% annual chance of occurring) is modeled as roughly 30 inches of rain in 24 hours or about 38 inches over four days; Harvey delivered as much as 47–56 inches in the hardest-hit spots over its four-day duration (see Section 8's exact NWS-recorded totals), meaning the storm exceeded even the 1,000-year threshold in its most extreme locations — source: reporting compiling HCFCD/NWS-derived design-rainfall figures, cited via Kinder Institute/Houston Chronicle coverage (as above) — confidence MED-HIGH (design-threshold figure well corroborated; recommend citing HCFCD's own design-standard document directly for an exact-number on-air claim).

## 5. STAY-PUT CALL

- **Houston Mayor Sylvester Turner — exact quotes, exact date**: at a **Sunday, August 27, 2017** press conference defending the decision not to order a mandatory evacuation, Turner said: **"You cannot put, in the city of Houston, 2.3 million people on the road ... ## 7. FLOOD INSURANCE
