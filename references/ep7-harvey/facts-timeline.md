# Hurricane Harvey — Meteorology, Rainfall & Timeline Facts
Scope: formation/track, landfall, stall mechanism, rainfall records, water-volume estimates, death
toll, damage, and day-by-day Houston-area timeline. **Primary sources only** (NHC Tropical Cyclone
Report AL092017, NWS Service Assessment, NOAA/NCEI, USGS, HCFCD, FEMA). Investigative-journalism
and news sources are NOT used here except where explicitly flagged MED/LOW as the only citable
source for a claim that could not be traced to a primary document in this pass — never silently
dropped. All times given as stated in the source (UTC vs local CDT noted).

## 1. Formation, weakening, and redevelopment (Yucatan/Bay of Campeche)

- The wave that spawned Harvey moved off the west coast of Africa on **12 August 2017**. A tropical
  depression formed around **0600 UTC 17 August 2017**, about 440 n mi east of Barbados. The
  depression became Tropical Storm Harvey **12 hours later (1800 UTC 17 August)**. — source: NHC
  Tropical Cyclone Report AL092017 (Blake/Zelinsky), Synoptic History — https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL092017_Harvey.pdf — confidence: HIGH
- Harvey's center passed over Barbados at 1000 UTC 18 August and St. Vincent five hours later,
  reaching an initial peak intensity of 40 kt early on 18 August. Increasing northerly wind shear
  then caused Harvey to **weaken back to a tropical depression early on 19 August** and to
  **degenerate into a tropical wave by 1800 UTC 19 August, over the central Caribbean Sea**. — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **Redevelopment, verified precisely:** the remnant wave moved west/west-northwest, staying
  convectively active while crossing the **Yucatan Peninsula on 22 August**. A low pressure area
  formed late that day associated with a short-lived burst of deep convection. The low moved
  west-northwestward into the **Bay of Campeche early on 23 August**, and shortly after 0600 UTC,
  deep convection increased near the low. **By 1200 UTC 23 August, an Air Force Reserve
  reconnaissance aircraft found the circulation had become better-defined, indicating Harvey had
  regenerated into a tropical depression, centered about 150 n mi west of Progreso, Mexico.** — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- Cross-corroboration (independent second primary source, consistent framing): "The wave developed
  into a tropical depression east of Barbados early on August 17 and became Tropical Storm Harvey
  later that day. The tropical storm moved across the Windward Islands on August 18 but decayed
  into a tropical wave the next day. The wave moved steadily westward, eventually crossing the
  Yucatan Peninsula and entering the southwest Gulf of Mexico on August 22. The wave redeveloped
  into a tropical storm on August 23." — source: NWS Service Assessment, "August–September 2017
  Hurricane Harvey" (June 2018), §2.1 Evolution of Storm — https://www.weather.gov/media/publications/assessments/harvey6-18.pdf — confidence: HIGH
- After redevelopment, the depression was initially poorly organized with a large radius-of-maximum
  winds; Harvey then began rapid intensification late on 23 August in an environment of light shear,
  very warm water, and high mid-level moisture, becoming a hurricane late on 24 August, reaching
  Category 3 by midday 25 August, and Category 4 by 0000 UTC 26 August (immediately before Texas
  landfall). — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH

## 2. Rockport, TX landfall — exact parameters

- **First (primary) Texas landfall: Harvey's center made landfall on the northern end of San Jose
  Island, about 5 n mi east of Rockport, Texas, at 0300 UTC on 26 August 2017** — this is
  **10:00 PM CDT on 25 August 2017 local time** (CDT = UTC−5; note the UTC date is one calendar
  day ahead of the local date). — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **Category at landfall: Category 4** (Saffir-Simpson). **Sustained winds: 115 kt (132 mph** per
  the TCR's own kt-to-mph convention; the companion NWS Service Assessment states "132 mph
  sustained winds" independently, confirming this figure). **Minimum central pressure: 937 mb**
  (estimated, based on a dropsonde measurement of 938 mb with 10 kt of surface wind at 0215 UTC 26
  August). — source: NHC TCR AL092017, "Winds and Pressure" — confidence: HIGH
- The lowest *observed* pressure on land was 940.8 mb, reported by a storm chaser in Rockport at
  0331 UTC 26 August. A second storm-chaser report of 932.8 mb at about the same time is explicitly
  flagged by NHC as questionable — "other pressure readings in the area were several mb higher... It
  is also possible this pressure was observed in an eyewall mesovortex, which would not be
  representative of the overall circulation." — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH (report explicitly disputes the 932.8 mb reading — treat 937 mb as the authoritative NHC estimate)
- The highest *observed* sustained wind on land was 96 kt near Aransas Pass; the highest observed
  gust was 126 kt (145 mph) near Rockport (NHC TCR, Table 3) — cross-confirmed by the NWS Service
  Assessment's independently-sourced peak-gust map: **Port Aransas 132 mph gust; Copano Village,
  TX, 125 mph gust; Rockport 152 mph gust (flagged as a 1-second value, not the NWS-standard
  3-second gust).** NHC notes the northeastern quadrant of the hurricane (San Jose Island/Matagorda
  Island/Aransas NWR) was largely unpopulated and unmonitored, so the true peak winds were
  "probably not sampled by any anemometer." — sources: NHC TCR AL092017 (Table 3); NWS Service
  Assessment Figure 5 ("Hurricane Harvey Peak 10-meter Wind Gusts," WFO Corpus Christi) — confidence: HIGH
- **Second landfall**, 3 hours after the first, at **0600 UTC 26 August (1:00 AM CDT)**, on the
  Texas mainland — "slightly weaker due to land interaction, with **105 kt (121 mph) winds and an
  estimated central pressure of 948 mb**, southeast of Refugio on the northeast coast of Copano Bay,
  west of Holiday Beach." Harvey weakened to tropical-storm strength within 12 hours of the first
  landfall. — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **Final (third) landfall**, in southwestern Louisiana, at **0800 UTC 30 August**, near Cameron, LA,
  with 40-kt sustained winds — the storm had by then weakened substantially after four days
  essentially stationary near the Texas coast. — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH

## 3. The stall mechanism — exact synoptic explanation (verified against the TCR discussion)

- **Verbatim, primary-source mechanism:** "The steady northwestward motion of the cyclone stopped
  as Harvey became embedded in light steering currents between one mid-tropospheric high over the
  **Four Corners region** and another high over the **northern Gulf of Mexico**. The storm made a
  slow loop late on 26 August into 27 August, and drifted eastward or southeastward for the next
  few days." — source: NHC TCR AL092017, Synoptic History — confidence: HIGH
- **Important correction to the popular framing:** the commonly repeated shorthand for this episode
  ("a ridge over the Rockies and the Bermuda high over the western Atlantic blocked Harvey") is
  **not quite what the NHC TCR says for the stall itself**. The TCR's two stall-causing ridges are
  (1) a mid-tropospheric high over the **Four Corners region** (AZ/NM/CO/UT — i.e., the western
  US/Intermountain West, consistent with "Rockies" as a loose gloss) and (2) a high over the
  **northern Gulf of Mexico** (not the western Atlantic/Bermuda high). The western Atlantic ridge
  is a real feature in the TCR, but it appears **later, and its role is to end the stall, not cause
  it**: "the vertical wind shear was too strong for much intensification, and Harvey reached a final
  peak intensity of 45 kt late on 29 August. By that time, the storm turned to the north-northeast
  due to a **strengthening ridge over the western Atlantic**, its center never having moved more
  than 60 n mi offshore of the Texas Coast." — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH (the
  distinction between the "blocking pair" and the "release" ridge is drawn directly from the report's
  own two-part discussion — this is not an inference)
- Cross-corroborating second primary source: "On the morning of August 24, NHC noted Hurricane
  Harvey's forward speed would slow in a couple of days, due in part to **high pressure sliding east
  from the Intermountain West**." — source: NWS Service Assessment, §2.2 Wind and Tornado — https://www.weather.gov/media/publications/assessments/harvey6-18.pdf — confidence: HIGH (independently names the same Four Corners/Intermountain West ridge as the TCR, without using the word "Bermuda high" or "western Atlantic" for the blocking pair — corroborates the correction above)
- **Why the rain was so extreme, not just the stall (verbatim mechanism):** "While Harvey was very
  slow moving over Texas, not all drifting cyclones produce such torrential rain totals, and it is
  notable that the heaviest rainfall fell outside of the core of the cyclone. Harvey moved into a
  somewhat baroclinic environment over Texas, with slightly cooler and drier air over the southern
  United States behind a weak stationary front... The weak front was situated across the Houston
  metro area from 26-27 August, enhancing surface convergence and lift within the very warm and
  humid air on the eastern side of Harvey, leading to several episodes of heavy rain... The rain
  rates observed in these bands were exceptional, with 6.8 inches of rain in just one hour documented
  in extremely heavy rain bands training over the same location [southeastern Houston]. The front
  hardly moved from 27-28 August... It should be noted that while the magnitude of this event was
  unprecedented, the synoptic situation was not, and previously has been associated with other
  tropical cyclone flood events near the coast." — source: NHC TCR AL092017, "Rainfall and Flooding" — confidence: HIGH

## 4. Rainfall records — Nederland, Cedar Bayou, and the map-color claim

- **Current official US tropical-cyclone rainfall record (as of the TCR's 9 May 2018 update):
  60.58 inches near Nederland, TX**, with a second report of **60.54 inches near Groves, TX** —
  both measured over the storm's full Texas duration (24 August – 1 September 2017). These values
  (and five other, unnamed stations) exceed the previously accepted US tropical-cyclone storm-total
  rainfall record of 52.00 inches at Kanalohuluhulu Ranger Station, Hawaii, from Hurricane Hiki
  (August 1950). For the *continental* United States specifically, the previous record was 48.00
  inches at Medina, TX, from Tropical Storm Amelia (1978). — source: NHC TCR AL092017, "Rainfall
  and Flooding" (report explicitly notes it was updated 9 May 2018 to add "a picture of Nederland,
  Texas, rain gauge location") — confidence: HIGH
- **Radar-estimated (not gauge-measured) totals were even higher: as high as 65-70 inches in
  southeastern Texas**, per NOAA's multi-radar, multi-sensor quantitative precipitation estimation —
  the TCR notes there were "few rainfall reports near the center of the radar-estimated maximum...
  and these radar estimates represent the highest rainfall that could have occurred (outside of the
  actual measurements)." — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **Cedar Bayou, TX — the in-storm preliminary record, later a live/current-events figure, NOT the
  final NHC total.** NHC's own advisory during the event: "A preliminary report from a rain gauge
  has broken the Texas tropical cyclone rainfall record. The Cedar Bayou gauge, east of Highlands,
  Texas, has reported 51.88 inches of rain as of 3 PM CDT [29 August 2017]... This total is higher
  than the previous record of 48 inches set during tropical cyclone Amelia of 1978 at Medina,
  Texas." — source: NHC, Tropical Storm Harvey Advisory Number 38, 4:00 PM CDT Tuesday, 29 August
  2017 — https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/archive/2017/al09/al092017.public.038.shtml — confidence: HIGH (this is the exact primary in-storm advisory text; it is a *preliminary* figure, not the final TCR record)
- **Cedar Bayou gauge later flagged as compromised.** Multiple contemporaneous news reports (ABC
  News, CBS News) state that NWS Houston/Galveston subsequently took the 51.88-inch Cedar Bayou
  reading "off the books," because the gauge (and several others) was flooded or wind-affected in a
  way that compromised its ability to record true rainfall. **This could not be traced to a primary
  NWS/NHC statement in this pass** — the final NHC TCR (Section above) does not name Cedar Bayou at
  all among its highest totals (Nederland/Groves plus "five other stations"), which is at least
  consistent with Cedar Bayou having been excluded from the final authoritative table, but the TCR
  gives no explicit statement about *why*. — sources: ABC News, "Harvey sets preliminary US rain
  record with 51.88 inches: National Weather Service" (https://abcnews.com/US/harvey-shatters-us-rainfall-record-tropical-system-national/story?id=49491827); CBS News, "Cedar Bayou, Texas, hits
  record-breaking rainfall, says National Weather Service" (https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/cedar-bayou-texas-record-rainfall-harvey-flooding-national-weather-service) — confidence: MED (news reporting of an NWS-attributed retraction, not a primary NWS document found directly stating the retraction and reason)
- **Houston metro area total: NHC TCR states "eighteen values over 48 inches were recorded across
  southeastern Texas, with 36 to 48 inches recorded in the Houston metro area."** — source: NHC TCR
  AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **The "NWS added new colors to rainfall maps" claim — VERIFIED, but only via secondary
  (journalistic) sourcing in this pass, not a primary NWS graphic/statement directly fetched.**
  Multiple contemporaneous outlets (CBS News, NPR, GeekWire, Government Executive), each
  attributing the change to the National Weather Service, describe the same specific mechanism:
  the NWS's rainfall/precipitation map color scale previously topped out at a single "greater than
  15 inches" category (dark mauve/purple); because Harvey's totals exceeded that scale, NWS added
  **two new shades of purple: one for 20-30 inches and a lighter "light pink" shade for over 30
  inches** (with the former top color reassigned to the new 15-20 inch band). NPR's framing: "The
  National Weather Service has had to update the color scheme on some of its graphics because the
  established range didn't have a color to represent the volume of rain that's fallen in Texas
  because of Harvey." — sources: NPR, "National Weather Service Adds New Colors So It Can Map
  Harvey's Rains" (https://www.npr.org/sections/thetwo-way/2017/08/28/546776542/national-weather-service-adds-new-colors-so-it-can-map-harveys-rains); CBS News,
  "Harvey forces weather service to add two new colors to maps" (https://www.cbsnews.com/news/harvey-forces-weather-service-to-add-two-new-colors-to-maps/) — confidence: MED (consistent, detailed reporting across multiple outlets, each attributing the specific color/threshold change to NWS — but this pass did not locate a primary NWS/WPC press release or the modified map product itself to confirm verbatim)

## 5. Total water volume — "trillions of gallons," with exact scope

- **Harris County specifically: approximately one trillion gallons of water fell across the county
  in four days** — "The Harris County Flood Control District estimated one trillion gallons of
  water was dumped on the county alone in four days," equivalent to running Niagara Falls for
  15 days or filling NRG Stadium roughly 1,400 times; this also represented nearly 70% of the
  county's entire average annual rainfall falling in those four days. — source: Harris County Flood
  Control District, as reported via NOAA Climate.gov event-tracker analysis (NOAA staff writer Tom
  Di Liberto, published 18 September 2017) — https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/reviewing-hurricane-harveys-catastrophic-rain-and-flooding — confidence: HIGH (HCFCD is the originating primary agency for the county-scoped figure; the NOAA Climate.gov page is itself an official NOAA publication citing it)
- **Broader Texas + Louisiana totals ("24.5 trillion" and "33 trillion" gallons) are journalistic
  calculations, not agency-issued totals, and could NOT be traced to a primary NWS/NOAA/USGS
  document in this pass.** The Washington Post's Capital Weather Gang calculated 24.5 trillion
  gallons across Texas and Louisiana as of 30 August 2017 (later updated to a 33-trillion-gallon
  total for the full event) by integrating official NWS/NOAA gauge and radar rainfall data over the
  affected area — the underlying rainfall data is NWS/NOAA-sourced, but the "trillions of gallons"
  aggregate itself is a newspaper's derived statistic, not a number NWS/NOAA/USGS published
  directly as a headline figure. — source: Washington Post, "Harvey has unloaded 24.5 trillion
  gallons of water on Texas and Louisiana" (30 August 2017) — https://www.washingtonpost.com/news/capital-weather-gang/wp/2017/08/30/harvey-has-unloaded-24-5-trillion-gallons-of-water-on-texas-and-louisiana/ — confidence: MED (methodology is transparent and data-source is primary, but the headline aggregate itself is a secondary/derived calculation, not an agency-published figure — flagged per this file's sourcing rule rather than dropped)
- **Recommendation for the script:** use the HCFCD/NOAA "one trillion gallons in Harris County in
  four days" figure as the HIGH-confidence anchor stat; if a Texas-wide "trillions" figure is
  wanted for scale, attribute it explicitly to the Washington Post's calculation rather than
  presenting it as an NWS/NOAA-stated number.

## 6. Total rainfall over Harris County / Houston — and the "500-year" / "1-in-1000-year" language

- **NHC TCR's own scoped Houston-area figure: "36 to 48 inches recorded in the Houston metro
  area,"** within a broader "eighteen values over 48 inches... recorded across southeastern Texas."
  — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **The actual NWS/NOAA return-period characterization (verbatim, primary source) — do not repeat
  the popular "500-year flood" line without this precise framing:** "NOAA recently completed an
  analysis of annual exceedance probabilities for southeastern Texas (Fig. 12) after Harvey, with a
  large portion of that area experiencing a flood with less than a 1-in-1000 (0.1%) chance of
  occurring in any given year (e.g., a 1000-year or greater flood). While established records of
  this nature are not kept, given the exceptional exceedance probabilities, it is unlikely the
  United States has ever seen such a sizable area of excessive tropical cyclone rainfall totals as
  it did from Harvey." — source: NHC TCR AL092017, "Rainfall and Flooding" — confidence: HIGH
- **The "500-year" framing does appear in the TCR, but in a different, narrower context (flood
  insurance, not the storm's meteorological return period):** "There is, however, still a large
  uncertainty in the total damage estimate (hence the large confidence interval). This is due to
  many factors, including that a majority of the residential flood loss claims are from outside the
  **500-year flood plain**, where there is low National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP)
  participation." — source: NHC TCR AL092017, "Casualty and Damage Statistics" — confidence: HIGH
- **Net verdict on the popular "500-year flood" claim:** the NHC TCR's own return-period statistic
  for the rainfall event itself is **"1-in-1000 (0.1%)... 1000-year or greater flood,"** not
  "500-year" — "500-year flood plain" in the primary source refers to FEMA flood-map zoning
  (insurance risk categories) for where residential damage occurred, not NOAA's characterization of
  how rare Harvey's rainfall was. Popular media commonly conflates the two. — confidence: HIGH (this distinction is drawn directly from the TCR's own two separate uses of the terms)

## 7. Death toll — NHC TCR official breakdown, with the report's own scoping language

- **NHC TCR (updated 9 May 2018): "Harvey is responsible for at least 68 direct deaths in the
  United States, all in Texas. Over half of the deaths (36) were in Harris County in the Houston
  metro area... All but three of the deaths were from freshwater flooding, and none of the deaths
  can be linked to the storm surge, which is quite remarkable for a category 4 hurricane landfall.
  Still, Harvey is the deadliest U.S. hurricane in terms of direct deaths since Sandy (2012) and is
  the deadliest hurricane to hit Texas since 1919. About 35 additional deaths are ascribed to
  indirect causes, such as electrocution, motor-vehicle crashes and isolation from necessary medical
  services."** This implies a combined direct + indirect total of approximately **68 + ~35 = ~103**.
  — source: NHC TCR AL092017, "Casualty and Damage Statistics" (footnote defines "direct" vs
  "indirect" per NHC convention: direct = drowning/storm surge/rough seas/rip currents/freshwater
  floods/lightning/wind-related structural failure; indirect = heart attacks, house fires,
  electrocution from downed lines, vehicle accidents on wet roads, etc.) — confidence: HIGH
- **Second primary source gives a different combined total: 105.** The NWS Service Assessment
  (June 2018) Executive Summary states: "Hurricane Katrina caused many more deaths than Hurricane
  Harvey, 1,833 deaths as compared to 105." Elsewhere in the same report (§2.3), it restates the
  direct-death figure identically to the TCR: "Approximately 68 deaths were directly attributed to
  impacts from Hurricane Harvey, and all but three of these fatalities were due to freshwater flood
  drowning. Harris County, TX, experienced the highest number of reported fatalities with 36
  flooding deaths." — source: NWS Service Assessment (June 2018), Executive Summary and §2.3 —
  https://www.weather.gov/media/publications/assessments/harvey6-18.pdf — confidence: HIGH
- **Discrepancy flagged, not resolved:** both primary NWS/NHC documents agree exactly on 68 direct
  deaths and 36 in Harris County, but the TCR's own arithmetic ("about 35" indirect) implies ~103
  total, while the Service Assessment states the combined total as 105 — a 2-death difference not
  explained in either document as fetched in this pass. Use "68 direct deaths (36 in Harris County),
  with a combined direct+indirect total in the 103-105 range depending on source/date" rather than
  a single precise combined number. — confidence: HIGH on each individual figure; MED on which
  combined total is "the" official one, since the two primary documents do not reconcile

## 8. Damage total — official NOAA/NCEI estimate, and the "tied with Katrina" claim precisely evaluated

- **NHC TCR's own damage estimate (as of its 9 May 2018 publication): "The latest NOAA damage
  estimate from Harvey is $125 billion, with the 90% confidence interval ranging from $90 to $160
  billion."** The TCR explicitly discusses the "tied with Katrina" question and resolves it
  directly: **"The mid-point of the estimate would tie Katrina (2005) as the costliest United
  States tropical cyclone, which was also $125 billion... However, the unadjusted costliest
  tropical cyclone list is not the most relevant record to examine because of inflation and other
  cost increases since 2005. A more reasonable comparison uses the Consumer Price Index
  (CPI)-adjusted technique, which modifies 2005 dollars to 2017. The adjustments make Katrina's
  total $161.3 billion in 2017 dollars, leading to Harvey being the 2nd costliest U.S. tropical
  cyclone."** — source: NHC TCR AL092017, "Casualty and Damage Statistics" — confidence: HIGH
- **Current (January 2025-vintage) NOAA NCEI "Costliest U.S. Tropical Cyclones" table confirms the
  same non-tie verdict, with updated adjustment-year dollars:**
  | Basis | Katrina (2005) | Harvey (2017) | Tied? |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Unadjusted (nominal dollars of the storm's own year) | $125.0B | $125.0B | Identical nominal figure — see caveat below |
  | CPI-adjusted to 2024 dollars | **$201.3B (rank 1)** | **$160.0B (rank 2)** | NOT tied — Katrina clearly ahead |
  — source: NOAA NCEI, "Costliest U.S. Tropical Cyclones" (last updated 10 January 2025) —
  https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/billions/dcmi.pdf — confidence: HIGH
- **Verdict: "tied with Katrina" is a SPECIFIC-DOLLAR-BASIS NUANCE, not simply TRUE or FALSE.**
  - It is **TRUE only in unadjusted nominal dollars** — both storms carry an identical headline
    figure of $125.0B in NOAA/NCEI's table — but this is not a true apples-to-apples tie, because
    the $125.0B for Katrina is in 2005 dollars and the $125.0B for Harvey is in 2017 dollars (12
    years of inflation apart). NOAA/NCEI itself flags this exact problem: the unadjusted list "is
    not the most relevant record to examine because of inflation."
  - It is **FALSE once adjusted for inflation to a common dollar-year** — Katrina is unambiguously
    costlier, whether using the TCR's own 2017-dollar comparison ($161.3B vs $125B) or the current
    2024-dollar NCEI table ($201.3B vs $160.0B). Harvey ranks 2nd costliest U.S. tropical cyclone on
    record by both the 2018 TCR's contemporary analysis and the most recent (Jan. 2025) NCEI
    table — a consistent, cross-time-period verdict from two independent primary-source
    vintages. — confidence: HIGH

## 9. Day-by-day Houston-area timeline, 24-30 August 2017

- **Thursday, 24 August (night):** rainfall from Harvey's outer bands began on the Texas coast
  between Brownsville and Galveston as Harvey's forward speed slowed to about 5 mph approaching the
  coast. — source: NWS Service Assessment, §2.3 — confidence: HIGH
- **Friday, 25 August:** Harvey intensified from Category 2 to Category 4 within 12 hours; NHC's
  first Hurricane Watch for Texas (Port Mansfield to San Luis Pass) had been issued the morning of
  23 August, 60 hours prior to landfall. — sources: NHC TCR AL092017; NWS Service Assessment §2.1 — confidence: HIGH
- **Saturday, 26 August, 0300 UTC (10:00 PM CDT Friday 25 August, local):** first landfall near
  Rockport, TX — Category 4, 115 kt/132 mph sustained, 937 mb. — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **26 August, 0600 UTC (1:00 AM CDT):** second landfall on the Texas mainland near Refugio/Copano
  Bay — 105 kt/121 mph, 948 mb. — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **26 August, 5:18 AM CDT:** highest measured storm surge of the event recorded at Port Lavaca, TX
  — 6.71 ft surge / 7.06 ft storm tide (NOS tide gauge). — source: NWS Service Assessment, §2.4
  Storm Surge — confidence: HIGH
- **26 August (first 48 hours after landfall):** rainfall amounts over 20 inches reported in the
  Corpus Christi area as Harvey weakened to tropical-storm strength and its center meandered
  northwest of Victoria, TX. — source: NWS Service Assessment, §2.3 — confidence: HIGH
- **26 August, 9:27 PM CDT:** WFO Houston issued the first of what became 10 flash flood emergency
  warnings, for West Central Harris, Eastern Fort Bend, and Northern Brazoria counties, as
  saturated soils and rain rates began outpacing Houston's drainage capacity. — source: NWS Service
  Assessment, §2.3 — confidence: HIGH
- **Late 26 August into 27 August:** the storm's steady northwestward motion stopped as Harvey
  became embedded between the Four Corners high and the northern Gulf of Mexico high (see Section
  3); it executed a slow loop and then drifted east/southeast for several days. — source: NHC TCR
  AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **Sunday, 27 August (morning):** 48-hour rainfall totals approaching 25 inches in the Houston
  area, with most of that falling in a 9-12 hour window late Saturday night into early Sunday
  morning; nearly 57% of Harris County Flood Control District gauges recorded record flooding, and
  Harris County Judge Ed Emmett issued the public appeal, quoted verbatim: **"If you have a boat, we
  need your help."** — source: NWS Service Assessment, §2.3 — confidence: HIGH (verbatim quote, attributed directly in the primary document)
- **27-28 August:** the near-stationary front responsible for the heaviest rain bands "hardly moved,"
  with rain rates up to 6.8 inches in a single hour documented in southeastern Houston from bands
  training repeatedly over the same location. — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **28 August, 0300 UTC:** the storm center moved back offshore over Matagorda Bay, with winds
  slightly re-strengthening as deep convection re-formed near and north of the center; vertical
  wind shear was too strong for much further intensification. — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **28-30 August:** the front's position shifted to the vicinity of Beaumont/Port Arthur late on 28
  August as Harvey's center moved offshore and began losing tropical characteristics; the heaviest
  rain shifted eastward into Jefferson County on 28-30 August, producing the event's absolute
  rainfall maxima (Nederland/Groves, Section 4) in that area. — source: NHC TCR AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **29 August (late):** Harvey reached a final peak intensity of 45 kt as it turned north-northeast
  due to the strengthening western Atlantic ridge (see Section 3), its center never having moved
  more than 60 n mi offshore of the Texas coast during the whole stalled period. — source: NHC TCR
  AL092017 — confidence: HIGH
- **29 August, 4:00 PM CDT:** NHC Advisory #38 reports the preliminary Cedar Bayou 51.88-inch total
  (Section 4) as the then-current Texas tropical-cyclone rainfall record, with isolated storm
  totals over the upper Texas coast/Houston-Galveston metro projected to reach 50 inches. — source:
  NHC, Tropical Storm Harvey Advisory Number 38 — https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/archive/2017/al09/al092017.public.038.shtml — confidence: HIGH
- **Tuesday-Wednesday, 29-30 August:** the center moved slowly east-southeast and back offshore;
  heavy rainfall continued across metropolitan Houston, spreading across east Texas from
  Beaumont/Port Arthur and extending eastward toward Lake Charles, LA, with numerous sites
  recording over 50 inches and two locations in Jefferson County (about 10 miles southeast of
  Beaumont) exceeding 60 inches. — source: NWS Service Assessment, §2.3 — confidence: HIGH
- **30 August, 0800 UTC:** Harvey's final landfall near Cameron, southwestern Louisiana, 40 kt
  sustained winds, after which it weakened to a tropical depression late on 30 August, transitioned
