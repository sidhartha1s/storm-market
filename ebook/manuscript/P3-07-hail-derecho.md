# Part Three, Playbook 7 — Hail, Derecho and Straight-Line Wind

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

On the afternoon of 10 August 2020 a line of thunderstorms crossed eastern Iowa and northwest Illinois
in a few hours. The Weather Service's own account: intermittent straight-line winds in the **100 to 130
mph** range through Benton, Linn, Jones, Cedar and Clinton counties, **maximum estimated winds around
140 mph** where an apartment complex in southwest Cedar Rapids was wrecked, and a **maximum measured
gust of 126 mph** at Atkins, Iowa.

But the number that makes this event worth opening a chapter with is not the peak. It is the duration.
**Many locations had sustained high winds and damaging gusts for 30 to 60 minutes, against the 10 to 20
minutes that is typical for a derecho.** An hour of category-4 wind out of an ordinary summer
thunderstorm line. NWS calls a derecho of this intensity roughly a once-in-a-decade event for the
region; the previous ones were in 1998 and 2011.
«SRC: NWS Quad Cities (Davenport), "Midwest Derecho — August 10, 2020" event summary. |
https://www.weather.gov/dvn/summary_081020 | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Almost nobody outside the affected area remembers it, and that is the theme of this playbook.

**Hail and straight-line wind are the most frequent billion-dollar hazard in the United States and the
one with the lowest public profile.** NOAA's billion-dollar disaster record for 1980 to 2024 counts **403
events costing over a billion dollars each, and 203 of them — fully half — were severe storms**: the
ordinary summer thunderstorm complex producing hail, downbursts and derechos. No other category comes
close on frequency. Tropical cyclones, the next most numerous, number 67.

**And here is the honest correction to the usual framing.** Those 203 severe storms account for **$514
billion, about 18 per cent of the $2.9 trillion total**, while 67 tropical cyclones account for **$1.54
trillion, about 53 per cent.** Hurricanes cost far more in total; severe storms happen far more often, at
an average of $2.5 billion an event against $23 billion for a tropical cyclone.

That difference is the entire reason this playbook exists as a *household* chapter rather than a regional
one. **A hurricane is a rarer, larger event that arrives with days of warning in a defined part of the
country. A severe convective storm is the one you should expect, wherever you live, several times a
decade, with an hour's notice.** For most readers of this book, over a lifetime, this is the hazard that
will actually visit — and 2,145 deaths across those 203 events says it is not merely a property matter.
«SRC: NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information, "Billion-Dollar Weather and Climate
Disasters," United States summary, 1980–2024 (CPI-adjusted) — 403 events totaling $2,918.1B and 16,941
deaths; severe storm 203 events (50.4% of frequency), $514.4B (17.6% of costs), $2.5B per event, 2,145
deaths; tropical cyclone 67 events (16.6%), $1,543.3B (52.9%), $23.0B per event, 7,211 deaths; 1980–2024
annual average 9.0 events, 2020–2024 annual average 23.0 events. |
https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/billions/state-summary/US | retrieved 2026-07-29»

A derecho, specifically, is a long-lived line of storms producing a swathe of damaging straight-line wind
— not a tornado, though it does tornado-scale damage over a vastly larger area. The damage pattern is the
tell: a tornado scatters debris in every direction; a derecho lays everything down the same way.

This is the hazard that funds the money chapter. It is also the one where an ordinary household most
often ends up in a dispute with an insurer, because the damage is real, partial, and arguable.

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Killed by:** falling trees and limbs, onto houses, cars and people; flying debris; and vehicles in it.
Fatalities are far lower than tornado numbers because the wind is unidirectional and structures fail less
catastrophically. Hail itself very rarely kills a person indoors, though it injures people caught out in
it, and large hail is genuinely dangerous to anyone unsheltered.

The indirect deaths matter more, as always: multi-day outages in August produce heat deaths and CO deaths
from generators run indoors.

**Costs — the reason this playbook is long:**

**Roofing** is the single largest claim category, and hail damage to shingles is frequently invisible from
the ground while still shortening the roof's life by years. **Vehicles**, in a hail event, are a large
loss all at once. **Siding and windows** on the windward side. And **the long outage**, which cascades
into spoiled food, hotel nights and lost work.

Then two financial traps specific to this hazard, both of which cost households real money:

**Separate wind and hail deductibles.** Many policies carry one, frequently as a percentage of the
dwelling limit rather than a flat sum. On a moderately valued house that turns an apparently insured
claim into a mostly uninsured one. This is why Protection 102's audit lists deductibles in the plural.

**Roof settlement on an actual cash value basis.** Two policies can both say they cover your roof and pay
very different amounts. **Replacement cost** pays up to the full cost to repair at current prices; **actual
cash value** pays replacement cost *minus depreciation* — less, because the roof was old.

**Here is the sentence from the Texas insurance regulator that most homeowners have never read: *"as roofs
age, some companies will switch to actual cash value."*** Not at purchase. At renewal. And *"if your roof
is in poor condition, your company might not cover your roof at all."* The regulator's instruction follows
from that: *"when you renew your policy, check to see if there are changes to your roof coverage."* Your
insurer should tell you when it changes — the renewal packet is where that notice lives, which is the
document nobody opens.

Two more things from the same source, both worth knowing before a storm rather than after one. **Ask
whether your wind-and-hail deductible is different from your deductible for everything else**, because in
hail states it frequently is, and it is frequently a percentage. And if you *do* have replacement cost
cover, expect **two cheques**: a partial payment first, and the balance *"after you've started repairs."*
Households that do not know this assume the first check is the settlement, and either argue with the
insurer or under-repair the roof.

And the line that ends most disputes before they start: **your insurer *"won't pay for a new roof just
because it's old or worn out."*** This is storm-damage cover, not a maintenance plan.
«SRC: Texas Department of Insurance, "Insurance and your roof: What to know when buying a policy or
filing a claim" and "Home policies: Replacement cost or actual cash value?" — replacement cost pays up to
the full cost to repair at current prices, actual cash value pays less if the roof is older or showing
wear; "as roofs age, some companies will switch to actual cash value"; if the roof is in poor condition the
company might not cover it at all and should tell you when coverage changes; check for changes to roof
coverage at renewal; ask whether the wind and hail deductible differs from other deductibles; replacement
cost claims are paid in two cheques, the balance after repairs have started; "your insurance company won't
pay for a new roof just because it's old or worn out." |
https://www.tdi.texas.gov/tips/replacing-your-roof.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»

## Before

- **71 — impact-rated shingles.** This is the protection with the clearest financial case in the book,
  because it addresses the country's biggest claim driver and it frequently earns a premium discount that
  is documented and available. Do it when you re-roof, not as a standalone job, and get the class
  certification in writing for Protection 104.
- **70 — ring-shank renailing**, at the same re-roof, and **69 — the sealed roof deck.** Together these
  three make the re-roof the single highest-value moment in a house's protective life.
- **73 — opening protection**, or at minimum knowing which openings are exposed on the storm's usual
  approach side.
- **102 — the insurance audit**, and here it is not optional. Find the wind/hail deductible, find out
  whether the roof is replacement cost or ACV, and find out the age at which your policy's schedule
  starts depreciating it.
- **104 — mitigation credits**, requested from the carrier's own table.
- **46 — whole-home surge protection**, because this hazard's outage-and-restoration cycle is the most
  common way household electronics die.
- **Trees.** The protection nobody lists: have a qualified arborist look at anything large enough to
  reach the house. Dead limbs, split crotches, decay at the base, and species that shed. This is the
  cheapest life-safety spend in this playbook and it is the thing that actually kills people.
- **1, 13, 37–46 — the outage stack**, because a derecho outage is measured in days to weeks over a wide
  area with every utility crew in the region committed.
- **100, 101 — video inventory and serial numbers**, including the roof and the vehicles, photographed
  from a ladder or with a drone if you can, *before* any storm. A dated "before" photograph of an intact
  roof is the strongest single piece of evidence in a hail dispute.

**Where you park matters.** A carport, a garage, or the lee side of a building is a meaningful protection
for a vehicle in a hail event, and moving the car when a warning is issued costs two minutes.

## During

**A severe thunderstorm warning is not a minor product.** It means winds of at least 58 miles an hour or
hail at least an inch across — and since 2021 the warning carries a **damage-threat tag** with three
levels, which is the single most useful thing to learn about this hazard.

The levels, lowest to highest, are **base, considerable and destructive.** *Considerable* means at least
1.75-inch hail (golf ball) or 70 mph winds. *Destructive* means at least **2.75-inch hail (baseball) or
80 mph winds** — and a destructive tag **automatically fires a wireless emergency alert to every phone
in the warned area.** A considerable tag does not. When no tag appears, the threat is base level.

That asymmetry is the practical point. **If your phone screams for a thunderstorm, it is not a routine
thunderstorm** — the weather service reserves that alert for roughly the top ten per cent of severe
storms nationally, and most of them are damaging-wind events: derechos and the largest supercells. It is
the same alert channel as a tornado warning, and it deserves the same response.
«SRC: NOAA National Weather Service, "New 'Destructive' Severe Thunderstorm Warning category to trigger
Wireless Emergency Alerts on mobile phones" (22 July 2021) — three damage-threat categories base,
considerable, destructive; destructive = 2.75 in hail and/or 80 mph winds and auto-activates a WEA;
considerable = 1.75 in hail and/or 70 mph and does not; on average only 10 per cent of severe
thunderstorms reach destructive each year, mostly damaging-wind events. |
https://www.weather.gov/news/072221-svr-wea | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**When it is issued:** vehicles under cover or against a building. Everything loose brought in. Charge
everything. Unplug what you can spare (Protection 120). Then get away from windows and go to an interior
room — the same room as the tornado plan, because large hail breaks glass and a derecho's leading gust
can arrive as suddenly as a tornado's.

**Do not go outside to watch, and do not go outside to move the car once it has started.** People are
injured by hail and by limbs coming down in the first minute of the gust front.

**Stay off the top floor if trees can reach the house.** The most common serious injury in this hazard is
a tree through a roof onto a bedroom, and the interior lowest room solves it as well as it solves the
wind.

**If you are driving:** get off the road, ideally under structural cover, away from trees and power
lines. If you are caught in large hail in the open, stop, stay in the vehicle, turn away from the
windscreen and cover your head and the children's — side windows break first, and the windscreen is
laminated and will craze rather than shatter.

**When the power goes out**, the outage playbook starts (Playbook 17): fridge and freezer stay shut,
generator goes outdoors only, and the CO rule has no exceptions.

## After

**The safety pass first.** Downed lines are live. Trees that are still standing may be unstable, and a
leaning tree with a lifted root plate is a serious hazard that will come down without more wind. Do not
walk under one and do not park under one.

**Chainsaws are the injury story of this hazard.** In the week after a derecho, emergency departments
fill with people who have never used a saw doing storm work with one. If you are not genuinely
competent — chaps, helmet, eye and ear protection, and experience of a bound-up limb under tension — the
tree waits for someone who is. Nothing about a fallen tree is urgent enough to justify losing a leg, and
nobody should be up a ladder with a saw at all.

**Then the damage documentation, which in this hazard is the money.**

Photograph the whole property from the ground, all four elevations, then the vehicles, then anything
punctured or dented. Look for and photograph the **soft indicators** that establish a hail event even
where the roof looks fine from below: dents on gutters, downspouts, vents, flashing, air conditioner
condenser fins, mailboxes, garden furniture and the car. Those metal surfaces are your evidence that hail
of a damaging size fell here, and they are how a legitimate roof claim gets substantiated when the
shingle damage is subtle.

Do not go on the roof yourself. Hail-damaged shingles are brittle and walking them causes damage that
complicates the claim, quite apart from falling off.

**Then mitigate and keep the receipts:** tarp anything open, board broken glass, get water out of the
building. This is required of you and it is reimbursable (Protection 105).

**File promptly**, because hail and wind claims frequently carry a shorter reporting window than other
perils, and because a claim filed months later invites the argument that the damage came from a
different storm.

**The contractor problem is at its worst in this hazard.** Storm-chasing roofing crews follow hail
swathes across states, and the pattern is consistent: an unsolicited door knock, an offer to "handle the
insurance", pressure to sign the same day, and a document that assigns your claim benefits to them.
Some of it is legitimate business and some is straightforwardly fraud. The rules that protect you are
simple — get an independent inspection, never sign an assignment of benefits without understanding it,
never let anyone else create damage or exaggerate a claim on your behalf, use a licensed local contractor
with a verifiable address, and never pay in full up front.

**Then the re-roof decision.** If you are replacing the roof anyway, this is the moment to take
Protections 69, 70 and 71 together — sealed deck, ring-shank nails, impact-rated covering — because the
marginal cost during a replacement is small and it buys a documented premium discount and a materially
lower chance of doing this again.
