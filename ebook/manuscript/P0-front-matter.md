## How to use this book

This book is organised around **130 numbered protections**. Each one is a specific thing you can do to your
house, your household or your paperwork, and each one is numbered permanently so the rest of the book can
point at it. When a hazard chapter says *Protection 74*, it means the garage door, and it means it every
time.

Every protection tells you the same seven things: what it prevents, what it costs, how long it takes, what
you need to buy, how to do it, **what it does not do**, and which regions put it in their first ten.

**That sixth field is the one to trust.** Every protection in this book has a paragraph explaining its
limits, because a book that tells you a purchase makes you safe has sold you something rather than
helped you. A helmet does not make a mobile home survivable. A water filter does not remove arsenic. A
generator kills you indoors. Those sentences are the reason to believe the rest.

**The seven parts, and what each is for:**

**Part One — Why people die.** Six mechanisms and one timeline. Read this first even if you skip everything
else, because it is what makes the rest coherent: almost everybody who dies in a disaster dies from one of
six things, and almost every fatal decision is made in one of six phases.

**Part Two — The core six.** Water, food, body temperature, power, air and sanitation, medical. These are
the protections that pay out in every hazard, which makes them the best value in the book.

**Part Three — The 23 hazard playbooks.** Read the two or three that apply to where you live. Each opens with
a real event, then tells you what actually kills, then what to do before, during and after. Every playbook
ends with a one-page version you can find under stress.

**Part Four — People and places.** The chapter that adapts the plan to your actual household: children, older
adults, disability and medical dependency, animals — and apartments, manufactured homes, cars, and being at
work when it happens.

**Part Five — Warning literacy, documents and money.** How to read what the weather service is actually
telling you, and how to not lose the recovery after surviving the event.

**Part Six — The Binder.** Fillable pages. Print them or write in them. A plan in your head is not a plan.

**Part Seven — The 60 days.** If you want to be told what to do in what order, start here and work backwards
into the reference. Eight weeks of short evenings.

**Then the appendix**, which is a compact directory of the agencies and programmes in your state.

**Three ways to read this book, and all of them are correct.**

*Cover to cover*, if that is how you read. It is written to work that way.

*Sixty days*, if you want a schedule: open Part Seven and follow it, dipping back into the protections it
names.

*Or fix the thing that worries you tonight*: find your hazard in Part Three, read the one-page version at the
end of it, and do the first three protections it lists. Nobody has to earn the right to be useful to their
household by reading four hundred pages first.

## The fifteen-minute start

If you put this book down after this page, do these six things. They cost nothing, they take about fifteen
minutes, and they are genuinely the highest-value fifteen minutes available to you.

**1. Turn on wireless emergency alerts** on every phone in the household, and confirm they are on rather than
assuming. *(Protection 85)*

**2. Enrol in your county's alert system.** Search your county's name and "emergency alerts." *(Protection
86)*

**3. Put a torch and a pair of sturdy shoes under every bed.** The floor after a tornado, an earthquake or a
window failure is glass and nails, and the lights are out. *(Protection 43)*

**4. Film your home inventory.** Twenty minutes with a phone, narrating, every room, every drawer opened. Then
get it off the phone tonight. In a total loss this is the only evidence that will exist, and it is rank ten in
every region in this book. *(Protection 100)*

**5. Name your out-of-area contact** — one person, well outside your region, whom everybody calls or texts if
separated. Everyone memorises the number, including the children. *(Protection 112)*

**6. Write one sentence.** Pick the hazard most likely where you live and finish this sentence: *when ___
happens, we ___, without further discussion.* Then say it out loud to whoever is in the room. *(Protection
95)*

That last one is the most important thing in this book. It is on this page because of what happened in
Joplin, Missouri on 22 May 2011. The sirens sounded at 5:11 in the afternoon. The tornado warning was
issued at 5:17. The tornado touched down at 5:34. The federal service assessment counted 158 people
killed directly and four more indirectly — and it found that the dominant behaviour was not disbelief or
panic but **looking for a second opinion.** Going to a window. Calling somebody. Checking a screen. The
assessment's own words: most residents "did not take protective action until receiving and processing
additional, credible confirmation of the threat."

Seventeen minutes was enough time. It was not enough time to spend most of it confirming.

A written trigger is the only reliable defence against that, and it costs one sentence.

## Find your region

**Regions in this book set priority, not access.** Every reader gets every chapter, because hazards are not
mutually exclusive — Tornado Alley floods, the Hurricane Coast has heat waves, and everybody loses power.
What your region changes is **the order of the first ten protections**, which is the difference between a
plan and a wish list.

heat and drought within a single decade. If your first ten looks wrong for your street, trust your street.

## Disclaimer, and what this book is not

**This book is general information, not professional advice.** It is not medical advice, not legal advice, not
engineering advice, not financial or insurance advice, and it is not a substitute for the instruction of the
emergency authorities where you live.

**Official instruction always wins.** If your county, your state, the National Weather Service or the person
knocking on your door tells you something that contradicts a page in this book, do what they say. They have
information about your specific situation that no book can have. Every playbook here is written to make you
better at understanding and acting on official instruction — never to replace it.

**Anything structural needs a professional.** The protections involving roofs, foundations, framing,
electrical panels, gas piping, transfer switches and flood openings are described here so you know what to
ask for, what it is called, and why it matters. Several of them require permits, and several require
licensed trades by law. Getting it wrong on a gas connection or a transfer switch injures people.

**Anything medical needs a clinician.** The medical protections are the interventions taught in public
first-aid courses, and the strong recommendation of this book is that you take one. The chapters on
medication, dialysis, oxygen and refrigerated drugs are prompts for conversations with your own clinician and
pharmacist, whose answers about you supersede anything written here.

**Insurance varies by policy, by state and by year.** Every figure and every rule in this book about coverage
is a prompt to read your own declarations page and to call your own agent. The specific numbers on your
policy are the only ones that will pay you.

**Costs are estimates from the time of writing** and they move. Treat them as bands for planning, not quotes.

**And this book does not cover self-defence, firearms or civil disorder.** That is a deliberate choice.
Almost every competitor title includes it; this one does not, because the evidence base for the protections
in this book is a set of government post-event assessments, and there is no comparable evidence base for the
other thing. This book is about nature, structures, water, power and paperwork.

**Nobody in this book is a survivor whose words were invented.** Every quoted account is drawn from a
published source and attributed. There are no composite characters, no illustrative anecdotes presented as
real events, and no fabricated names.

**Nothing here is endorsed by any agency.** This book cites public documents from federal, state and local
agencies extensively, because they are the best available evidence and because they are public. Citation is
not endorsement, and no agency named in these pages has reviewed, approved or is associated with this book.

## About the sources

The claims in this book come, wherever possible, from **primary documents**: National Weather Service service
assessments, National Hurricane Center tropical cyclone reports, USGS hazard assessments, NIST investigation
reports, NTSB investigations, CDC and EPA guidance, federal register notices, state statutes, and peer-
reviewed research.

That preference is deliberate and it costs something. It means this book sometimes tells you that a number
you have seen elsewhere is not supportable — for example, the premium-discount percentages that appear
everywhere on the internet for wind-mitigation upgrades turn out, on inspection, to come from the marketing
material of companies selling roofs, not from any insurer's filed rate schedule. So this book does not quote
one. It tells you to call your carrier and ask for their table, because that document exists and is
authoritative and is yours to request.

Where a figure could not be sourced to a primary document, it is not in this book. Where a subject is
genuinely disputed among the people who study it — the effects of a high-altitude electromagnetic pulse on
small consumer electronics is the clearest example — this book says so, rather than picking whichever answer
makes a better chapter.

**Full citations are at the back**, by chapter. Images and illustrations are original diagrams or public
domain material from US government sources, credited individually in the credits pages. Nothing in this book
is stock photography of a disaster.

## One more thing before you start

This book contains a great deal about how people die, because that is what makes the protections make sense.
It is not written to frighten you and there is nothing in it about collapse, chaos or the end of anything.

The evidence is genuinely reassuring, and it is worth saying plainly at the front: **the people who come
through these events well are overwhelmingly ordinary households who did a small number of unglamorous
things in advance.** Stored water. Wrote a trigger down. Bought helmets. Filmed the house. Knew where the
gas shut-off was. Had a neighbour's phone number. Left early.

None of it is heroic. All of it is available to you. And most of the highest-value items in these four
hundred pages are free.

Start with the fifteen minutes.
