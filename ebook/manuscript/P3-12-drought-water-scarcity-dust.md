# Part Three, Playbook 12 — Drought, Water Scarcity and Dust

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

Drought is the only hazard in this book with no moment. There is no siren, no landfall, no minute at
which it begins. It is a slow subtraction, and the households it ruins are the ones who did not notice the
subtraction had reached them.

The Colorado River basin spent more than two decades in a drought severe enough to take its two largest
reservoirs to levels that triggered federal shortage declarations and mandatory cuts across states
supplying tens of millions of people.
«CITE: Bureau of Reclamation records for Lake Mead/Lake Powell elevations and the tier declarations issued,
with dates.»

At the household scale, the version that matters is smaller and more brutal: in the California droughts
of the last decade, thousands of private domestic wells went dry, one house at a time, in communities where
the water table dropped below people's pump intakes.
«CITE: California state dry-well reporting data — number of household wells reported dry in a given
drought period.»

That is the household event this playbook is about. Not a reservoir statistic — **your tap stops, and the
fix costs tens of thousands of dollars and takes months because every driller in the county is booked.**

---

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Drought itself rarely kills directly** in the United States, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.
Its harm is transmitted through four other things:

**Wildfire**, which drought loads the gun for — dry fuels, dry soil, longer season. Playbook 5.

**Extreme heat**, which drought amplifies: dry ground heats the air more efficiently than moist ground, so
drought and heat wave reinforce each other. Playbook 10.

**Water quality**, which degrades as supplies shrink — concentrated contaminants, algal blooms in warm
low reservoirs producing toxins that boiling does not remove, saltwater intrusion in coastal aquifers, and
higher nitrate and arsenic concentrations in falling groundwater.

**Dust**, which is the acute health hazard here. Blowing dust from dried agricultural land carries
particulates, and in the American Southwest it carries the fungal spores that cause coccidioidomycosis —
Valley fever — a genuine and increasing illness that follows dust exposure and disturbance of dry soil.
CDC's numbers: **around 20,000 cases reported in the United States each year, most of them in
California and Arizona** — and CDC states plainly that the disease is underdiagnosed, putting the
estimated true burden at **206,000 to 360,000 cases a year, ten to eighteen times the reported
figure.** Reported cases rose from 2,271 in 1998 to the tens of thousands, so this is a hazard that is
growing rather than a historical curiosity.
«SRC: CDC, "Reported Cases of Valley Fever" (coccidioidomycosis surveillance). |
https://www.cdc.gov/valley-fever/php/statistics/index.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»

A **haboob** is the acute version: a wall of dust driven ahead of a collapsing thunderstorm, arriving in
minutes, reducing visibility on a highway to nothing. The deaths are traffic deaths, in multi-vehicle
pile-ups, and they are the single most preventable fatality in this playbook.

**Costs:** a dry well and a deeper replacement, which is the largest single household number in this
chapter. Foundation damage from soil shrinkage, which in expansive clay soils is a serious and widely
under-recognised cost and is generally excluded from homeowner policies. Dead landscaping and trees, which
then become fuel and falling hazards. Water restriction penalties. Crop and livestock losses for anyone
whose income touches agriculture.

## Before

**If you are on a private well** — and this is the reader this playbook is written for:

- **9 — the well owner's plan.** Know your well's depth, your pump's setting depth, and your static water
  level. Those three numbers tell you how much margin you have before the pump breaks suction, and almost
  no well owner knows them. Get them from the well log, which is usually a public record, or measure them.
- Watch for the warning signs, because a well gives notice: sediment or cloudiness that was not there
  before, air spitting from taps, the pump running longer or cycling oddly, reduced pressure, and a
  recovery that takes longer after heavy use. Any of those means the water level is approaching the intake.
- Have the conversation with a driller **before** you need one. Lowering a pump is far cheaper than
  deepening or re-drilling, and it is only possible if the borehole has depth below the pump. Knowing which
  situation you are in changes what you should be saving for.
- **10 — cistern and pressure tank basics.** A storage tank plus a delivery arrangement is the interim
  solution when a well fails, and having costed it in advance is the difference between three days and
  three weeks without water.

**If you are on a municipal supply:**

- Understand your utility's restriction stages and what each one prohibits, because the penalties are real
  and the stages escalate on a schedule you can read now.
- Know where your water comes from — reservoir, river, or groundwater — because that determines what
  happens to it in a long drought and whether taste, hardness and treatment changes are coming.

**For every household in a drought-prone region:**

- **1, 2 — the water store and its rotation**, which in this hazard is not for an outage but for an
  interruption or a do-not-drink notice.
- **6, 7 — filtration and chemical disinfection**, with the honest caveat that matters here: a filter
  rated for microbes does nothing for nitrate, arsenic or the toxins from an algal bloom, and boiling
  **concentrates** those rather than removing them. Know what your filter actually addresses.
- **11 — grey-water reuse**, done lawfully. Bath and laundry water on the garden is legal in some places
  and regulated in others; look it up rather than assuming.
- **Fix the leaks**, which is the least glamorous and highest-return water action: a running toilet
  wastes more than any behavioural change you can make, and finding it is a meter reading with everything
  off.
- **Replace the landscape rather than watering it.** Drought-tolerant planting, mulch, drip irrigation
  instead of sprinklers, and a shift to watering deeply and rarely. This also serves Protection 77,
  because a dead lawn and dry shrubs against the house are wildfire fuel.
- **Water the foundation, not the lawn**, if you are on expansive clay. Soil that shrinks away from a
  foundation causes movement and cracking, and maintaining consistent moisture around the perimeter during
  a long drought is genuinely cheaper than the repair. Watch for new diagonal cracks at door and window
  corners, doors that stop closing, and gaps opening between skirting and floor.
- **52 — N95 supply** for dust, and **49 — HVAC filtration**, which serve this hazard as well as smoke.

## During

**Drought, which is the slow part.**

Comply with the restrictions, and understand that the water you save early is what prevents the harsher
stage later. Prioritise in the order the restrictions themselves imply: drinking and cooking, hygiene,
then trees, then everything else. **Trees before lawn** is the right call — a lawn recovers in a season and
a mature shade tree does not, and that shade is worth real money in the heat playbook.

Watch your own well's behaviour rather than the reservoir's, and act at the first signs rather than at the
failure. A well that is spitting air on Monday can be dry on Friday.

**If your well does fail:** stop running the pump, because running it dry destroys it and adds a
replacement to your bill. Then arrange water — bottled for drinking, a delivery and a temporary tank for
the rest — and get on a driller's list immediately. Several states have emergency assistance programmes for
household water supply, and county emergency management is the place to ask.

**A do-not-drink or do-not-boil notice is different from a boil notice** and this distinction matters in a
drought: boiling handles microbes, and it concentrates chemicals and does nothing for cyanotoxins from an
algal bloom. If the notice says do not drink, bottled water is the only answer and that includes for
brushing teeth, making ice and preparing food. Protection 8 covers the difference.

**Dust, which is the fast part.**

A **dust storm warning** means visibility is about to collapse. If you are indoors, close everything, run
the HVAC on recirculate, and put the box-fan filter on if the air is bad.

**If you are driving into blowing dust, the guidance is specific and it is the opposite of what people
instinctively do.** Get off the road as far as you can, **switch the lights off** including the hazards,
take your foot off the brake so the brake lights are dark, put it in park and stay put with the seatbelt
on. The reason is the fatality mechanism: drivers ahead stop in the dust, drivers behind see a tail light,
steer toward it because it looks like the road, and drive into them. A dark vehicle off the pavement does
not attract a collision. Do not stop in a travel lane. Do not try to drive through at reduced speed.
«CITE: the Arizona DOT / NWS "Pull Aside, Stay Alive" guidance, including the lights-off instruction.»

**And N95s during and after a dust event**, particularly if you are moving or disturbing dry soil, because
that is the Valley fever exposure. People at higher risk of severe disease — the immunocompromised,
pregnant people, and several groups with elevated risk — should avoid dust-generating work in endemic areas
altogether.

## After

**Dust settles indoors and gets re-suspended.** Clean wet, not dry, and change the filters, exactly as in
the smoke playbook.

**Watch for the illness that arrives late.** Valley fever presents weeks after exposure and looks like
persistent pneumonia — cough, fever, fatigue, chest pain, weight loss — and it is routinely missed. If you
were in a dust event in an endemic region and you have a respiratory illness that will not resolve, say so
to the clinician and ask directly, because it changes the test they order.

**After a drought breaks, the first rain is a hazard rather than a relief.** Hard, dry, hydrophobic ground
sheds water instead of absorbing it, which produces flash flooding and, in burned areas, debris flows.
Playbooks 3 and 5 apply, and the transition period is when people are least expecting them.

**Check the foundation and the trees.** Soil re-wetting after a long drought moves foundations again, in
the opposite direction. Trees that were drought-stressed die over the following year or two and become
falling hazards and fuel — get them assessed rather than assuming they recovered because they leafed out.

**Re-test the well** after any drought in which the level dropped substantially, because concentration
changes and drawing from a different part of the aquifer can change what is in the water. And re-test after
it recovers, because a rising table can bring surface contamination with it.

**Then reconsider the landscape and the storage**, which is the real after-action here. Drought recurs on
a decadal rhythm in the American West, and the households who do well in the next one are the ones who
used the wet years to change the planting, fix the leaks, add the storage tank and get the well's numbers
written down.
