## Electronics, and the rare events

Two topics live in this chapter, and they are together because they share a failure mode: both attract
a great deal of confident nonsense, and both are places where a reader can waste money buying the wrong
thing while leaving the real exposure untouched.

The electronics half is ordinary and useful — keeping your ability to communicate, navigate and pay
alive through an outage. The rare-events half is surge, lightning, geomagnetic disturbance, EMP,
radiological proximity and household hazmat. It is deliberately the shortest section in the book.

The honest framing, stated once so it governs everything below: **the probability-weighted risk here is
dominated by lightning and utility surge, not by EMP or a reactor.** A household that buys a whole-home
surge protector and a small shielded box has spent its money in the right order. A household that buys
a large Faraday cage and no surge protection has bought the story instead of the protection.

### 117 · Charge, in layers

**Prevents:** losing the phone, which is your radio, map, torch, camera and bank.
**Cost:** $30–150 · **Time:** an hour · **Materials:** battery packs, a car adapter
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 6 Hurricane Coast.

Three layers, in the order you should buy them.

**Battery packs**, enough for two to three full phone charges per person, kept charged — check them
quarterly, because a pack left in a drawer for two years is frequently at zero and sometimes swollen.

**A vehicle charger** with more than one port, because your car is a generator you already own. Run the
engine to charge rather than draining the starter battery, and do it outdoors.

**A small solar panel** with a USB output, 20 watts or more, for an outage measured in days rather than
hours. Test it before you need it — the cheap ones frequently do not deliver anything like their rated
output, and you want to find that out in your garden rather than on day four.

The habit that matters more than any of the hardware: **when a watch is issued, charge everything.**
Phones, packs, laptops, torches, the radio, the power tool batteries. It costs nothing and it is the
difference between starting an outage at a hundred percent and at nineteen.

**What this does not do:** none of this powers a house. It powers phones, radios, torches and a laptop.
Refrigeration and medical equipment are Protections 39 and 45.

### 118 · Offline maps, documents and reference

**Prevents:** a phone that needs the internet to be useful.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Materials:** storage space
· **Regions:** all five.

Download your region's maps for offline use in whichever navigation app you use, at a radius that covers
both evacuation routes and both destinations. Do it before the season, not during the warning, because
you are downloading a large file on a congested network.

Then put on the phone, as files rather than links: the document vault index, the medication list, the
insurance declarations page, the household contact sheet, the two route descriptions, and the
shut-down and return checklists. Anything that lives only in a cloud folder is gone the moment the
signal is.

**What this does not do:** offline maps do not know about closures, and a downloaded checklist does not
replace the paper copy in the document envelope. Assume the phone dies and the paper is the fallback.

### 119 · A radio that is not a phone

**Prevents:** being informed only for as long as the cellular network survives.
**Cost:** $30–60 · **Time:** 20 minutes to set up · **Materials:** a NOAA weather radio
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 2 Tornado Alley.

Covered as Protection 87 for its alerting function; it appears here as infrastructure. A weather radio
with battery backup and SAME programming receives directly from the transmitter and does not care
whether the towers, the internet or the power are working.

Add a broadcast radio — AM/FM, hand-crank or battery — because local AM stations carry emergency
information during regional events and frequently stay on the air when everything else is down.

Keep both with fresh batteries in the same place as the torches, and confirm the weather radio actually
alerts by using its test function, not by trusting the light.

**What this does not do:** a radio receives. It does not let you call for help, and it does not tell you
about anything the transmitter is not broadcasting.

### 120 · Unplug on warning

**Prevents:** the loss that a device suffers because it was connected.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** five minutes · **Materials:** none
· **Regions:** all five.

The free companion to Protection 46, and it works: when a severe thunderstorm warning is issued, unplug
what you can spare — the desktop, the television, the games console, the printer, the things that are
expensive and not needed for the next hour.

Leave in what you need: the fridge and freezer, the router while it still has service, the medical
equipment, and anything on a hardwired circuit you cannot reach.

Sequence matters for the restoration case as well. **After a long outage, leave sensitive equipment
unplugged for a few minutes after power returns**, because restoration is frequently dirty — voltage
swings and repeated brief cycling as the utility brings sections back.

**What this does not do:** unplugging protects the item, not the wiring. And it depends entirely on
having a warning, which is why the installed device in Protection 46 exists.

### 121 · Short-range radios, for talking to each other

**Prevents:** a household that cannot coordinate across two miles.
**Cost:** $30–70 a pair · **Time:** an hour · **Materials:** two or more FRS/GMRS handsets
· **Regions:** all five, at moderate priority.

Protection 112 solves the long-distance problem with an out-of-area contact. This solves the local one,
which the cellular network is worst at: two adults, a mile apart, one at the house and one at the
school, with the towers congested or down.

A pair of FRS handsets needs no licence, works line-of-sight over roughly half a mile to two miles in
built-up terrain — considerably less than the packaging claims — and costs less than a tank of fuel.
GMRS gives more power and better range, requires a licence for the higher-power radios in the United
States, and that licence covers a whole family.
«CITE: FCC rules for FRS versus GMRS — the current power limits, and the family-wide scope of the GMRS
licence.»

Two habits make them useful rather than decorative. **Agree a channel and a check-in time in advance**
and write both on the form in the binder, because a radio nobody has tuned to the same channel is two
paperweights. And **charge or fit fresh batteries when a watch is issued**, alongside everything else in
Protection 117.

Add a scanner or an AM radio if you want to hear what responders and local stations are saying, but
understand that listening and talking are separate functions.

**What this does not do:** these are not a way to call for help — nobody official is monitoring your
channel. They coordinate your own household. Amateur radio is a genuinely capable step beyond this and
it needs a licence and real study; it is out of scope for this book.

### 122 · The shielded box, done proportionately

**Prevents:** losing a spare communication set to a high-altitude EMP or a severe geomagnetic event.
**Cost:** $0–60 · **Time:** an hour · **Materials:** a metal container, insulating material
· **Regions:** all five, at low priority. Not in any region's top ten.

Placed here honestly: this is a small, cheap, low-probability protection that is worth doing precisely
because it is small and cheap. It is not worth spending hundreds on, and it is not worth worrying
about.

**What it is.** A conductive enclosure surrounds its contents and diverts an incoming electromagnetic
pulse around them rather than through them. A steel ammunition box, a paint tin, or a galvanised bin
with a tight-fitting lid all work, provided the metal makes continuous contact all the way around the
seam — the lid overlapping the body is the part that matters, and a plastic-lidded container is not a
shielded box.

**How to build one.** Line the inside completely with cardboard, foam or bubble wrap so that nothing
inside touches the metal. Put the contents in, close it so the lid seats metal-to-metal all round, and
store it somewhere dry. That is the whole build. A second layer — a wrapped item inside a lined box —
adds margin at no real cost.

**What goes in it.** A spare cheap phone, a spare battery pack, a spare weather radio, a spare USB
drive holding the document vault, a spare torch, and a set of spare batteries in their packaging. All
of it powered off, because a device does not have to be plugged in to be damaged.

**The honest scale of the threat.** Two distinct things get conflated. A **high-altitude nuclear EMP**
is a deliberate act of war whose effect on unconnected consumer electronics is genuinely disputed among
people who study it, and whose real damage is to the grid rather than to your phone. A **severe
geomagnetic storm** is a natural event with a real historical precedent, and it damages long
conductors — transmission lines, transformers, pipelines — not the handset in your pocket.
«CITE: the Carrington event, and the 1989 Hydro-Québec geomagnetic disturbance, with dates and the
documented grid effect.»
«CITE: a government or standards-body assessment of HEMP effects on small unconnected consumer
electronics — needed to keep the disputed framing accurate rather than dismissive.»

In both cases, **the thing that actually reaches you is a very long power outage**, which is Protections
37 to 46, plus water, food and heat. The box is a cheap hedge on top of that. If you have not done the
outage protections yet, do not build the box.

**What this does not do:** a shielded box does nothing for the grid, does not power anything, and does
not protect what is outside it. It preserves a spare kit. Treat any product marketing itself as
EMP-proof at a high price with suspicion.

### 123 · A backup of your life, off-site

**Prevents:** losing twenty years of photographs to an event you survived.
**Cost:** $0–120/yr · **Time:** an hour to set up · **Materials:** cloud storage or two drives
· **Regions:** all five.

The same three-two-one rule as Protection 96, applied to everything else: photographs, records, work,
the things nobody can reissue.

Encrypted cloud backup running automatically is the easiest version, because a backup that requires you
to remember it does not happen. Add a physical drive that lives somewhere else — an office, a relative's
house — and swap it occasionally.

Then the step everyone skips: **restore something.** Pull one file back and open it. An untested backup
is a belief, not a backup, and silently broken backups are extremely common.

**What this does not do:** a backup does not protect the device. It protects the contents, which is the
irreplaceable part.

### 124 · Lightning, for people

**Prevents:** the deaths that happen outdoors in ordinary summer weather.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** reading, once · **Materials:** none
· **Regions:** all five.

Lightning kills a small number of people every year and injures many more, and almost all of it happens
to people outdoors who had time to go inside.
«CITE: NWS annual lightning fatality figures, and the share occurring during recreational activity.»

**When thunder roars, go indoors.** If you can hear thunder, you are within range of the next strike;
there is no safe distance while thunder is audible. Wait thirty minutes after the last thunder before
going back out.

Safe means a substantial building or a hard-topped vehicle with the windows up. Not a porch, not a
shelter with open sides, not a tent, not under a tree, which is one of the most dangerous places
available.

Indoors, stay off corded phones, away from plumbing and away from anything connected to the wiring
during a close storm, because current travels the conductors.

And treat a strike victim immediately — they carry no charge, they are safe to touch, and cardiac arrest
is the usual cause of death, which makes immediate CPR the intervention.

**What this does not do:** nothing outdoors is safe. The crouch you may have been taught is a last
resort for someone with no option, not a technique, and it is no longer recommended as advice.

### 125 · Knowing your radiological proximity

**Prevents:** finding out during the event that you are inside a planning zone.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **Materials:** a map
· **Regions:** households near a nuclear plant.

Find out whether you live within a nuclear plant's emergency planning zone, and if so, which one — the
inner zone concerns direct exposure and the outer, much larger zone concerns the food and water supply.
«CITE: NRC/FEMA emergency planning zone definitions and the current radii for the plume exposure
pathway and ingestion pathway zones.»

If you are inside one, your local emergency management office publishes a specific plan with specific
routes and specific reception centres, and you should have read it once. Households in the zone are
typically included in a notification scheme and sometimes a potassium iodide distribution programme,
which is the next protection.

**Then do the same lookup for the industrial hazards, which affect far more households than reactors
do.** A rail line carrying tank cars, a hazardous-materials truck route, a pipeline, a refinery, a
chemical plant, a large fuel depot, a water treatment works holding chlorine — any of these within a
mile or two of you makes Protection 126 a live protection rather than a theoretical one. Rail and
pipeline routes are mapped publicly, and your local emergency management office maintains the hazard
analysis that names the specific facilities and substances near you. Read it once and note which way
the prevailing wind blows relative to your house, because that single fact decides whether you shelter
or leave.
«CITE: the public federal pipeline mapping system, and the state or local hazard-analysis document
type that lists nearby facilities and substances.»

If neither applies to you, this protection is complete and you can stop.

**What this does not do:** knowing the zone is not a plan for a reactor accident. That plan is written
by the operator and the state, and in an actual event you follow their instruction, which will be
specific and local.

### 126 · Shelter-in-place for a plume

**Prevents:** breathing the thing you could have kept out.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 15 minutes to prepare a room · **Materials:** plastic sheeting, tape
· **Regions:** near a plant; also chemical-corridor and hazmat-route households.

The same protocol serves a radiological plume, a chemical release and heavy wildfire smoke, which is
why it is worth knowing even far from a reactor: **go in, stay in, tune in.**

Go inside, to an interior room, high up if the hazard is heavier-than-air and low down if the guidance
says otherwise — which is why the tuning-in part is not optional. Shut all windows and doors. Turn off
anything that moves outside air in: air conditioning, ventilation, extract fans, and close the
fireplace damper. Seal the room's door edges and any vents with plastic sheeting and tape if you have
been told to.

Then listen, on the radio, and stay put until officially told otherwise. Distance, shielding and time
are the three things that reduce dose, and a sealed interior room provides two of them.

Do not evacuate into a plume. This is the case where leaving is wrong unless you are specifically
directed to leave, because a car is not sealed and the road may run downwind.

**If you are outdoors when it happens, or a responder at the door tells you to leave on foot, the rule
is upwind and uphill.** Move across the wind rather than with it, and gain height, because almost every
industrial gas and vapour of concern is heavier than air and pools in low ground — basements, ditches,
underpasses, valley floors. Cover your nose and mouth with cloth, which does very little but is better
than nothing, and keep moving until you are out of the low ground and clear of the plume's line. Then
find a building. Do not go back for anything, and do not go back down.

**What this does not do:** taping a room is not a filtered shelter, and it is not indefinite — a sealed
room accumulates carbon dioxide, which is why these instructions are always time-bounded and why you
follow the official all-clear rather than your own judgment.

### 127 · Potassium iodide, and its limits

**Prevents:** thyroid uptake of radioactive iodine, and nothing else.
**Cost:** $10–15 · **Time:** an hour of reading · **Materials:** KI tablets, if in a zone
· **Regions:** near a plant only.

Potassium iodide saturates the thyroid so it cannot take up radioactive iodine. That is its entire
function, it does not protect any other organ, and it does not protect against any other radioactive
material.
«CITE: FDA guidance on KI — dosing by age group, and the explicit statement of what it does and does
not protect.»

**Timing decides whether it works at all.** It is effective in a narrow window around exposure, and
taking it a day late achieves close to nothing.

**It is taken when public health officials say to take it, not when you feel worried.** Dosing differs
by age, it matters most for children and pregnant people, and there are real contraindications —
thyroid conditions and iodine allergy among them.

If you live in a planning zone, your state may distribute it free. Get it, read the dosing on the
packet, note the expiry, and put it with the medication supply.

**What this does not do:** KI is not a radiation antidote and is not protective for adults over forty in
the way people assume. It is a single narrow intervention that is worth having if you are in the zone
and worth ignoring if you are not.

### 128 · Household chemicals, stored so a flood does not make them a hazard

**Prevents:** turning your own garage into a contamination event.
**Cost:** $0–40 · **Time:** an hour · **Materials:** shelving, a bin
· **Regions:** flood-exposed households, all five.

Walk the garage, the shed and the basement and look at what is on the floor: petrol, paint, solvents,
pool chemicals, pesticides, fertiliser, drain cleaner, propane cylinders, motor oil.

Then fix three things. **Get it off the floor** — a shelf above your expected flood level costs nothing
and keeps containers out of the water. **Separate what should not meet**: pool chemicals away from
fuels, oxidisers away from anything combustible, and nothing stored above or beside the water heater or
the furnace. **Get rid of what you do not use**, at a household hazardous waste collection rather than
in the bin or the drain.

Propane cylinders live outdoors, upright, secured, never in a basement or a closed garage.

For the fire case, this is the same protection viewed from the other side: a garage full of solvents on
the floor is the reason a wildfire ember that reaches your garage is a total loss rather than a repair.

**What this does not do:** shelving does not contain a spill. If floodwater has been in contact with
stored chemicals, treat the water and the residue as contaminated, and do not pressure-wash it into the
garden.

### 129 · The gas and carbon monoxide response

**Prevents:** the explosion and the poisoning, which are the two utility deaths after an event.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **Materials:** a wrench, a labelled valve
· **Regions:** Quake West rank 6, all five.

Know where the gas meter is, know which way the valve turns, and keep the right wrench attached to it —
not in a toolbox in the basement.

Then know the rule, because the default is not to touch it. **Shut the gas off only if you smell gas,
hear it, see damage to the line, or are told to.** Otherwise leave it alone: a gas supply shut at the
meter usually requires the utility to relight and recommission appliances, and after a regional event
that wait is measured in weeks.

If you do smell gas: **do not switch anything on or off**, do not use a phone inside, do not light
anything. Get everyone out, shut the valve if you can reach it safely from outside, and call from a
distance.

Carbon monoxide is the other half. Alarms on every level, tested, and the absolute rule from Protection
41 restated because it kills more people than the events themselves in a cold outage: nothing that
burns fuel runs indoors — no generator, no charcoal, no camp stove, no unvented heater, no vehicle in an
attached garage. If the alarm sounds, everyone goes outside and stays outside until someone with an
instrument says otherwise.

**What this does not do:** shutting the gas does not make a damaged building safe, and a CO alarm does
not detect natural gas or propane. They are different sensors for different gases and you need both.

### 130 · The annual review

**Prevents:** a plan that was true two years ago.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** two hours a year · **Materials:** the binder
· **Regions:** all five. The last protection because it protects every other one.

Preparedness decays. Batteries flatten, food expires, medication supplies get raided, children outgrow
the clothes in the go-bag, the out-of-area contact moves, the policy renews with different terms, the
neighbour with the chainsaw sells up, the new roof changes what credits you qualify for, and the phone
holding the offline maps gets replaced.

So one date a year, in the calendar, before your hazard season. Sit down with the binder and go through
it:

Rotate the water and food. Replace the batteries and test every torch, alarm and radio. Refresh the
medication and check every expiry, including the KI if you have it. Repack the go-bags for the season and
the current size of the children. Redo the video home inventory. Reread the declarations page and
confirm the five numbers. Re-drive one evacuation route. Reconfirm the out-of-area contact and the three
destinations. Retest one backup restore. Walk the outside of the house and look at the roof, the vents,
the gutters, the grading and Zone 0. Practise one thing with the household — the tornado drill, the fire
exit, the trigger conversation.

Then update the binder and write the date on the front page.

**What this does not do:** a review is not a rebuild. It catches decay, not gaps. Once every few years,
reread Part One and ask whether your exposure has changed — a new house, a new child, a new diagnosis,
a new region — because that is when the plan needs rewriting rather than refreshing.
