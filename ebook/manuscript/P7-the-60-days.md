# Part Seven — The 60 Days

Target: 22 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## How this works

Sixty days. Eight weeks of weekdays at fifteen to forty minutes each, and weekends for anything that needs a
shop, a ladder or a second person.

**The sequence carries forty-five of the one hundred and thirty protections** — the highest-return ones. The
other eighty-five are reference, taken as budget and interest allow. That is deliberate: a book that
schedules all one hundred and thirty is homework, and homework does not get done.

**Three rules for using it.**

**Week four is the paper week and it is placed before every week that costs money.** It is the cheapest week
in the sequence and the one that recovers the most, because documentation is what decides the size of your
recovery. If you do one week of this book, do week four.

**Skip what does not apply to you.** No basement, no sump pump. No well, no well protections. Renting, skip
the structural weekends and do the renter's version noted in each. A plan that ignores your actual house is
not a plan.

**And put the dates in a calendar now.** Not "this summer." Eight specific weekends. The single largest reason
household preparedness does not happen is that it is never scheduled.

**What it costs.** The first two weeks are almost free. The middle weeks are ordinary grocery-and-hardware
money spread out. Week eight is the only one that can be genuinely expensive, and it is region-dependent and
optional — most of it is a list to hand a contractor rather than a purchase.

## Week 1 — Free and instant

Nothing is bought this week. Everything here costs time, and this is the highest-value week per dollar in the
book because the dollar count is zero.

**Day 1 — Two alert paths (85).** Turn wireless emergency alerts on for every phone in the household, and
check they are on rather than assuming. Find one second path — a local news app with push notifications, or
your county's system, set up tomorrow.

**Day 2 — County alert enrollment (86).** Search your county's name plus "emergency alerts" and sign up.
Enroll every adult. This is the alert that tells you about local things the national system does not carry.

**Day 3 — Learn the words (88).** Watch, warning, advisory. Ten minutes with Part Five, Chapter One. Then say
out loud, to whoever is in the room, what each of the three means for your household.

**Day 4 — Write the trigger plan (95).** The most important half-hour in this book. For each hazard that
applies to you, one sentence: *when X happens, we do Y, without further discussion.* Then everyone signs it.
The Joplin finding is the reason: the failure mode is seeking confirmation, and a written trigger is the only
thing that beats it.

**Day 5 — The out-of-area contact (112).** Pick the person, ask them, and get everybody in the household to
save the number and memorize it — including the children. Then find out your school's release policy and who
is on the authorized list.

**Day 6 — Lighting audit (43).** Find every flashlight in the house, test it, and put one at every bedside with a
pair of sturdy shoes. Note what batteries you need; you will buy them in week five. And agree the no-candles
rule out loud.

**Weekend — The walk-around.** Two hours. Find and label the water main, the gas meter and the breaker panel,
and put the right wrench next to the gas meter. Walk the outside of the house and look at the gutters, the
grading, the vents and the first five feet. Then choose your safe room, your warm room and your cooling room,
and write all three on the binder's front page.

## Week 2 — Water

The week that matters most if you only do two.

**Day 8 — Do the sizing (1).** One gallon per person per day, times fourteen days, times the people, plus the
animals. Write the number down. It will be larger than you expected and that is the point.

**Day 9 — Buy the first half.** Bottled or containers, whatever fits your budget and storage this week.
Half now, half next week is fine — a partial store is not a failure.

**Day 10 — Date-mark and shelve (2).** Marker pen, date on everything, and a note in the calendar for
rotation. A store you cannot date is a store you will not trust.

**Day 11 — Write the pre-fill list (3).** Tape it inside a cabinet door: which tubs, which pots, which
bottles, in what order, and where the tub bladder is if you buy one. Fifteen minutes on a warning, and it
doubles your store.

**Day 12 — Learn the water heater draw-off (4).** Find the drain valve, understand the sequence — power or gas
off first, then a tap opened to break the vacuum, then the valve — and write it on the card. Forty to eighty
gallons already in your house.

**Day 13 — Disinfection card (7).** Buy plain unscented household bleach, and write the correct ratios on a
card taped inside the cabinet. Never recalled from memory, never the scented kind.

**Weekend — Finish the store, and the well owners' hour.** Buy the rest. If you are on a well, this is the
weekend to find your well log, write down the well depth, pump setting depth and static water level, and price
a hand pump or a generator-fed option (9).

## Week 3 — Food and the cold chain

**Day 15 — The no-cook shelf list (13).** Fourteen days of food that needs no power and no cooking, chosen
from what your household actually eats. Write the list before you shop.

**Day 16 — Shop it.** One trip. Ordinary groceries, not survival products.

**Day 17 — Thermometers in (15).** One in the fridge, one in the freezer. Learn the four-hour rule and the
safe temperatures, and write them on the door. The freezer decision is made with a number, not a nose.

**Day 18 — Fill the freezer's air space.** Water bottles or ice blocks into every gap. A full freezer holds
roughly twice as long as a half-empty one, and it costs nothing.

**Day 19 — Openers and redundancy (18).** A manual can opener that works, and a second one. Then check
everything in the pantry that needs a tool you only have one of.

**Day 20 — Special diets, infants, pets (19, 20).** Formula, allergy-safe food, pet food and pet medication.
This is the reserve that cannot be improvised, so it gets its own day.

**Weekend — Coolers and ice (22).** Work out your cooler strategy and where the nearest ice would come from.
Then eat one meal from the store, deliberately, to find out whether it is actually food you will eat.

## Week 4 — The paper week

**The cheapest week. The one that recovers the most money. Do this week even if you do nothing else.**

**Day 22 — The document vault, part one (96).** Photograph or scan every document that would be hard to
replace. The list is in Part Five, Chapter Two. An hour with a phone.

**Day 23 — The vault, part two.** Three copies, two places, one off-site. Encrypted cloud, a local drive, and
something that physically leaves the house. Tell one trusted person it exists.

**Day 24 — The grab-and-go envelope (97).** Waterproof envelope, paper copies of the essentials, and it lives
with the go-bag. Digital fails in exactly the conditions you built it for.

**Day 25 — The video home inventory (100).** Twenty minutes, narrating, every room, every drawer opened, the
garage and the loft. This is rank ten in every region and the only evidence that will exist in a total loss.
Off the phone into the vault tonight.

**Day 26 — Serial numbers and receipts (101).** Photograph the plates on anything over a few hundred dollars.
Set up an email folder for receipts.

**Day 27 — The insurance audit (102).** Get the declarations page and find the five numbers: dwelling limit,
contents limit **and whether contents are replacement cost or actual cash value**, the deductibles including
the separate wind and hail one, the exclusions, and loss of use. Write them on the binder form.

**Weekend — The phone call, and the money items.** Call your agent and ask: *if my house were destroyed
tomorrow, what would this policy not cover?* Take notes. Then get cash in small bills (98), sort out payment
redundancy (99), and if you are flood-exposed, get a flood insurance quote and start the thirty-day clock (103).
Renters: buy renter's insurance this weekend if you do not have it.

## Week 5 — Power and light

**Day 29 — The power priority list (37).** Write down what actually needs electricity in your household, in
order. Medical equipment, refrigeration, heating or cooling, water pump, communications, light. This document
decides what you buy — not the other way round.

**Day 30 — The medical device plan (42).** If anyone depends on powered equipment, this is the most important
day in the sequence. Register with the utility, get real battery run times from the supplier, and name the
facility you would go to.

**Day 31 — Buy the light (43).** Headlamps for everyone, a lantern or two, spare batteries. A headlamp is a
better purchase than almost anything at the same price.

**Day 32 — CO alarms (47).** One on every level and outside sleeping areas. **Check the expiry dates on the
ones you have** — the sensors expire and an expired alarm still chirps reassuringly.

**Day 33 — Smoke alarms (48).** Test every one. Replace batteries. Check the manufacture date; they expire
too.

**Day 34 — Battery capacity (38).** Price a power station against your priority list. Buy if it fits the
budget; if not, buy battery packs and the car charger (117, 44), which cover the first day for far less.

**Weekend — Generator or not.** If you already have one: start it, run it under load, check the oil, and
confirm where it will sit — outdoors, twenty feet out, never a garage (32). If you are buying one, this is the
weekend to price it **with a transfer switch** (39), because the switch is what prevents backfeed and what lets
you run the furnace. Then sort fuel storage properly (40) and fit whole-home surge protection (46) or at least
book the electrician.

## Week 6 — Shelter, and getting out

**Day 36 — Clear the safe room (23).** Physically empty it of anything heavy or breakable stored high, and
put the permanent contents in: water, flashlight, whistles each, shoes, helmets, a heavy blanket.

**Day 37 — Buy the helmets (25).** Bicycle or sports helmets, one per person, stored in the safe room. The
cheapest life-saving object in this book.

**Day 38 — Go-bags, part one (107).** One bag per person. Pack it. Do not write a list of what to pack.

**Day 39 — Go-bags, part two.** Children's bags in the current size, with comfort items they chose. Cards in
pockets with names and numbers. Then put the bags where they belong — by the door, or in the car during your
season.

**Day 40 — The five- and thirty-minute lists (108).** Written and taped inside a cabinet door. Every adult
gets an assigned area so two people are not carrying the same box.

**Day 41 — Pets (109).** Carriers out and made ordinary, pet bag packed, vaccination records copied, and the
three phone numbers found: pet-friendly shelter, kennel, and out-of-area contact who would take an animal.
Check the microchip registration is current.

**Weekend — Drive the routes (106).** Both of them, in daylight, noting the bottlenecks a map does not show.
Write the turn-by-turn in words and put a paper map in each car. Then name the three destinations (111) and make
the phone calls to confirm the first one is real. Manufactured-home households: this is the weekend you locate
the community shelter and confirm night access.

## Week 7 — Air and medical

**Day 43 — Filtration (49).** Check what filter your HVAC can take, buy the right one, and learn how to set it
to recirculate and where the fresh-air intake is.

**Day 44 — Build the box-fan filter (50).** An hour, one box fan and filters. The best benefit-per-dollar
build in the book, and the filters sell out in the first week of smoke.

**Day 45 — N95s (52).** A real supply, fitted and checked. Everyone tries one on so you find out now which
sizes work.

**Day 46 — The trauma kit (57).** Buy it or build it against the list in Playbook 23, put it where everyone
knows, and put a second one in the car.

**Day 47 — Book the training (58).** Stop the Bleed and CPR, two adults minimum, plus the teenagers. Booking
counts as doing this day; attending is the point. This is the highest-yield protection in the book.

**Day 48 — The prescription buffer (59).** Talk to the pharmacy about building a thirty-day buffer, look up
your state's emergency refill law, and write the medication list for every person into the document envelope.

**Weekend — Sanitation, and the medical dependencies.** Build the twin-bucket toilet (53) and buy the cover
material — the least glamorous hour in this book and the one people are most grateful for. Sort hand hygiene
without running water (55). Then, if your household includes dialysis, oxygen, infusion or refrigerated
medication, spend the rest of the weekend on Protections 60 and 61 with the clinic on the phone, because that
is the plan that actually keeps somebody alive.

## Week 8 — Structure

**This week is region-dependent, mostly costs real money, and is mostly a list rather than a task. Renters and
apartment dwellers: skip to the alternative below.**

Pick from your region's list rather than doing all of it:

**Hurricane Coast:** opening protection cut, labeled and drilled ready (73), garage door bracing (74), and
the roof list for your next re-roof — sealed deck (69), ring-shank renailing (70), hurricane straps (72).

**Tornado Alley:** garage door bracing (74), the sealed roof deck at re-roof (69), and if you have no basement,
price a rated above-ground safe room and demand documented compliance (24).

**Wildfire West:** **Zone 0** — clear everything combustible within five feet of the building, which is free
and is the highest-value protection in that region (76). Then metal mesh on every vent, gutters cleared (78),
and defensible space out to thirty feet and beyond (77).

**Ice & Snow North:** pipe insulation on every accessible run in unheated space (34), gutters cleared, attic
insulation and ventilation checked (31), and a sump pump battery backup (36).

**Quake West:** the anchoring weekend (83) — straps on tall furniture into studs, cabinet latches, the water
heater strapped top and bottom, nothing heavy above a bed. Then gas flex connectors (84).

**Hail regions:** impact-rated shingles specified for your next re-roof (71), and an arborist looking at
anything big enough to reach the house.

**Flood-exposed:** grading and gutter discharge away from the foundation (78, 79), elevated mechanicals costed
(81), and a backwater valve priced (82).

**The renter and apartment alternative for this week:** read the building's emergency plan and ask management
the questions in Part Four, Chapter Two. Count the stairs with a flashlight. Move furniture away from glass. Fit
interior window film if the lease allows. Introduce yourself to three neighbors and find out which of them is
elderly, alone or disabled. That is a genuinely full week and it is worth more than most of the list above.

**And whatever you do this week, write the contractor list** — the things to demand next time anybody is
working on your property for any reason. That list is the real deliverable of week eight.

## The close — Run the drill

**Two scenarios, timed, with everybody present. Then a conversation.**

**Drill one: the no-warning event.** Say the word at a random moment. Everyone to the safe room, with helmets,
shoes, pets and phones. Time it. **Under ninety seconds is the target.** If it is longer, find out why —
usually a door that sticks, an animal nobody can catch, or a child who did not know where to go.

**Drill two: the evacuation.** Say the word. Work the five-minute list. People, pets, bags, medication,
documents, keys, out the door, in the car. Time it. Then do the thirty-minute list on paper: does everyone know
what they are carrying and from where?

**Then the conversation, which is the actual point of the whole sixty days.** Sit down and say out loud:

Where do we meet if we cannot get home? Where do we meet if the area is evacuated? Who do we all message? Who
collects the children, and are they on the school's list? Who checks on the neighbor? What is our trigger for
each hazard? **And do we all agree that nobody goes back for anybody?**

Write the answers on the binder's plan pages. Then put the date on the front page, put a reminder in the
calendar for twelve months from now, and go and do something else — because that is genuinely it. Your
household is now better prepared than the overwhelming majority of American households, and it took eight
weeks of short evenings.

**Then next year, Protection 130: the annual review.** Two hours, once, before your season. Rotate, replace,
repack, re-drive, redo the inventory, re-read the declarations page, and practice one thing. Preparedness
decays, and two hours a year is what stops it.
