## Leaving

Most of this book is about staying — because most of the time staying is correct, and because the
house you hardened is the best shelter you have access to.

This chapter is the exception, and it is the highest-stakes chapter in the book, because the two ways
to die in an evacuation are opposites. You die by leaving too late, in a car, on a road, in the fire or
the water. And you die by leaving too early into worse conditions than the ones you left, or by leaving
when you should have gone up rather than out.

Wildfire and storm surge are the two hazards where leaving is nearly always correct and nearly always
time-critical. Tornado and flash flood are the two where getting into a car is frequently the thing
that kills you. Knowing which situation you are in is the whole skill, and it is decided by the trigger
plan from Protection 95, written in advance, when nobody is frightened.

---

### 106 · Two routes out, driven

**Prevents:** discovering your only road is the one everybody else is on, or under water.
**Cost:** the fuel · **Time:** two hours, once · **Materials:** a map
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast rank 2, Wildfire West rank 1.

Pick two routes out of your area that do not share a bottleneck — different bridges, different
canyons, different highways. Then **drive them both**, in daylight, and notice the things a map does
not show: the low-water crossing, the single-lane bridge, the section that runs through unbroken brush
for four miles, the level crossing, the gate that is locked at night.

Write the turn-by-turn on the form in the binder, in words, and keep a paper map in each vehicle. A
navigation app routes you into the closure, and it needs a signal.

For wildfire specifically, the honest and uncomfortable point: a road with fuel on both sides and no
turnout is not an escape route, it is a chimney. If both your routes look like that, your trigger has
to fire much earlier than a coastal household's would.

**What this does not do:** two routes are not two open routes. Authorities close roads and reverse
lanes, and you follow their instruction over your plan. The plan exists so you are choosing between
alternatives rather than inventing one.

### 107 · The go-bag, per person

**Prevents:** packing under a deadline, which reliably produces the wrong bag.
**Cost:** $30–80 per person · **Time:** two hours · **Materials:** a bag each
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 3 Wildfire West.

One bag per person, packed and by the door — not a list of things to pack.

Contents that earn their weight: three days of water and food that needs no cooking, a change of
clothes appropriate to the season and sturdy shoes, the medication supply from Protection 59, a flashlight
and spare batteries, a phone battery pack and cables, the document envelope from Protection 97, cash,
sanitation basics, a first aid kit, a whistle, an N95, a hat and gloves, a blanket or sleeping bag, a
charged radio, and for children a specific comfort item chosen by them.

Weight matters more than completeness. A bag a person cannot carry for twenty minutes is a bag that
gets abandoned, and children carry their own only if it is genuinely small.

Check it twice a year on the same schedule as the smoke alarms: rotate the food and water, replace
outgrown clothes, refresh the medication, and confirm the batteries.

**What this does not do:** a go-bag is three days of survival for a person who has left, not
self-sufficiency. It bridges you to a shelter, a relative or a hotel. It is not a wilderness kit.

### 108 · The five-minute list and the thirty-minute list

**Prevents:** freezing in the doorway, or dying while looking for a photo album.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Materials:** the form in the binder
· **Regions:** Wildfire West rank 4, all five.

Two lists, written now, taped inside a cupboard door where anyone can find them.

**The five-minute list** is what leaves when there is no time: people, pets, go-bags, medication,
phones and chargers, document envelope, keys, wallets. Nothing else. That is the entire list, and its
purpose is to make the decision to leave cost five minutes rather than an argument.

**The thirty-minute list** is what leaves when you have warning: the hard drive, the photographs, the
irreplaceable object, the second set of clothes, the pet carrier and litter, the laptop, the
grandmother's whatever it is. Write the actual items and where in the house they are, because under
stress people search rooms they have already searched.

Give every adult a copy and assign each one a specific area, so two people are not both carrying the
same box.

**What this does not do:** neither list gets you a decision. The trigger plan does that. These lists
only make the leaving fast once the trigger has fired.

### 109 · Pets, planned properly

**Prevents:** the returning-for-the-dog death, which is a recurring pattern.
**Cost:** $40–120 · **Time:** an hour · **Materials:** carriers, records, a list
· **Regions:** all five.

People die going back for animals. Plan so that they never have to.

A carrier per animal, sized correctly, stored where it can be reached without moving furniture — and
the animals should have been in them before, because a cat that has never seen a carrier costs you
fifteen minutes you do not have.

A pet bag: a week of food, water, bowls, medication, a leash, waste bags or litter, and a copy of the
vaccination records, because a shelter that accepts animals will ask for them.

Then the part people skip: **many emergency shelters do not accept pets.** Find out now which shelters
in your area are pet-friendly, which boarding kennels and veterinary practices take evacuees, and
which of your out-of-area contacts would take an animal. Write the three phone numbers down.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Prepare Your Pets for Disasters" — **many public shelters and hotels do not allow pets inside**, so identify a safe place in advance; develop a buddy system with neighbours; microchip the animal and keep the address, phone number and an out-of-area emergency contact current. https://www.ready.gov/pets | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Microchip the animals and confirm the registration details are current — the chip is useless if it
points at a phone number you have not used in six years. Photograph each animal with a household member
in frame, which is how ownership gets settled at a reunification point.

**What this does not do:** none of this covers livestock, which needs its own plan made with your
veterinarian and neighbors, and which frequently means moving animals days rather than hours ahead.

### 110 · Fuel discipline

**Prevents:** an evacuation route you cannot reach the end of.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** a habit · **Materials:** none
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, Wildfire West, all five.

Keep the tank above half through your hazard season. That is the whole protection, and it fails for a
predictable reason: pumps need mains power, and in a regional outage the stations are shut. The queues
that form before a hurricane landfall empty the stations that are still working within hours.

The same logic applies to an electric vehicle — charge above half in season, know where the chargers on
both routes are, and understand that a charging stop is measured in tens of minutes rather than five,
which changes when your trigger has to fire.

If you store fuel, store it properly: an approved container, outdoors or in a detached structure, never
in the living space, stabilized and rotated. Gasoline stored badly is a fire risk you have added to a fire
season.

**What this does not do:** half a tank is not unlimited range. On a gridlocked evacuation route with the
air conditioning running, consumption is far higher than you expect.

### 111 · Where you are going, specifically

**Prevents:** driving with nowhere to arrive.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Materials:** three phone calls
· **Regions:** all five.

Three destinations, in order, written down.

**First choice:** a specific relative or friend far enough away to be outside the event — and this is a
conversation you have now, in advance, both directions, because the arrangement that works is
reciprocal. Ask how many people and animals they can take.

**Second choice:** a hotel in a named town on your route, at a distance that makes sense — and know that
the ones nearby fill first and that pet policies vary.

**Third choice:** the public shelter system, and know how to find out which shelters have opened,
because it is announced during the event rather than published in advance.

**If anyone in the household cannot drive or cannot easily be moved**, the destination question comes
with a transport question, and it has to be answered before the event rather than during it. Many
counties operate a voluntary registry for residents who would need assistance to evacuate — mobility
limitations, oxygen, dialysis, a wheelchair, no vehicle — and enrolment is free and usually a single
form. Enrol, and then do not rely on it alone: arrange a named neighbor or relative who has agreed
to come, because a registry is a list of intentions and a neighbor is a person. This is the same
registry conversation as Protection 42, extended from power to movement.

Distance is a judgment call. Too close and you are inside the event or its power outage. Too far and
you may not reach it. For a hurricane, a couple of hundred miles inland is a different proposition from
crossing three states.

**If you do not drive, or someone in the house cannot move fast.** Your trigger is your driver's
trigger. If they want to "wait for the order," you do not have a driver. You have a hope.

Name **two drivers**, not one. Ask each of them out loud: "If a hurricane watch is issued for our
county, will you take me that day?" Both must say yes to that trigger, not to "if it gets bad." Put
both numbers on the fridge.

«FIG: leave-early-no-car»

Then call your county emergency office and ask four questions. Write the answers down.

1. Am I on your list? Ask them to confirm it in writing. A form you started does not count.
2. When does transport come? Ask whether it runs only after an evacuation order.
3. Where does it take me?
4. Can my pet and my walker come with me?

Time yourself once. Start a clock at "go" and stop it when you are seated in the car with your bag,
your medicine and your pet. If it takes longer than you expected, move your trigger earlier.

Pack a bag you can lift or roll by yourself. If three days of water from Protection 107 is too heavy
to carry, pack less and let your driver load the rest.

«FIG: illus-walker-porch»

**What this does not do:** a destination is not a reservation. In a mass evacuation the hotels fill in
hours. Having three named options is what stops you driving without a plan.

### 112 · The out-of-area contact

**Prevents:** a household that cannot find itself.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **Materials:** the form in the binder
· **Regions:** all five.

One person, well outside your region, who everybody in the household calls or messages if separated.

The reason it works is that local networks congest and long-distance frequently gets through when local
does not, and because it converts a many-to-many problem into a hub. Nobody has to reach anybody
specific — they only have to reach the hub, and the hub knows where everyone is.

**Text rather than call**, and this is federal guidance rather than folk wisdom. FEMA's instruction, in
its own words: *"Send text messages or use social media to reach out to family and friends. Phone systems
often are busy after a disaster. Only make emergency calls."* Messages queue and deliver on a congested
network; voice calls need a circuit that may not exist. Keeping the voice channel clear is also the
reason for the second half of that instruction — every non-emergency call you make is capacity somebody
else needs.
«SRC: Ready.gov / FEMA — "Send text messages or use social media to reach out to family and friends.
Phone systems often are busy after a disaster. Only make emergency calls." |
https://www.ready.gov/volcanoes | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**Then the part that concerns the children specifically.** Find out your school's actual
reunification procedure — where children are taken, who is permitted to collect them, and what
identification is required — because the answer is frequently not "the school" and frequently not
"any parent who arrives." Put every adult who might collect a child on the school's authorized
release form now, including the grandparent and the neighbor, because a school following its own
policy correctly will refuse to release a child to someone not on that list. Do the same for a
nursery or an after-school program, and tell the children who is allowed to collect them.

Everybody memorizes that number or carries it on paper, including the children, and it goes in the
document envelope.

**A correction to advice you have probably read elsewhere, including in older editions of books like this
one.** For years the standard instruction was to register on the Red Cross "Safe and Well" website so that
family could search for your name. **That self-registration website no longer exists**, and a book that
told you to go and register on it would be sending you to a dead link at the worst possible moment. It has
been replaced by a **person-mediated reunification service**: if you cannot reach someone affected by a
current disaster, you contact the Red Cross and ask for help with an **Emergency Welfare Inquiry**, and a
local chapter may connect you with Reunification Activity workers in the disaster area.

The practical consequence for your plan is real. **You can no longer make yourself findable in advance by
typing your name into a registry** — which means the out-of-area contact in Protection 112 is doing more
work than it used to, not less, and it is the mechanism to rely on. The reunification service is what your
relatives use when the contact chain itself has failed, and it works on requests rather than searches.

Check the current mechanism before you write it into your binder, because this is the part of the plan
most likely to have changed since anything you read about it.
«SRC: American Red Cross, "Contact and Locate Loved Ones" — reunification of family in the US and US
territories; if you are unable to locate or connect with a loved one or friend impacted by a current
disaster event, contact the Red Cross; a representative from your local chapter may connect you with
Reunification Activity workers in the disaster area (ask for help with an Emergency Welfare Inquiry). No
public self-registration site is offered; the former "Safe and Well" registration website is not present. | https://www.redcross.org/get-help/disaster-relief-and-recovery-services/contact-and-locate-loved-ones.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**What this does not do:** a contact does not locate a missing person. It coordinates people who are
each fine but out of touch, which is the overwhelmingly common case.

### 113 · Shutting the house down as you leave

**Prevents:** the empty house that burns, floods or fills with gas.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 15 minutes · **Materials:** the checklist by the door
· **Regions:** Wildfire West rank 7, Hurricane Coast.

A short sequence, on the wall, so that the last person out does it without thinking.

Close every window and interior door, which slows fire spread through the building. Take down curtains
or push them away from glass, and move upholstered furniture away from windows, because radiant heat
through glass is how wildfire ignites interiors without flame contact.

For flood: shut the main water valve if you are leaving a freezing house, and move what you can
upstairs — but only if the time exists, and it usually does not.

Electricity and gas: shut the main breaker only if instructed or if flooding is expected. **Gas is
different — turn it off only if you have been told to, or if you smell or hear a leak.** A gas supply
turned off at the meter often requires the utility to restore it, and that can take weeks after a
regional event, so this is not a default action.

Leave the exterior lights on so responders can see the building, leave a note if local practice asks
for one, and lock up.

**What this does not do:** none of this saves a house from a fire front or from surge. It removes the
easy ignition paths and the secondary damage, and it is done only with time to spare.

### 114 · Vertical evacuation — going up, not out

**Prevents:** driving into the thing you are trying to escape.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** thinking, once · **Materials:** honest assessment
· **Regions:** flood-exposed households, Hurricane Coast.

Sometimes leaving means going upstairs.

In a flash flood with water already moving on the roads, a vehicle is the most dangerous place
available. The National Weather Service's own numbers: **six inches of fast-moving water can knock
over an adult, twelve inches carries away most cars, two feet carries away SUVs and trucks** — and,
citing CDC, **over half of all flood-related drownings happen when a vehicle is driven into flood
water.** The next largest share is people walking into or near it. Going up inside a solid building
beats driving.
«SRC: National Weather Service, "Turn Around Don't Drown." |
https://www.weather.gov/safety/flood-turn-around-dont-drown | retrieved 2026-07-29»

The rule that governs it: **the decision to go up is only survivable if you can keep going up.** An
attic with no exit is a sealed box, which is why an axe or a hatchet in the attic is standard advice in
surge-exposed coastal housing, and why a single-storey house in a surge zone is not a vertical
evacuation candidate at all — it is a leave-early house.

Storm surge is the case where vertical thinking gets people killed. Surge is not rising water in a
street, it is a moving wall with debris in it, and it destroys the structure you were planning to be
above. In a surge evacuation zone, you leave. That is not a judgment call.

**What this does not do:** vertical evacuation is a last resort forced by timing, not a plan. If the
trigger fires and the roads are open, you leave.

### 115 · Driving in it

**Prevents:** the deaths that happen after the correct decision to leave.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** reading this once · **Materials:** none
· **Regions:** all five.

Four rules, and they are all counterintuitive under stress.

**Never drive into water.** Not moving water, not standing water of unknown depth, not a road that
looks shallow. The roadbed under it may not exist. Turn around; there is a route that is not flooded, or
there is high ground.

**Never drive into smoke.** Visibility collapses to nothing in seconds, the vehicles ahead of you have
stopped, and the vehicles behind you have not. If you are caught in it, headlights and hazards on,
slow, and get off the road at the first genuine opportunity.

**Do not drive under a tornado warning if you can shelter instead.** A vehicle is one of the worst
places to be in a tornado, and the standard advice to abandon a car for a ditch exists because the car
is worse than the ditch. The exception is a clear, verified escape at right angles to the storm's track
with real information and open roads, and that exception is far rarer than people assume.

**Assume the fuel, the food and the toilets on your route are gone.** Leave with what you need in the
car.

And the one that follows from breaking the first rule: **if a vehicle is in rising water, get out
immediately, through a window.** Water pressure holds the doors shut and the electric windows fail
once the water reaches the wiring, so the window is the exit and the first seconds are the ones that
work. A spring-loaded window punch on the keyring costs a few dollars and breaks tempered side glass
where a fist will not — the windscreen is laminated and will not break, so it is always a side window.
Unbuckle, window, out, and move to higher ground rather than staying with the car. Say this out loud to
everyone who drives, because it has to be recalled rather than reasoned.

Then the boring one that matters: keep the tires, wipers and lights on your vehicles working, because
an evacuation is the wrong time to discover the wipers are shot.

**What this does not do:** none of this makes driving in a disaster safe. It removes the four specific
mistakes that recur in every case review.

### 116 · Coming back

**Prevents:** getting hurt after the event is over, which is when a large share of injuries happen.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** reading, once · **Materials:** the checklist in the binder
· **Regions:** all five.

Return when authorities say the area is open, not when you can physically get there. Roadblocks exist
because of live wires, gas leaks, unstable structures and unmapped washouts.

Then approach the property the way an inspector would, from outside in. Look at the structure before
you enter it: a shifted foundation, a sagging roofline, a bulging wall, a doorframe out of square. Smell
before you enter, and if there is gas, do not enter and do not touch a switch. Assume every downed wire
is live and every puddle touching one is energized.

Inside, do not switch anything on until you know whether water reached the electrics. Photograph
everything before you move it, per Protection 105. Wear boots, gloves, long sleeves and eye
protection, because the injuries in the recovery week are cuts, punctures, falls and eye injuries, not
dramatic ones.

Two things that kill people in the week after: **generators and heaters run indoors**, which is
Protection 32 and the single largest post-event killer in a cold outage, and **chainsaws used by
people who have not used one**, which produces the other large injury category. If you are not
competent with the saw, the tree waits.

Water is not safe because it runs. Follow the boil notice, and if you do not know whether there is
one, assume there is.

**What this does not do:** returning safely does not make the property habitable. That is a separate
judgment, sometimes a formal one, and a house you can stand in is not always a house you should sleep
in.
