# Part Three, Playbook 23 — Medical Care When Nobody Is Coming

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

The 911 system in Joplin took more calls in the hour after the tornado than it could answer. Hospitals were
themselves destroyed — St. John's Regional Medical Center was struck directly and evacuated, which removed a
trauma centre from the response at the exact moment the city needed it most.
«CITE: NWS Joplin service assessment and/or the hospital's own record — the damage to St. John's Regional
Medical Center and its evacuation.»

That is the assumption this playbook is built on, and it is not pessimism. It is arithmetic. **In any event
large enough to be in this book, emergency medical services are simultaneously overwhelmed, physically
blocked, and possibly damaged themselves.** Roads are impassable. Every call arrives in the same twenty
minutes. Ambulances are queued at a hospital that has no power. The response time you are used to is a
peacetime number.

So for some period — an hour, a day, in a Cascadia scenario possibly a week — **the medical care available to
your household is the medical care inside your household.**

The good news, and it is genuinely good: the interventions that change outcomes in that window are few,
cheap, and learnable in an afternoon. This is not about practising medicine. It is about doing four or five
specific things well while waiting for people who are coming as fast as they can.

---

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Uncontrolled bleeding is the leading preventable cause of death from trauma**, and the window is minutes.
A person with an arterial injury to a limb can die in less time than it takes to describe the problem, and
the intervention — direct pressure, packing, a tourniquet — is within the reach of anybody who has been shown
once.
«CITE: the Stop the Bleed / Hartford Consensus source establishing haemorrhage as the leading preventable
cause of trauma death and the time frame involved.»

**Airway obstruction and breathing failure**, from injury, from smoke inhalation, from asthma without an
inhaler.

**Cardiac arrest**, where immediate CPR and, where available, a defibrillator are the difference. Survival
falls sharply with every minute of delay.

**Then the slow ones, which dominate the death toll in longer events:**

**Interrupted chronic care.** Dialysis missed. Insulin spoiled or exhausted. Oxygen concentrator without
power. Cardiac and seizure medications run out. Blood pressure medication stopped. These kill more people
across a multi-day disaster than the trauma does, and every one of them is prevented by Protection 59's
thirty-day buffer and Protection 42's power plan.

**Infection**, from wounds contaminated by floodwater and debris, untreated because nobody thought a
puncture wound mattered.

**Dehydration**, in children and elderly people, from gastrointestinal illness in a household with limited
clean water.

**Hypothermia and heat illness**, covered in their own playbooks and worth naming again here because they
present as medical emergencies rather than as weather.

**Costs:** the kit in this playbook totals a couple of hundred dollars, and the training is frequently free.
There is no cheaper life-saving expenditure in this book.

## Before

- **58 — bleeding control and CPR training.** Do this. It is the highest-yield protection in the entire book
  and it is usually free or nearly so — a Stop the Bleed course is typically a couple of hours, and CPR
  courses run constantly through hospitals, fire departments and community centres. Get two adults in the
  household trained, and get teenagers trained, because they are frequently the person who is there.
- **57 — the trauma kit, and where it lives.** Not a supermarket first aid tin. A real kit, in a known
  place, with a second one in the vehicle. Contents that earn their space:
  - **A commercial tourniquet**, and the training to use it. Improvised tourniquets frequently fail.
  - **Haemostatic gauze**, and plain gauze for packing wounds.
  - **Trauma dressings and pressure bandages**, and a chest seal if you have been trained for it.
  - **Gloves** — many pairs, because you will use them faster than you expect.
  - **Trauma shears**, to get clothing off an injury.
  - Splinting material — a rolled SAM splint or improvised from what is around.
  - **Irrigation supplies**: a large syringe and clean water, because irrigation is the actual treatment for
    a contaminated wound.
  - Wound closure strips, dressings, tape, and a proper supply of bandages rather than a token few.
  - **A CPR barrier mask.**
  - Oral rehydration salts, which are the treatment for the most likely illness in a long event.
  - A thermometer, tweezers, a torch, and a permanent marker for writing times on a dressing or on skin.
  - Basic medications with a written note of doses: paracetamol, ibuprofen, an antihistamine, an
    anti-diarrhoeal, oral rehydration, and any household-specific rescue medication.
- **59 — the thirty-day prescription buffer**, and knowing your state's emergency refill law, which in most
  states permits a pharmacist to dispense an emergency supply during a declared disaster.
- **42 — the medical device power plan**, and the utility's medical registry, and a conversation with the
  equipment supplier about batteries and with the clinician about what to do if the machine stops.
- **60, 61 — refrigerated medication and the dialysis, oxygen and infusion contingency.** For a household
  with any of these, this is not a chapter to read — it is a plan to write, with the clinic, naming the
  backup facility and the transport arrangement.
- **68 — spares.** Glasses, hearing aid batteries, mobility aid parts, catheters, ostomy supplies, whatever
  your household actually depends on. People spend weeks without these.
- **A written medical summary per person**, in the document envelope: conditions, medications with doses,
  allergies, recent surgeries, devices and implants, the clinician's name, and the insurance details. This
  is what a stranger providing care needs, and it is what nobody can recall accurately under stress.
- **67 — the when-EMS-cannot-reach-you protection**, which is mostly a set of decisions made in advance:
  which hospitals are where, which routes reach them, which are likely to be affected, and who in your
  neighbourhood is a nurse, a paramedic, a doctor or a veterinarian. That last one matters — knowing who is
  on your street is a genuine resource and it costs a conversation.

**And learn the small number of things worth knowing cold**, because you will not be reading a book: how to
pack a wound and apply a tourniquet, how to do compression-only CPR, how to recognise stroke, how to
recognise a heart attack in someone who is describing something vague, how to recognise hypothermia and heat
stroke, and how to put an unconscious breathing person on their side.

## During

**First, the thing nobody teaches and everybody needs: keep yourself out of the casualty list.** You cannot
help from under the debris. Assess the scene, do not enter unstable structures, do not touch downed lines,
and put gloves on.

**Then the priorities, in order.**

**Massive bleeding first.** Direct, hard pressure with your hands, immediately, and do not lift it to look.
For a limb wound that will not stop, a tourniquet high and tight above the injury, tightened until the
bleeding stops even though it hurts, and **write the time on it**. For a wound in a place you cannot
tourniquet — neck, armpit, groin — pack it firmly with gauze and hold hard pressure. Modern guidance is that
a tourniquet applied correctly saves the person and does not cost them the limb in the timescales that
matter.

**Airway and breathing next.** An unconscious person who is breathing goes on their side. Someone not
breathing normally gets CPR: hard and fast compressions in the centre of the chest, about two per second,
allowing full recoil, and continue. Compression-only CPR is genuinely effective and it is what a
non-professional should do without hesitation.

**Then everything else**, which is where most household injuries actually sit: wounds, burns, fractures,
illness. Protections 62 to 65 cover burns, hypothermia, heat illness and floodwater wounds specifically.

**The practical rules for the ordinary injuries.**

**Wounds:** irrigate generously with clean water — that is the treatment — remove obvious debris, cover, and
do not close a contaminated wound tightly. Then watch it for infection: increasing pain, spreading redness,
swelling, heat, discharge, red streaks, fever. Any of those in a disaster context is a reason to get to care.

**Puncture wounds and anything from disaster debris need a tetanus check.** Write down the date and the
mechanism, because you will be asked.

**Burns:** cool with cool running water for a genuinely long time — twenty minutes — then cover loosely with
something clean and non-adherent. Nothing greasy, no ice, and do not break blisters. Anything large, deep,
circumferential, on the face, hands, feet or genitals, or in a child, is a get-to-care injury regardless of
how hard that is.

**Fractures:** splint in the position found, pad it, check circulation beyond the splint before and after,
and do not attempt to straighten anything.

**Then triage, honestly**, if there are more casualties than hands. This is the hardest paragraph in the
book. The people who are quiet are frequently the sickest; the people shouting are breathing. In a
multi-casualty situation the discipline is to do the quick, high-yield things for the people who will benefit
most — stop bleeding, open airways, position people — rather than everything for one person while others
die. Nobody is trained for this and no household expects to face it, and knowing the principle in advance is
the only preparation available.

**Document as you go.** Times, interventions, doses given, and what you observed. Write it on paper or on
the person's skin. The clinician who eventually receives them needs it, and you will not remember.

**Then keep trying to get help.** Text rather than call. Try 911 even with no signal indicator, because
emergency calls route over any available carrier. Know your county's ten-digit alternative. Send someone
physically to a fire station or a hospital if that is realistic, and understand that a functioning
neighbourhood with a nurse in it is a better resource in hour two than a phone.

## After

**Get everybody properly assessed, including the people who seem fine.** Head injuries, chest injuries and
internal bleeding all present late. Anyone knocked unconscious, anyone with chest or abdominal pain, anyone
who was crushed or trapped even briefly, and anyone with a wound in floodwater needs professional review.

**Crush injury deserves a specific warning**, because it is the one that surprises people: a person trapped
for a prolonged period can deteriorate rapidly **after** release, as products from damaged muscle enter the
circulation. If someone has been trapped under weight for a long time, that is an urgent medical situation
even if they walk away from it, and it should be said to whoever receives them.

**Watch for infection over the following days**, and treat any wound that is getting worse rather than better
as a reason to get to care rather than a reason to add another dressing.

**Restore the chronic care immediately.** Medication refilled, dialysis rescheduled, oxygen resupplied,
insulin replaced if the fridge failed, appointments remade. This is what actually kills people in the fortnight
after, and it is administrative work rather than medicine.

**Then the mind, which is not optional.** Protection 66 exists because the psychological injury from these
events is real, common, and delayed — in children especially, and in the adults who held everything together
during the week and fall over in the month. Sleep disruption, intrusive memories, irritability,
hypervigilance, withdrawal, and in children regression and clinginess. Most of it settles. Some of it does
not, and the people who get help early do better. Say to your household, out loud, that this is expected and
allowed.

**And look after the responders in your own household**, meaning whoever did the treating. The person who
applied the tourniquet to a family member carries something afterwards, and they are usually the last person
anyone asks about.

**Then restock the kit and book the training.** Every household that has used its trauma kit knows exactly
what was missing from it, and the honest majority discover the thing that was missing was the training rather
than the equipment.
