# Part Three, Playbook 14 — Tsunami

Target: 8 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

## The event

On 11 March 2011 a magnitude nine earthquake off the coast of Tōhoku produced a tsunami that overtopped
sea walls built for a smaller event, travelled miles inland, and killed the great majority of the
disaster's roughly eighteen thousand dead. It also crossed the Pacific and did damage on the American West
Coast, including a fatality in California — a person who had gone to the shore to watch it.
«CITE: authoritative record of the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami — fatality total, the share caused by
the tsunami, inundation distances, and the US West Coast impacts including the California fatality.»

The American planning case is not Japan, though. It is Cascadia: a subduction zone running from northern
California to British Columbia, capable of the same magnitude, with a recurrence interval that places the
last event in January 1700 — dated precisely, because the tsunami it sent across the Pacific was recorded
in Japanese documents and the drowned coastal forests along the Pacific Northwest coast can be dated to the
same winter.
«CITE: the 1700 Cascadia earthquake — the Japanese tsunami records and the dendrochronology establishing
the date, plus current USGS recurrence estimates.»

The critical fact for a household on that coast: **a Cascadia tsunami arrives in fifteen to thirty minutes
in many places.** No official alert can outrun that. The earthquake itself is the warning, and there is no
time to await confirmation, collect the car, or drive anywhere in traffic.

---

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Drowning, and blunt trauma from what the water carries.** A tsunami is not a wave that breaks on you. It
is a fast, prolonged rise in sea level that keeps coming, carrying vehicles, boats, buildings, timber and
debris, and then withdraws with the same force, taking people out to sea. Water a few feet deep moving at
that speed is unsurvivable on foot.

**There is never only one.** Tsunamis arrive as a series over hours, and the first is frequently not the
largest. People die returning after the first wave, and people die going down to look.

**The two categories that matter operationally:**

A **local (near-field) tsunami**, generated by an earthquake you felt, arrives in minutes. Self-evacuation
on foot, immediately, is the only protection.

A **distant (far-field) tsunami**, generated across the ocean, arrives in hours, with a full official
alerting cascade and time to move properly.

The protections are the same; only the pace differs, and confusing the two is fatal.

**Costs:** near-total destruction inside the inundation zone. Tsunami damage is flood damage for insurance
purposes, which means it is not covered by a standard homeowner policy and requires flood insurance —
Protection 103, with its thirty-day clock, and the same substantial-damage rebuilding rules as the flood
playbooks.

## Before

- **Find out whether you are in a tsunami hazard zone, and where the zone's edge is.** Every West Coast,
  Alaskan and Hawaiian coastal community has mapped inundation zones with signed evacuation routes, and the
  maps are public. This is the single fact this playbook exists to make you look up.
- **Then find your high ground and time the walk.** Pick the specific place — a named hill, a specific
  street corner, a designated vertical evacuation structure — that is high enough and far enough, and walk
  the route, at the pace of the slowest person in your household, with a stopwatch. If the walk takes longer
  than the arrival time, you need a closer objective, and that is a decision to make now rather than
  during.
- **Plan on foot.** Roads in a coastal town after a magnitude nine will be cracked, blocked by debris, and
  jammed. A car is frequently slower than walking and it is a trap. Some communities have designated
  vertical evacuation structures — engineered towers or buildings — and if yours does, know which and how to
  get in.
- **91 — know your zone**, and **85, 86 — alert paths**, which matter for the distant-source case where
  official warnings are the trigger.
- **107 — go-bags, grab-and-run light.** In this hazard the bag has to be carryable at speed uphill. Water,
  medication, documents, shoes, a warm layer, a torch, a whistle. Everything else is optional.
- **The whole earthquake playbook**, because the local case is an earthquake first: 43 (shoes and torch at
  the bed), 83 (anchoring), 57 (trauma kit).
- **103 — flood insurance**, which is the only insurance instrument that responds.
- **96, 100 — document vault and video inventory**, stored off-site, because inside the inundation zone
  nothing survives.
- **Drill it**, including at night, and drill it with children, because school and household plans need to
  match. Coastal schools in the zone have their own plans; find out what yours is.

**And decide the hard thing in advance**: in the local case nobody comes back for anybody. Everyone goes
uphill to the agreed place and meets there. That agreement, made now, is what stops a household losing
someone who went back.

## During

**The local case — you felt a strong earthquake near the coast.**

**If the shaking is strong, or lasts more than about twenty seconds, that is your warning.** Drop, cover and
hold on until it stops — then move immediately.

**Go inland and uphill, on foot, now.** Do not wait for an official alert. Do not wait for a siren. Do not
go to the beach to look. Do not collect the car. Do not go back for anything. Take the people, take the
bag if it is at hand, and walk fast.

**Keep going past what feels far enough.** The mapped zone edge is the target, and higher and further is
better.

**Natural warnings, any one of which is sufficient on its own:** strong or long shaking; a sudden rise or
fall in the water, especially the sea withdrawing and exposing the sea bed; and a loud roaring sound
offshore. If you see or hear any of them, you have minutes, and the response is the same.

**If you genuinely cannot reach high ground:** get as high as possible in the strongest available building —
upper floors of a reinforced concrete or steel structure — or, as a last resort, climb a sturdy tree.
Neither is a plan; both are what you do when the plan failed.

**If you are swept into the water:** grab something that floats and hold on. Do not try to swim in it.

**The distant case — an official alert with hours of lead time.**

Understand the alert levels: a **warning** means dangerous inundation is expected and the zone evacuates; an
**advisory** means strong currents and dangerous conditions in the water and at the shoreline, so people
stay out of the water and off beaches and harbours; a **watch** means a distant event has occurred and more
information is coming.

With hours, evacuate properly: take the go-bags, the documents, the medication and the pets, secure the
house, and use vehicles if the roads are clear and the authorities have not directed otherwise. Then stay
out of the zone until it is officially cleared.

**And in both cases, boats:** small craft in a harbour during a tsunami are destroyed by currents and
surge. Do not go to the marina to move a boat, and do not put to sea unless there is genuinely time and
depth to reach deep water — a decision for professional mariners, not a household one.

## After

**Stay out until officials say the sequence is over.** This is the instruction people break, and the later
waves in a tsunami series are frequently larger than the first. Hours, not minutes.

**Expect the aftershocks** to continue, and each is capable of generating another tsunami.

**When you return, treat the area as a demolition site with contamination.** The water carried fuel,
sewage, chemicals, and everything the buildings contained. Structural damage is severe and hidden: buildings
that look standing have had their foundations scoured. Do not enter anything that has been inundated until
it has been assessed.

**Debris is the hazard.** Nails, glass, splintered timber, sharp metal, unstable piles, and gas cylinders.
Full protective equipment, and nothing moved by hand that should be moved by machine.

**Utilities all stay off** until inspected — and after a subduction earthquake plus inundation, "until
inspected" is a long time. Everything submerged is condemned electrically. The water supply is unsafe.
Sewers are broken.

**The recovery is a Part One problem, not a Part Three problem.** Realistically, a Cascadia event means a
region without water, power, fuel, roads, bridges, hospitals or supply chains for weeks and in places
months — which is why coastal Northwest households are advised to plan for a **two-week minimum** of
self-sufficiency rather than the three days the national messaging uses.
«CITE: the specific two-week preparedness recommendation issued by Pacific Northwest state emergency
management agencies, with the agency named.»

That is the honest bottom line of this playbook: your survival is decided by a fifteen-minute walk you
planned in advance, and your recovery is decided by Protections 1, 13, 37 to 46 and 53 to 55 — the ordinary
supply protections, held at a much larger scale than the rest of the country needs.

**Then the money.** Flood insurance if you have it, federal assistance, and the substantial-damage rules,
which in an inundation zone frequently mean rebuilding to a higher elevation standard or not rebuilding
there at all. That conversation, painful as it is, belongs at the start of the recovery rather than the
end.
