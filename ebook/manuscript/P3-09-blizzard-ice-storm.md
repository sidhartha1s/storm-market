# Part Three, Playbook 9 — Blizzard and Ice Storm

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

An ice storm is the most destructive winter event a household can experience, and it does not look like
much while it is happening.

In January 1998, freezing rain fell for days across northern New England and eastern Canada. Ice
accumulated on every conductor and every branch until transmission towers folded and the distribution
network came apart over a vast area. Millions of people lost power, some of them for weeks, in
January.
«CITE: authoritative record for the January 1998 North American ice storm — ice accretion depths, people
without power, and the longest restoration times.»

Then February 2021 in Texas showed the other version of the same failure: a cold event that took out
generation and distribution in a state whose housing stock, plumbing and grid were not built for it.
Hundreds of people died, most of them from cold inside their own homes and from carbon monoxide
poisoning as they tried to keep warm.
«CITE: Texas DSHS or peer-reviewed analysis of the February 2021 winter storm death toll, with the
breakdown by hypothermia and carbon monoxide.»

The lesson both events teach is the same one, and it is the reason this playbook exists separately from
the extreme cold playbook that follows it: **the winter storm is not the hazard. The multi-day power
failure inside the winter storm is the hazard.** A house without power in January stops being shelter in
about a day.

Half an inch of ice accretion brings down limbs. Three-quarters of an inch brings down power lines.
An inch and a half brings down the poles and towers themselves.
«CITE: NWS ice accretion damage thresholds — the accretion depths associated with limb, line and pole
failure.»

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Killed by, in order of how many:**

**Carbon monoxide**, from every improvised heat source people reach for when the furnace stops: a
generator in a garage or a basement, a charcoal grill indoors, a camp stove, an unvented kerosene heater,
a gas oven left open, a car running in an attached garage. This is the largest killer in a winter outage
and it is entirely preventable.

**Hypothermia indoors**, disproportionately among elderly people living alone, in a house that cooled
over two days while they added blankets rather than leaving.

**Cardiac events during snow clearing.** Shovelling wet snow is heavy exertion in cold air for people
who are frequently sedentary, and the effect on heart attack incidence after heavy snowfall is well
documented.
«CITE: a study or public-health source quantifying cardiac events associated with snow shovelling after
heavy snowfall.»

**Traffic**, and specifically black ice and multi-vehicle collisions in whiteout conditions.

**House fires**, from space heaters placed too close to furnishings, from fireplaces and chimneys used
after years of disuse, and from candles.

**Costs:** the burst pipe is the defining property loss of this hazard, and it is expensive out of
proportion to its drama — a pipe that freezes and splits in an unheated house floods it continuously
until someone turns the water off, which in an evacuated house can be days. Then roof damage from ice
dams and snow load, tree damage, vehicle damage, and food loss.

## Before

Priority order for an Ice & Snow North household:

- **34 — pipe freeze prevention.** Insulate pipes in unheated spaces, know where the vulnerable runs are,
  and know the drip protocol. This is rank one because it is the most likely loss.
- **35 — shut-off literacy.** Where the water main is, which way it turns, and a labelled tag on it. The
  entire cost of a burst pipe is decided by how long it runs.
- **26 — warm-room consolidation.** Pick the room now: small, sunny if possible, interior, with the fewest
  exterior walls, ideally with a safe heat source. During the outage you live in it, sleep in it, and shut
  the rest of the house off.
- **32 — generator siting** and **47 — CO alarms on every level.** These two together are the
  intervention for the biggest killer, and the alarms need to be in date — the sensors expire, typically
  after seven to ten years, and an expired alarm still chirps reassuringly.
- **28 — sleeping bags rated for the temperature** rather than duvets, per person. A properly rated bag is
  the cheapest way to make an unheated house survivable overnight.
- **27 — window insulation**, and thermal coverings for the warm room.
- **36 — sump pump with battery backup**, for the thaw.
- **13, 1 — no-cook pantry and water store**, remembering that in a freeze the stored water needs
  protecting too (Protection 12).
- **43 — light layers and the no-candles rule**, which matters more here than anywhere because winter
  outages are long and dark and candles are how they become fires.
- **39, 38 — generator or battery capacity** sized honestly against what the furnace actually needs. Many
  gas furnaces need only a modest amount of electricity for the blower and controls, which makes them one
  of the highest-value things a small generator or a battery station can run. Find out your figure before
  you buy anything.
- **Chimney and flue serviced** if you intend to use a fireplace or wood stove as backup, because an
  unswept chimney used for the first time in five years is a chimney fire.
- **Roof and gutters**: gutters clear before winter, and attic insulation and ventilation checked
  (Protection 31), because ice dams are caused by heat leaking into the roof space and melting snow that
  then refreezes at the cold eave.

**Then the vehicle**, which is a genuine part of this hazard: winter tyres or proper all-season tyres, a
scraper and brush, a shovel, sand or grit, a blanket, a torch, and warm layers in the boot from November.
People are stranded in cars on interstates for many hours in this hazard every year.

**And a phone call to make.** Anyone in your circle who is old, alone or medically dependent needs a plan
that includes somebody checking on them, physically, during a multi-day outage. Agree it now.

## During

**On a winter storm watch or warning.** Charge everything. Fill water containers. Fuel the vehicles and
the generator. Get the prescriptions. Bring the shovel and grit to the door rather than the back of the
shed. Set the warm room up. Move firewood to the porch. And plan to go nowhere: almost every road death
in this hazard involves a journey that could have waited.

**On an ice storm warning specifically**, expect to lose power and plan on that basis, because freezing
rain is the single most reliable predictor of a long outage. Park vehicles away from trees and lines.
Fill the tubs.

**When the power goes.**

**Close the house down to the warm room.** Shut interior doors, close off unused rooms, and put a towel
along the bottom of the door. You are heating one small volume, not a house.

**Then protect the plumbing**, in the order that matters. If you have heat, leave a slow drip from the
taps furthest from the entry point and open the cupboard doors under sinks on exterior walls. If the house
is going to fall below freezing and you are leaving, **shut the water main off and drain the system** —
this is the whole difference between coming home to a cold house and coming home to a waterfall.

**Never heat with anything that burns fuel indoors.** No generator inside or in a garage, no charcoal, no
camp stove, no unvented kerosene or propane heater not specifically rated and vented for indoor use, no
gas oven as a heater, no car idling in an attached garage. If your CO alarm sounds, everyone goes outside
immediately and stays outside.

**Space heaters** need three feet of clearance from everything, a plug direct into a wall socket rather
than an extension lead, and switching off when people sleep.

**Watch for hypothermia**, which does not look dramatic — shivering that stops, clumsiness, slurred
speech, confusion, drowsiness. Protection 63 covers rewarming; the decision to look for is when the house
has become genuinely unsafe, and that is the moment to leave for a warming centre, a relative or a hotel.
Deciding in advance what indoor temperature triggers leaving makes that decision possible, because the
cold itself impairs the judgment needed to make it.

**Shovel like someone with a heart.** Little and often rather than all at the end. Push rather than lift.
Warm up first. Stop if you feel chest discomfort, pressure, breathlessness or nausea, and treat those
symptoms as a cardiac event rather than as being unfit.

**If you are stranded in a vehicle**, stay with it — it is visible and it is shelter. Run the engine
about ten minutes in the hour for heat, and **check the exhaust pipe is clear of snow every time**,
because a blocked exhaust fills the cabin with carbon monoxide.
«SRC: NWS, "What To Do If You're Caught in a Winter Storm" — if your car is stuck: **stay in the vehicle**, because you become disoriented quickly in wind-driven snow; **run the motor about 10 minutes each hour** for heat. Driving: over **5,000 road fatalities a year** are attributed to weather conditions; clear all snow off the vehicle; if visibility fails, pull over, stop, **turn your lights off** and use the parking brake so another driver does not follow your tail lights into you. https://www.weather.gov/safety/winter-during · retrieved 2026-07-29» Crack a window slightly on the downwind
side. Make the car visible.

## After

**The thaw is the second event.** Frozen pipes fail when they thaw, not while frozen, so the flood
arrives when the heat comes back. Be in the house when it thaws if you can, know where the main is, and
check every level and the basement as the temperature comes up.

**If you find a frozen pipe that has not yet split:** open the tap it feeds, and warm it gently along its
length with a hair dryer or towels, starting from the tap end. Never a blowtorch or any open flame. If
it has split, water off first, then deal with it.

**Ice dams** get removed carefully or not at all. Do not go on an icy roof, and do not attack the ice
with an axe — you will destroy the roof covering. A roof rake from the ground, calcium chloride in a
stocking laid across the dam, or a professional with steam. Then fix the cause in the summer, which is
almost always attic heat loss and inadequate ventilation.

**Snow load** matters on flat and low-slope roofs and on porches, carports and outbuildings, and it
matters most when rain falls onto deep snow. Look for new interior door and window binding, new cracks
in ceilings, and sagging — and clear the roof only from the ground or with a professional.

**Roof and gutter damage, and tree damage**, gets photographed before anything is moved (Protection 105).
Ice storm tree damage is frequently extensive and the chainsaw warning from Playbook 7 applies with full
force — ice-loaded limbs are under tension and release violently.

**The food** follows Protection 15: the fridge is good for a few hours closed, the freezer longer if it is
full and stays shut, and the deciding factor is temperature rather than appearance. If it went above the
safe threshold for hours, it goes. In this hazard there is a genuine temptation to use the snow outside as
a fridge, and it works only if the food is in sealed containers, out of sunlight and animals, and the air
temperature genuinely stays below the threshold — check with the thermometer rather than assuming.

**Then the honest post-mortem** for the annual review: did the warm room hold? Did the sleeping bags do
the job? Did the CO alarms work and are they in date? How long did the food and water last? Did the
generator start? Every household that has been through one winter outage knows exactly what to fix, and
most do not write it down.
