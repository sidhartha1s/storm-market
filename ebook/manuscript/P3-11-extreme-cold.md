# Part Three, Playbook 11 — Extreme Cold and Deep Freeze

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

In mid-February 2021 an Arctic air mass reached deep into Texas and stayed for days. Natural gas
wellheads and pipelines froze, generating plant tripped offline, and the state's grid came within
minutes of a collapse that would have taken months to recover from. Instead it shed load — which on the
ground meant days without power in single-digit temperatures, in houses built to shed heat rather than
hold it, with uninsulated plumbing running through unheated attics.
«CITE: FERC/NERC joint inquiry into the February 2021 cold weather event — the causes of generation loss
and how close the grid came to uncontrolled collapse.»

Hundreds died. Hypothermia indoors and carbon monoxide poisoning were the two largest categories.
«CITE: the official Texas mortality analysis, with the counts by cause.»

Then the burst pipes. Hundreds of thousands of homes flooded from inside as the freeze released, in a
state with almost no plumbing insulation, producing one of the largest single-event property losses in
American history.
«CITE: an insurance-industry estimate of the February 2021 event's insured losses and the share
attributable to freeze/burst-pipe claims.»

The distinction from the previous playbook is worth being precise about, because the response differs.
A blizzard or ice storm is a **storm** — wind, precipitation, downed lines, blocked roads. A deep freeze
can happen under a clear blue sky with no precipitation at all, and its mechanism is duration: hours
below freezing accumulating until things that have never frozen do.

**And its most important property is that it reaches places that have never seen it.** A cold snap that
is a Tuesday in Minnesota is a catastrophe in Houston, because the housing, the plumbing, the grid and
the people are all calibrated to a different climate. If you live in the South, this playbook is written
for you more than for the North.

---

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Carbon monoxide, first.** Same as the blizzard playbook and worth repeating because it kills more
people than the cold does: generators indoors or in garages, charcoal grills brought inside, camp stoves,
gas ovens used as heaters, unvented kerosene and propane heaters, cars idling in attached garages.

**Hypothermia indoors, second**, and mostly among elderly people living alone and people whose homes lose
heat fastest — mobile homes, poorly insulated rentals, and anyone whose heating was already marginal or
shut off for non-payment.

**House fires, third**, from space heaters and fireplaces, and from thawing pipes with open flame, which
people genuinely do.

**Then the pipes**, which are the money.

A pipe bursts because water expands as it freezes, and the split is usually not where the ice is — the
ice plug traps water between itself and a closed tap, and pressure builds until the weakest point fails.
That is why the drip protocol works: an open path means no pressure build-up.

The vulnerable runs are always the same: pipes in unheated attics, crawl spaces, garages, exterior walls
and unheated basements; outdoor spigots and irrigation lines; and anything in a vacant or unheated
property. A half-inch supply line running through an uninsulated attic in a house heated to sixty degrees
can freeze at an outdoor temperature well above zero if the wind is right.

**And the flood happens on the thaw**, continuously, until someone shuts the water off. An unoccupied
house can lose everything from one split pipe over three days.

## Before

- **34 — pipe freeze prevention**, and in the South this is the protection to do this year. Foam sleeve
  insulation on every accessible pipe in an unheated space is cheap, takes an afternoon and is cut with a
  knife. Heat tape on the runs that need it, installed to the manufacturer's instruction because badly
  installed heat tape is a fire risk. Insulate the outdoor spigots and disconnect hoses, because a hose
  left connected holds water against the valve.
- **35 — shut-off literacy**, and here it is rank two for a reason: the difference between a five hundred
  dollar repair and a fifty thousand dollar one is how many minutes the water ran. Find the main, label
  it, put the right tool on it, and make sure at least two people in the household know it. Know the
  water heater's shut-off too.
- **26 — the warm room**, chosen and set up before the season.
- **32, 47 — generator siting and CO alarms**, in date, on every level, and outside sleeping areas.
- **28 — sleeping bags rated for the temperature**, per person. In a house at forty degrees this is what
  makes the night survivable.
- **27 — window insulation.** Plastic film kits, thermal curtains, and a rolled towel at the bottom of
  exterior doors. Cheap, ugly, effective.
- **31 — insulation generally**, especially in the attic hatch and the rim joists, which is where the heat
  actually leaves.
- **13, 1 — pantry and water**, plus **12 — freeze protection for the stored water**, because a garage
  full of frozen water bottles is not a water store.
- **16 — off-grid cooking, outdoors only.** This is where the CO rule is most often broken, because it is
  cold outside and the grill is right there.
- **39, 38 — enough power to run the furnace blower**, which is frequently a modest requirement and the
  single highest-value load in this hazard. Find out the figure for your furnace.
- **Service the heating before winter**, and if you have a fireplace or wood stove intended as backup,
  have the chimney swept and the flue checked.
- **Know your utility's rules.** Most states restrict winter disconnection for non-payment and have
  low-income heating assistance, and medical certificates can add protection. This belongs in the state
  directory, and it matters most for the households most at risk.

**If you own a property that sits empty in winter** — a rental between tenants, a second home, a
relative's house — the freeze plan is different: keep it heated to at least fifty-five degrees, or
properly winterise it by shutting the water off and draining the system. Half measures are how empty
houses flood.

## During

**On a hard freeze warning.**

Insulate anything you have not got to. Disconnect hoses. Cover the outdoor spigots. Open the cupboard
doors under sinks on exterior walls so household heat reaches the pipes. Set the thermostat no lower than
about sixty-five and do not turn it down at night, which is exactly the wrong economy in this weather.

**Drip the taps** — a slow, steady trickle from the taps furthest from where the water enters, and on any
run you know is exposed. Both hot and cold. The cost of the water is trivial against a burst pipe.

Open the attic hatch a little if plumbing runs up there, and if you have a crawl space with pipes,
close its vents.

**Charge everything, fill water containers, get the prescriptions and set up the warm room**, because in
this hazard the freeze and the outage are correlated: the grid is under peak load precisely when it is
coldest.

**When the power goes.**

Close the house down to the warm room. Layer clothing rather than trying to heat air. Eat and drink —
metabolism is heat, and people forget to eat when they are stressed and cold.

**Nothing that burns fuel comes indoors.** No exceptions, no "just for a few minutes", no "the garage
door is open."

**Then make the plumbing decision.** If the house is going to drop below freezing and you are staying with
it, keep the taps dripping. **If you are leaving, shut the water main off and drain the system** — open
every tap, flush the toilets, and drain the water heater if you can. It takes twenty minutes and it is the
difference between coming home to a cold house and coming home to a ruined one.

**Watch for hypothermia and act on it early**, because it impairs the judgment needed to recognise it.
Shivering that stops is a late and serious sign. Protection 63 covers rewarming, and the decision that
matters is when to leave: agree an indoor temperature in advance below which the household goes to a
warming centre, a relative or a hotel, and go while everyone can still make decisions and drive.

**If a tap stops running while it is freezing**, you have a frozen pipe and probably a few hours before it
splits. Leave that tap open and warm the pipe along its length with a hair dryer, a heat lamp or towels
soaked in hot water, working from the tap end back. **Never an open flame** — every winter someone burns a
house down thawing a pipe with a torch. If you cannot find or reach it, shut the main off and call a
plumber.

---

## After

**The thaw is the flood.** Be in the house as it warms if you possibly can. Walk every level, the
basement, the crawl space and the attic. Listen for running water with everything switched off, and look
at the water meter — a meter turning with every tap closed means water is escaping somewhere.

**If a pipe has burst:** water main off first, then power off to the affected area if water is anywhere
near electrics, then get the water out and start drying. Photograph everything before you move it. Then
call the insurer — this is a covered peril under standard homeowner policies in most cases, unlike almost
every other water loss in this book, though a policy can exclude freeze damage in a property left
unheated or unwinterised, which is exactly why the vacant-property rule above matters.

**Dry it properly.** Same as the flood playbooks: air moving, cavities open, dehumidifiers, and a moisture
check before closing anything up. A burst pipe inside a wall soaks framing and insulation that looks fine
from the room.

**Check what else froze.** Outdoor spigots and irrigation backflow preventers frequently split and only
reveal themselves when the system is next pressurised in spring. Well heads and pump houses. Septic
lines. Swimming pool equipment. The washing machine supply lines in a garage.

**Check the heating system before trusting it.** A furnace that ran continuously for four days, or one
that was off in a house that dropped below freezing, deserves a look — especially condensing furnaces,
whose condensate lines freeze and shut them down.

**Then look after the people.** Days of cold and stress in a dark house takes a real toll, particularly on
older people, and the medical consequences show up afterwards rather than during.

**And the after-action that pays**, because this hazard recurs and the fixes are cheap: which pipes
froze, where the heat escaped, whether the warm room worked, whether the CO alarms were in date, whether
anyone could find the water main. Write it on the annual review, and do the insulation in the summer when
the shops have it.
