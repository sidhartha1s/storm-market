# Part Three, Playbook 4 — Riverine and Coastal Flooding

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

The Mississippi and Missouri flood of 1993 ran **from May through September**, across nine states and
about **400,000 square miles**. Fifty people died and the damage approached **$15 billion** in the dollars
of the time. **Over 1,000 levees, federal and non-federal, were topped or failed.** At one point roughly
**600 river forecast points were above flood stage simultaneously** and nearly 150 rivers and tributaries
were affected.

But the number that defines this hazard is the duration: **at some locations the river stayed above flood
stage for nearly 200 days.** Not a night. Not a weekend. Two-thirds of a year. Ten commercial airports
flooded, all Midwestern rail traffic halted, and numerous water and sewage treatment plants were
destroyed — which is how a river flood becomes a water-supply emergency for households that never got
wet.

The weather service's own explanation of why it was so severe is worth holding on to, because it is the
signature to watch for: not one storm but **rain that would not stop.** Many locations had rain on twenty
or more days in July against a normal of eight or nine, and in parts of the upper Mississippi basin there
was measurable rain **on every single day from late June to late July.**
«SRC: Lee W. Larson, NOAA National Weather Service, "The Great USA Flood of 1993" (Destructive Water:
Water-Caused Natural Disasters — Their Abatement and Control, IAHS Publication no. 239, 1996) — May
through September 1993, nine states, 400,000 square miles, 50 deaths, damages approaching $15 billion,
over 1,000 federal and non-federal levees topped or failed, ~600 forecast points above flood stage
simultaneously, nearly 150 rivers affected, some locations above flood stage nearly 200 days; rain on 20+
days in July against a normal of 8-9; ten commercial airports flooded, all Midwest rail halted, numerous
water and sewage treatment plants destroyed. | https://www.nwrfc.noaa.gov/floods/papers/oh_2/great.htm |
retrieved 2026-07-29»

It is the opposite hazard to the previous playbook in every respect except the water. Flash flooding is
minutes; this is days to weeks of forecast, which sounds like an advantage and produces its own specific
failure: **people watch the river rise for four days and still have not moved anything upstairs.**

Slow-onset floods are lost through disbelief and delay, not surprise. The forecast says the crest will
be a foot above your floor level on Thursday. It is Monday. Everyone can see the number. And the
household waits, because acting means admitting the number is real.

The second failure mode is structural and invisible: **living behind a levee or below a dam feels like
being outside the flood zone, and is not.** Protection depends on a structure with a design limit, a
maintenance history and an owner. When it is exceeded or it fails, the water arrives faster and deeper
than an unprotected floodplain's would, because it has been held back.
«CITE: a federal source on the number of US dams rated high-hazard-potential and/or the accredited-levee
population, with the date of the assessment.»

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Killed by:** the same vehicle pattern as flash flooding, on roads that flood gradually and get driven
anyway; by entering flooded structures; and — the distinct one here — by the **duration**. A
long-duration flood is a long-duration displacement, and the harm shows up as interrupted medical care,
lost medication, heat or cold in inadequate temporary accommodation, and cardiac events during a cleanup
that goes on for weeks.

**Levee and dam failure** is the exception to the slowness. When the structure goes, the event becomes a
flash flood with more water in it, and the warning may be minutes.

**Costs:** this is the most expensive hazard per affected household in the book, for two reasons.
Prolonged submersion destroys everything it touches, including the structure itself in a way a brief
inundation does not. And it is the least insured — standard policies exclude flood, and households
outside mapped high-risk zones overwhelmingly do not carry it.

Then the cost that surprises people: **the substantial damage rule, and the number is 50 per cent.**
Federal regulation defines *substantial damage* as *"damage of any origin sustained by a structure
whereby the cost of restoring the structure to its before damaged condition would equal or exceed 50
percent of the market value of the structure before the damage occurred."*

What it triggers is the part that costs money. A structure that has taken substantial damage is treated
as a **substantial improvement** — the definition says so explicitly, *"regardless of the actual repair
work performed"* — and substantial improvements in a mapped floodplain must be brought up to current
standards, which for most households means **elevating the building** rather than repairing the ground
floor. Note two things about that. **"Damage of any origin"** means the trigger is not flood-specific;
fire, wind and flood damage all count toward it. And it is **market value of the structure**, not the
purchase price and not the land — so an older, modest building crosses the threshold on a surprisingly
small repair bill.

That is a good rule for the long run and a large unbudgeted number in the moment. It is also why the
damage assessment your local floodplain administrator performs after an event matters as much as the
insurance adjuster's, and why keeping your own documented estimates is worth the effort.
«SRC: 44 CFR 59.1 (NFIP definitions) — "Substantial damage means damage of any origin sustained by a
structure whereby the cost of restoring the structure to its before damaged condition would equal or
exceed 50 percent of the market value of the structure before the damage occurred"; "substantial
improvement" includes structures that have incurred substantial damage regardless of the actual repair
work performed. | https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-44/section-59.1 | retrieved 2026-07-29»

## Before

- **103 — flood insurance**, with the thirty-day clock, and a clear understanding that structure and
  contents are separately purchased. This playbook is the one where its absence is most likely to be
  financially permanent.
- **Find out what is upstream of you.** Which dam, which levee, who owns it, what its condition rating
  is, and whether your county's emergency plan includes an inundation map. This is public information
  and almost nobody has looked at it.
- **92 — your gauge**, and here it is genuinely predictive: read the river forecast, which projects a
  crest height and a date. Learn what stage corresponds to water at your floor level. That single number
  converts a forecast into a decision.
- **81 — elevate the mechanicals**: furnace, water heater, electrical panel, air handler. In a repeat-
  flood house this is the highest-value structural spend, because it is the difference between a wet
  ground floor and a house with no heat, power or hot water for two months.
- **80 — flood openings and wet floodproofing** for garages and crawl spaces: let water in and out
  deliberately rather than letting pressure destroy the walls.
- **79 — grading**, and where relevant, permanent barriers and closures. Sandbags are a last resort, not
  a plan, and filling them takes far more people and hours than anyone estimates.
- **36 — sump with battery backup**, **82 — backwater valve**, because a river at flood stage backs the
  storm and sanitary systems up regardless of what is happening at your door.
- **106, 111 — routes and destinations**, chosen knowing that a regional flood closes roads in
  unpredictable combinations and that a route can be cut behind you.
- **96, 97, 100 — the paper protections**, done properly, because a long displacement means running your
  life from a folder for months.

**The one decision to make in advance:** at what forecast crest do you move out rather than up? Write it
as a number, on the form in the binder. It is the same trigger-plan logic as Protection 95, applied to a
gauge reading instead of a warning.

## During

The advantage here is days, so this reads as a schedule rather than a decision tree.

**When the river forecast first shows a problem.** Believe it. Note the projected crest and compare it
with your floor level. Start the log.

**Three to four days out.** Move upward, in priority order: documents and irreplaceables first, then
electronics, then furniture, then everything else. Put what matters on the top floor, not on blocks on
the ground floor — the difference between four inches and fourteen is a forecast revision.

**Two days out.** Fuel the vehicles and move them to high ground. Fill water containers, because
treatment plants flood too and boil notices follow river floods reliably. Refill prescriptions.
Photograph the whole property, inside and out. Do the video inventory again if it is stale.

**One day out.** Shut down and disconnect what is staying: gas appliances at the appliance, power at the
breaker if water will reach the panel or the outlets, and the water main. Empty the fridge and freezer of
anything that will rot for a month, because a house full of spoiled food is a remediation problem on top
of a flood.

**Decide, and go, before the roads close.** Leaving with the water already on the road is how a slow
flood kills people, and it makes no sense at all after four days of notice.

**If you are staying because your building is genuinely above the projected crest**, understand what
staying means: no power, no water, no sewer, no shops, no access, possibly for a fortnight, with water
around you. That is a supply question — Protections 1, 13, 37 to 46, 53 to 55 — and it is a decision
about medical dependency more than about bravery.

**If a levee or dam fails** while you are anywhere below it, that is no longer this playbook. Move to
high ground immediately, do not drive along the floodplain, and do not stop to collect anything.

## After

**Return when the area is opened, not when the water leaves.** A structure that has been submerged for a
week is a different structure — foundations undermined, walls loaded, floors delaminating.

**Approach it as an inspection.** Look at the foundation and the walls from outside before entering.
Look for scour around the footings, for a wall out of plumb, for a floor that has lifted. If anything
looks structural, get it looked at before you go in. Buildings kill people in the week after a flood.

**Power and gas stay off** until inspected. Anything submerged is condemned as an electrical component:
outlets, switches, the panel, the furnace, the water heater, the air handler, and every appliance with
a motor or a board in it. This is not an area for optimism — a dried-out submerged appliance is a fire.

**Assume the water supply is unsafe.** Boil notices follow river floods almost universally
(Protection 8). Private wells that have been inundated need shock chlorination and testing before use
(Protection 9), and "it looks clear" means nothing.

**The contamination is worse here than in a flash flood** because the water has been sitting in
everything: sewage, agricultural runoff, fuel, and industrial residue from wherever upstream flooded.
Full protective equipment, and no pressure-washing contaminated residue into the air or the garden.

**Discard the porous, save the hard.** Carpet, mattresses, upholstery, particleboard, insulation and
wallboard to above the water line all go. Solid timber, masonry, tile and metal can usually be cleaned,
disinfected and dried. Open the wall cavities — water inside a wall that looks dry outside is the single
most common cause of the mould problem that arrives two months later.

**Dry it properly and prove it.** Dehumidifiers, air movement and time, and if you can, a moisture meter
before closing anything up. Rebuilding onto wet framing is the expensive mistake.

**The money sequence.** Flood claim on the flood policy if you have one, sewer backup on the endorsement,
homeowner policy for anything wind-related, and federal individual assistance separately — which is
where the gap from Part One is realest. Register, keep the log, keep every receipt including lodging and
mileage. If the repair looks close to the substantial-damage threshold, find out early what your local
ordinance requires, because it changes the whole project and there are mitigation grant programmes that
exist precisely for that case.

**Then the honest long question.** A house that has flooded twice will flood again. Elevation, a
buyout, or relocation are real options with real programmes behind them, and the moment to look at them
is during the rebuild rather than after the third flood.
