# Part Three, Playbook 20 — Water and Sanitation Failure

Target: 8 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

In August 2022 the water treatment system serving Jackson, Mississippi failed after flooding on the Pearl
River compounded years of deferred maintenance. A city of roughly one hundred and fifty thousand people lost
safe running water — not for a day, but for weeks, on top of a boil notice that had already been in effect
for a month.
«CITE: authoritative record of the 2022 Jackson, Mississippi water crisis — the duration of the outage and
the boil notice, and the population affected.»

Then February 2021 in Texas produced the same outcome from a completely different cause: pumps without
power, mains broken by freezing, pressure lost, and boil notices affecting millions of people across the
state at once.
«CITE: TCEQ figures for the number of public water systems and people under boil-water notices after the
February 2021 storm.»

**The point of this playbook is that the water goes off in almost every hazard in this book, and households
plan for it least.** People buy a generator before they buy a water container, which is exactly backwards:
you can live without electricity for a fortnight and you cannot live without water for three days.

And the half nobody wants to discuss at all is the second half. **When the water stops, the toilet stops**,
and a household of four generates a sanitation problem within hours. That is not a comfort issue. In a
prolonged event, in a household with no water for handwashing, it is the most likely way somebody gets
genuinely ill.

---

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Dehydration** is the obvious mechanism and the least common in the American context, because water is
almost always available somewhere within a few days.

**Gastrointestinal illness is the real one**, and it comes from the interaction of contaminated water,
inadequate handwashing and improper waste handling. In an event where a household is already stressed,
possibly cut off, and possibly without medical access, a norovirus outbreak or a bacterial infection running
through a family is a serious problem — and for infants, elderly people and the immunocompromised it is
dangerous.

**The specific contamination routes** after a disaster: mains losing pressure and drawing in contamination
through breaks; sewage entering surface water and flooding wells; treatment plants losing power or capacity;
premise plumbing sitting stagnant for weeks, which produces its own bacterial growth including Legionella
risk in buildings that were shut down; and the chemical routes — fuel, solvents, agricultural runoff and, in
some post-wildfire cases, benzene drawn into damaged distribution systems.

**Then the mistakes people make with treatment**, which cause real harm:

**Boiling handles microbes and nothing else.** It concentrates nitrate, arsenic, lead and cyanotoxins from
algal blooms. Under a **do-not-drink** notice, boiling makes the water worse, not better.

**A filter rated for bacteria and protozoa may do nothing for viruses or chemicals.** Filters have a
specification and it is on the box.

**Bleach dosing is easy to get wrong**, and both directions are bad: too little leaves the water unsafe, too
much is a chemical exposure. Scented, gel and cleaning-additive bleaches must never be used.

**Costs:** low in dollars, high in misery. Bottled water at panic prices, plumbing repair, and — in the
Legionella case in a large building — a serious liability and remediation problem.

## Before

- **1 — the water store**, and the sizing done honestly: roughly a gallon per person per day for drinking and
  basic hygiene, and more in heat, for children, for nursing mothers and for pets. Two weeks is the number to
  aim at. This is the highest-priority purchase in this book for most households and it costs almost nothing.
- **2 — rotation and date-marking**, so the store is real rather than archaeological.
- **3 — the pre-fill protocol.** Free, and it doubles or triples your store in fifteen minutes: fill the
  bathtubs, every pot, every bottle, and any bladder you own, at the point a warning is issued. A tub liner
  bladder is a cheap upgrade because a bathtub itself is not a clean container.
- **4 — the water heater as a reserve**, forty to eighty gallons already in the house, with the draw-off
  procedure known in advance — power or gas off to it first, then the drain valve, and a tap opened somewhere
  to break the vacuum.
- **6 — filter selection**, chosen against what you might actually face: a gravity filter or hollow-fibre
  unit for microbes, and the honest understanding that chemical contamination needs a different technology or
  bottled water.
- **7 — chemical disinfection**, with the correct ratios for unscented household bleach written on a card
  taped inside the cupboard rather than recalled from memory, and dosing tablets as a compact alternative.
- **8 — the boil protocol**, and knowing the three notices apart: **boil water**, **do not drink**, and **do
  not use**.
- **9, 10 — the well owner's plan**: no power means no water, so a hand pump or a generator-fed pump matters;
  and shock chlorination plus testing after any flooding.
- **12 — freeze protection** for stored water, because a garage full of frozen bottles is not a store.
- **53 — the twin-bucket toilet.** Two buckets, heavy liners, a well-fitting lid, and a dry cover material —
  sawdust, wood shavings, peat, cat litter or shredded paper. One bucket for solids, one for liquids, because
  separating them is what controls the smell almost entirely. Build it before you need it; it costs about the
  price of a takeaway.
- **54 — waste containment and lawful disposal**, agreed in advance, because the question of where the bags
  go is the part people have not thought about.
- **55 — hand hygiene with no running water**: alcohol gel at sixty percent or above, plus a tippy-tap or a
  jug-and-basin routine, plus soap. Gel does not work on visibly dirty hands and it does not work well
  against norovirus, which is precisely the pathogen most likely to run through a household — so soap and
  water, from a jug, is the primary method and the gel is the backup.
- **A wrench on the water main** (Protection 35), because in a contamination event you may want to isolate
  the house.

**And the free thing worth doing today:** find out where your water comes from and who to call, and sign up
for your utility's alerts. Boil notices are announced by the utility and the county, and households
frequently learn about them days late from a neighbour.

## During

**On a warning, before anything happens: fill everything.** Protection 3. This is fifteen minutes of work
that changes the whole event, and it is the most-skipped free action in this book.

**When pressure drops or a notice is issued.**

**Stop drinking from the tap immediately**, and remember all the ways you use it without thinking: ice from
the ice maker, the water dispenser in the fridge door, coffee machines and their internal reservoirs,
toothbrushing, rinsing salad, filling a pet bowl, and a child in a bath. Turn the ice maker off and discard
the ice already made.

**Then find out which notice you are under**, because the response is different:

**Boil water.** Bring to a rolling boil for one minute — three minutes at high altitude — and cool. This
handles bacteria, viruses and protozoa.

**Do not drink.** Boiling will not help and may hurt. Bottled water only, for drinking, cooking,
toothbrushing, ice and food preparation.

**Do not use.** Also do not bathe or wash in it, because absorption or inhalation is the concern. Follow the
utility's instruction exactly.

**Ration in priority order**: drinking first, then food preparation, then hygiene, then everything else. Do
not let the dishes and the laundry consume drinking water — use paper and disposables while the shortage
lasts, which is a rare case where this book recommends the wasteful option.

**Sanitation, from the moment the water is off.**

If the sewer or septic system is intact and you have any water at all, you can flush with non-potable
water — a bucket poured briskly into the bowl. Tub water, pool water and rainwater all work for this, and it
is the best reason to fill the tub.

**If the sewer is broken, or the system is flooded, or the treatment plant has failed, do not flush at
all.** Sewage will back up into the house, which converts a water problem into a contamination event inside
your living space. Go to the twin-bucket system. Liquids in one, solids in the other, cover material after
each use, lid on. Bag the solids and store them sealed outside, away from the house and away from animals,
until you know where they can lawfully go — your county will say.

**Handwashing is the protection that decides whether anybody gets ill.** Soap and running water from a jug,
after every toilet use and before every food contact, every time, for everyone including the children who
will not want to. This is not fussiness. It is the intervention.

**Then watch for illness** and treat it seriously: diarrhoea and vomiting in an event with limited water
causes dehydration quickly, especially in children and elderly people. Oral rehydration solution belongs in
the medical kit (Protection 57), and a child who cannot keep fluids down needs medical help rather than
patience.

## After

**Follow the notice until it is formally lifted**, not until the water looks clear. Utilities lift notices
after testing, and the gap between "water is flowing again" and "water is safe" is frequently days.

**Then flush the plumbing properly**, because your own pipes are now the problem. After a pressure loss or a
long shutdown, run every cold tap for several minutes until the water is cold and clear, starting with the
tap nearest the supply and working outward. Then run the hot taps. Flush and clean the ice maker — discard
the first several batches — and run the dishwasher and washing machine empty. Replace point-of-use filters,
including the fridge filter, because a filter that processed contaminated water is now a contaminated
filter.

**In a building that was unoccupied for weeks, take stagnation seriously.** Warm, still water in premise
plumbing grows bacteria including Legionella, and the exposure route is inhaled aerosol from showers and
taps rather than drinking. Flush thoroughly, consider raising the water heater temperature per guidance
before flushing, and avoid creating spray during the first flush — open taps gently, and remove and clean
aerators and shower heads.
«CITE: CDC or ASHRAE guidance on flushing building water systems after prolonged low use, including the
Legionella risk and the recommended procedure.»

**If you are on a well and it was flooded:** do not use it. Shock chlorinate, flush, and **test** before
drinking — testing is the only way to know, and county health departments and commercial labs both do it.
Repeat the test after a few weeks, because contamination can persist and recur.

**Discard what was contaminated:** food and drink that contacted floodwater or unsafe water, ice, and
anything porous that was in it (Protection 21).

**Then re-stock the store and the sanitation kit immediately**, and note what actually ran out. In almost
every household after a water event the answer is the same: not enough stored water, no plan for the toilet,
and no soap arrangement that worked without a tap. All three are cheap, and all three are on the annual
review sheet.
