# Part Five, Chapter Two — Documents, Money and Insurance

Protections 96–105 · Target: 14 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

## Documents, money and insurance

This is the chapter that decides how the next two years of your life go, and it is almost entirely
free.

Nothing here protects a body. Everything here protects the recovery — and the recovery is where the
permanent damage happens. A household that comes through the event physically fine and then loses
eighty thousand dollars of uninsured, undocumented, unclaimable loss has had a worse decade than a
household that lost a roof and could prove what was under it.

The gap from Part One holds: the average federal award is a few thousand dollars against a statutory
maximum of tens of thousands, and where you land is decided almost entirely by proof — proof that has
to exist **before** the event, because afterwards it is under water or in a skip.

Week four of the sixty-day sequence is this chapter, and it is placed deliberately before every week
that costs money.

---

### 96 · The document vault

**Prevents:** losing your identity, your title, and your policy in the same hour as your house.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Materials:** a phone and cloud storage
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 9 Tornado Alley and Hurricane Coast.

Photograph or scan everything that would be difficult to replace: driver's licences and passports,
birth and marriage certificates, deeds and titles, vehicle registrations, insurance policies with the
declarations page, mortgage and loan documents, tax returns, prescriptions and the medication list,
immunisation and pet vaccination records, professional licences, wills and powers of attorney, account
numbers, and photographs of the household including the pets — because a lost pet is found with a
photograph, not a description.

Then the rule that makes it a vault rather than a folder: **three copies, two places, one off-site.**
Encrypted cloud storage, a local drive, and something that physically leaves the house — a relative's
safe, a deposit box, or a drive in the go-bag.

Give one trusted out-of-area person access, or at least the knowledge that it exists and how to reach
it. The person who needs these documents may be the person in the hospital.

**What this does not do:** a photograph of a document is not always legally equivalent to the document.
It is enough to start almost every claim, replacement and application, which is what matters at the
time.

### 97 · The grab-and-go document envelope

**Prevents:** needing a laptop and a signal to prove who you are.
**Cost:** $5 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **Materials:** a waterproof envelope
· **Regions:** all five.

Digital fails in exactly the conditions you built it for: no power, no signal, no charge. So a
physical envelope lives with the go-bag, waterproof, containing paper copies of the essentials —
identification, the insurance declarations page with the policy number and the claims phone number,
the medication list, the medical summary, the household contact sheet, and some cash.

Add a printed copy of the completed communication plan from the binder. In a shelter, on paper, with a
dead phone, this envelope is the difference between being a person with a plan and a person answering
questions from memory.

**What this does not do:** paper is not secure. This envelope contains enough to enable identity theft
if lost, so it lives inside the bag rather than clipped to the outside, and it does not contain
passwords.

### 98 · Cash, in small bills

**Prevents:** having money and no way to spend it.
**Cost:** the cash · **Time:** one bank visit · **Materials:** small denominations
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 8 Quake West.

In a regional outage, card terminals fail, ATMs fail, and the ones still working have queues and empty
hoppers. Cash works.

**Small bills specifically**, because a shop running on a calculator and a cash box cannot change a
large note, and because you may be paying twenty dollars for something rather than two hundred. A mix
of fives, tens and twenties, plus some coins for vending and payphones where they still exist.

Keep it somewhere fire-resistant and separate from the go-bag's outer pocket, with a smaller amount in
the go-bag itself. Decide the amount from how many days of fuel, food and lodging you would need to
cover — and then rotate it, because cash sitting for a decade tends to get spent.

**What this does not do:** cash does nothing if the shops are shut or the roads are closed, and it is
not a substitute for having the supplies. It buys what still exists.

### 99 · Payment redundancy

**Prevents:** every payment method failing the same way at once.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 15 minutes · **Materials:** a second card, a written list
· **Regions:** all five.

Carry more than one card, from more than one issuer, and do not keep them together. Know at least one
account number and one customer service number on paper, because your banking app requires the phone
you no longer have.

Two practical points people learn the hard way. **Tell your bank you have evacuated** if you can —
fraud systems flag out-of-area spending exactly when you are spending out of area, and a frozen card
in an evacuation is a genuine problem. And **know your withdrawal limits**, because they are lower
than most people assume.

**What this does not do:** payment redundancy is not liquidity. If the network is down regionally,
every card fails, which is why Protection 98 exists in front of this one.

### 100 · The home inventory, by video

**Prevents:** the loss you cannot prove, which is the only part of the gap you control.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **Materials:** a phone
· **Regions:** all five. **Rank 10 in every single region — the one action that pays in every hazard.**

Twenty minutes, phone in hand, narrating.

Walk every room. Open every drawer, cupboard and closet, and say out loud what things are, roughly when
you got them, and roughly what they cost. Do the garage, the basement, the shed, the loft. Open the
wardrobe and pan slowly across the clothes. Film the contents of the freezer. Film the tools.

Narration is what makes this work — an adjuster reviewing footage of an open drawer with a voice saying
"that's the good cutlery, we bought it in 2019" is being handed evidence, not asked to guess.

Then get it off the phone tonight, into the same vault as Protection 96, because a video of your
possessions that burns with your possessions is not an inventory.

Redo it once a year and after anything significant arrives.

**What this does not do:** a video is not a valuation and does not establish the amount you are owed.
It establishes **existence**, which is the thing that is otherwise impossible to prove and the thing
insurers and aid programmes will not simply take your word for.

### 101 · Serial numbers and receipts

**Prevents:** arguing about the value of the expensive things.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 15 minutes · **Materials:** a phone
· **Regions:** all five.

For anything over a few hundred dollars, photograph the make, model and serial number — usually on a
plate or sticker. Furnace, air handler, water heater, electrical panel, appliances, televisions,
computers, tools, bicycles, instruments, cameras.

Then keep the receipts you already get: forward digital ones to a dedicated email folder, photograph
paper ones. It costs seconds each and it converts "a television" into a specific model with a purchase
date and a price.

Anything unusual — jewellery, art, collections, firearms, instruments — may need a professional
appraisal and may need a specific policy endorsement, because standard policies cap those categories
far lower than people expect. That is the next protection.

**What this does not do:** a serial number does not prove ownership on its own. It works together with
the inventory and the receipts, which is why the three protections are consecutive.

### 102 · The insurance audit

**Prevents:** discovering the exclusion after the loss.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 45 minutes · **Materials:** your declarations page
· **Regions:** all five.

Get the declarations page and find five things. Write them on the form in the binder, because a
household that knows these five numbers behaves differently.

**The dwelling limit**, and whether it is anywhere near what rebuilding actually costs now —
construction costs have moved sharply and a policy that has drifted is underinsured without anyone
noticing.

**The contents limit**, and critically whether contents are **replacement cost or actual cash value**.
Actual cash value pays depreciated value, which on a ten-year-old household means a fraction of what
replacement costs. This single line is the largest surprise in most claims.

**The deductibles** — plural, because wind, hail and hurricane frequently carry a separate and much
larger deductible, sometimes a percentage of the dwelling limit rather than a flat sum. A two percent
hurricane deductible on a large policy is a five-figure number.

**The exclusions.** Flood is excluded from standard homeowner policies, always, and that surprises
people every single year. Sewer and drain backup is usually excluded unless a specific endorsement is
added, and that endorsement is typically inexpensive. Earthquake is separate. Mould is often capped.

**Loss of use** — whether the policy pays for you to live somewhere else, and up to what limit and for
how long. This is the coverage that determines your quality of life during the recovery and almost
nobody reads it.

Then call your agent with a single question: *if my house were destroyed tomorrow, what would this
policy not cover?* Take notes.

**What this does not do:** an audit is not a purchase. It tells you what you have. Fixing gaps costs
money and some of it is worth spending — but knowing is free and comes first.

### 103 · Flood insurance, and the waiting period

**Prevents:** buying flood cover the week you need it, which does not work.
**Cost:** varies widely · **Time:** an hour · **Materials:** a quote
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast rank 5, and every flood-exposed household anywhere.

Two facts, and the second is why this protection is urgent rather than eventual.

**Flood is not covered by your homeowner policy.** Not partially, not by accident. If water arrives
from outside the building, a standard policy does not respond.

**A new flood policy does not take effect for thirty days.** The rule is federal regulation, not
company policy: 44 CFR 61.11(d) sets the effective time of any new policy, added coverage or increase
at **12:01 a.m. on the 30th calendar day after the application date and payment of premium.** The
regulation gives its own worked example — apply and pay on 1 May, cover begins 12:01 a.m. on 31 May.

**The exceptions are narrow and worth knowing exactly, because they are the only ways to move faster:**

- **Buying in connection with a loan** — making, increasing, extending or renewing it. Cover is
  effective **at the loan closing**, provided the application and premium are in at or before closing.
- **The 13 months after your community's flood map is revised.** During that window initial coverage
  starts at **12:01 a.m. the day after** you apply and pay. One day, not thirty.
- **Post-wildfire flooding on federal land.** Where FEMA's Administrator has determined the property is
  affected by flooding on federal land caused or worsened by post-wildfire conditions, and the policy is
  bought **within 60 days of the fire containment date**, cover again starts the next day.

And one procedural trap in the same section: the waiting period runs from the **date of receipt**, not
the date of application, unless the application and full payment reach the NFIP **within 10 calendar
days** — or are sent by **certified mail within 4 calendar days.** Otherwise your thirty days starts
later than you think it does.
«SRC: 44 CFR § 61.11, "Effective date and time of coverage under the Standard Flood Insurance
Policy — New Business Applications and Endorsements." |
https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-44/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-61/section-61.11 |
retrieved 2026-07-29»

The other thing people get wrong is the assumption that flood risk equals a mapped high-risk zone.

Here the book has to correct a number you will see everywhere. A great many articles claim that "over
40 percent" of flood claims come from outside high-risk areas. That figure could not be traced to any
FEMA document, so it is not in this book. What is available is FEMA's own claims dataset, and it can be
counted: across all **2.72 million NFIP claims on record, 25.2 percent were on policies rated in the
non-special-hazard zones B, C or X.** Restricting to losses from 2015 onward, it is **28.4 percent.**

So: **roughly one flood claim in four, and rising toward three in ten.** That is a smaller number than
the internet's, and it is still the point — because premiums outside the high-risk zone are much
cheaper, so a quarter of the claims are being paid on the cheap policies. For an inland household that
assumes it is not exposed, that ratio is the argument.
«SRC: Computed from FEMA's OpenFEMA NFIP Redacted Claims dataset (FimaNfipClaims), field
`ratedFloodZone`, queried 2026-07-29. All 2,721,780 claims on record: zones B, C and X (outside the
Special Flood Hazard Area) = 686,045, or 25.2 percent; zones A and V = 1,892,368, or 69.5 percent. For
losses from 2015 onward, 622,677 claims: B/C/X = 177,065, or 28.4 percent; A/V = 442,617, or 71.1
percent. |
https://www.fema.gov/api/open/v2/FimaNfipClaims?$inlinecount=allpages&$top=1&$filter=startswith(ratedFloodZone,'X') | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Get a quote. Understand what it covers, what it caps, and that contents are separate from structure.

**What this does not do:** flood insurance does not cover sewer backup, which is a separate
endorsement on the homeowner policy. Two different water problems, two different products, and
households often need both.

### 104 · Mitigation credits — asking for what you are owed

**Prevents:** paying full premium on a house you already hardened.
**Cost:** $0 to ask · **Time:** an hour, plus an inspection fee where applicable
· **Materials:** documentation of your upgrades · **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, hail regions.

Several states require insurers to discount premiums for verified wind-resistant construction, and
Florida's statute is worth quoting because it tells you precisely which upgrades are in scope. Fla.
Stat. § 627.0629 states the Legislature's intent that insurers "provide savings to consumers who
install or implement windstorm damage mitigation techniques," and requires that a residential property
rate filing **"must include actuarially reasonable discounts, credits, or other rate differentials, or
appropriate reductions in deductibles"** for construction demonstrated to reduce windstorm loss. The
statute then lists the categories, and they map almost exactly onto this book's Part Two chapter on
structure: **wind uplift prevention, roof strength, roof covering performance, roof-to-wall strength,
wall-to-floor-to-foundation strength, opening protection, and window, door and skylight strength.**

And since **1 October 2023** each insurer subject to the section **must publish its hurricane mitigation
discounts on its own website**, reachable from the home page or the property-policyholder page. That is
the document to go and read before you telephone anybody.
«SRC: Florida Statutes § 627.0629, "Residential property insurance; rate filings." |
https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2024/627.0629 | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Other states run their own schemes with their own minimums; your state's is in the Appendix B entry.

But here is the honest and useful part, and it is why this protection is a phone call rather than a
percentage: **the actual credit is specific to your carrier and your county, and it is published in
their filed rate schedule.** National figures quoted online are marketing copy from people selling
roofs and windows. So do not look up a percentage. **Call your carrier and ask for their mitigation
credit table.** It exists, it is theirs, and they will send it.

Then work the list against your house. Roof deck attachment, roof-to-wall connections, opening
protection, roof covering rating, roof geometry, secondary water barrier. Where a credit requires
verification, it usually requires a specific inspection form completed by an approved inspector — and
the inspection frequently pays for itself in the first year.

File the paperwork from every upgrade in Protection 96 as you do it, because a credit you cannot
document is a credit you do not get.

**What this does not do:** credits apply to the wind portion of the premium, which is a fraction of
the bill and varies enormously by location. The saving is real and it is not the whole premium.

### The state will pay for some of Part Two — a note on grants, and on what things cost

This is not a numbered protection, because it depends entirely on where you live. It is here because
**the most expensive protections in this book are the ones most likely to be part-funded by your
state**, and because the programmes that fund them also publish the only current, non-marketing cost
figures available for this work.

**Florida — My Safe Florida Home.** For a home built before 2008, the state runs a **free
wind-mitigation inspection** and then a matching grant: **$2 of state money for every $1 you put in, to
a maximum state contribution of $10,000.** The programme's own worked examples make the arithmetic
concrete: spend $9,000 on impact-rated windows and you are reimbursed $6,000; spend $15,000 and you are
reimbursed the $10,000 cap with $5,000 as your match. Low-income homeowners are not required to provide
matching funds at all, up to the same $10,000. **Eligibility is prioritised by income and age**, in four
groups from low-income over-60 downwards, and at the time of writing higher-income households are
eligible for inspections only. The order matters: **you cannot apply for the grant until the free
inspection has produced a report**, and the grant only pays for improvements that report recommends.

**And here is the cost table the same programme publishes**, which is as close to an honest current
price list for hurricane retrofit as exists in public:

| Upgrade | Estimated cost |
|---|---|
| Roof-to-wall reinforcement | $800 – $3,000 |
| Roof deck nailing upgrade | $1,000 – $4,000 |
| Secondary water barrier | $500 – $2,500 |
| Impact windows or shutters | $5,000 – $15,000 |
| Roof replacement | $8,000 – $20,000+ |

Those are Florida numbers, from a state programme, for Florida labour. Treat them as the shape of the
market rather than a quote for your street — but note that the three cheapest lines are also the three
the programme names as the most cost-effective, which is the same ranking this book's Part Two arrives at
from the structural side.

**California — Earthquake Brace + Bolt.** For a **wood-framed house built before 1980 on a raised
foundation**, in one of more than 1,100 eligible ZIP codes, EBB pays **up to $3,000** toward a
code-compliant seismic retrofit — bolting the frame to the foundation and bracing the cripple walls,
which is Protection 83's structural cousin and the single highest-return earthquake spend available to a
homeowner. **Income-eligible households earning $89,040 or less can apply for up to $7,000 more**, which
the state says covers most or all of a typical retrofit. Since 2013 the programme has funded more than
**32,500 retrofits**, and in 2025 it opened to **rental properties** for the first time. Registration
runs in a defined window each year rather than continuously — check the dates before you plan the work,
not after.

**Alabama — Strengthen Alabama Homes**, the model several other states have copied, pays **up to
$10,000** toward bringing an existing home to the IBHS FORTIFIED Roof or Silver standard, as a grant
rather than a loan, in specified coastal counties. Its real significance is what happens afterwards: the
state's insurance department publishes a **discount chart for FORTIFIED designations**, with reductions
on the wind portion of the premium ranging from **20 per cent for an older FORTIFIED Roof up to 50 per
cent for a new FORTIFIED Gold** house. That is the clearest published link in the country between doing
the work in Part Two and a number on your bill.

**Three honest caveats, and then the instruction.** These programmes are **funded by annual
appropriation**, so they open, close, run out of money and change their rules — every figure above is
current at the time of writing and none of it is a promise. They are **first-come, first-served**, which
means the useful action is registering early in a cycle rather than deciding in a hurricane watch. And
**they are geographically narrow**: three states are named here because their programmes publish real
numbers, not because they are the only ones. This book does not print a count of how many states run
something, because no authority publishes one — which is exactly why the instruction below is a search
rather than a list.

**So the instruction is one search, not a decision:** search your state's name with "wind mitigation
grant", "seismic retrofit grant" or "hazard mitigation grant program", and look for the state emergency
management agency or the state insurance department rather than a contractor's page. Your Appendix B
entry names the agencies. If a programme exists, the sequence is almost always the same — free or
subsidised inspection first, then a grant against what the inspection recommends — and doing the work
before you apply is the single most common way people disqualify themselves.

### A note on every cost figure in this book

**The cost line on each protection is a planning band, not a quote, and the bands are deliberately
wide.** For the cheap items — a torch, a filter, a helmet, a set of bins — the band is what the item
actually sells for across the range of quality worth buying, and the low end is usually adequate. For
the expensive structural items, the bands are anchored where possible to the state cost tables above,
which is the only current, non-commercial pricing published for this work.

**Two things move a structural number more than anything in this book can predict:** whether the work is
done *with* a re-roof or as a standalone job, which is frequently a difference of several thousand
dollars for identical work, and where you live. That is why the protections are written to tell you
**what to ask for by name** rather than what to pay. A specification you can put on a contract is worth
more than a price, because the price you will actually pay comes from three quotes against that
specification.

### 105 · Claim documentation discipline, starting the same hour

**Prevents:** cleaning up the evidence.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** ongoing · **Materials:** a phone, a notebook
· **Regions:** all five.

The instinct after damage is to clean up. It is a good instinct and it destroys your claim.

**Photograph and film everything before you touch it.** Wide shots that establish the room, then close
shots of specific damage, then the serial plates on anything destroyed. Film the water line on the wall
before it dries and becomes invisible. Photograph the contents of the freezer before you empty it.

Then do the things that protect the property from getting worse, because you are usually obliged to —
tarp the roof, board the opening, extract the water, run the dehumidifiers — and **document those too,
and keep every receipt.** Reasonable emergency mitigation costs are frequently reimbursable, and
almost nobody keeps the receipts.

Keep a running log: the date and time of every call, who you spoke to, what they said, what reference
number they gave. Claims are decided by people working from files, and the claimant with a dated log
is a different claimant.

Do not throw away damaged items until the adjuster has seen them or told you in writing that you may.
If something has to go for health reasons, photograph it thoroughly first — and note who told you it
had to go.

And do not sign anything a passing contractor puts in front of you. Post-disaster contractor fraud is
a documented, organised pattern, and the pressure to sign immediately is itself the warning sign.

**What this does not do:** documentation does not make an excluded loss covered. It makes a covered
loss provable, which is the difference between the average award and the maximum.
