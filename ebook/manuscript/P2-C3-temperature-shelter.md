## Temperature and shelter

This chapter answers two different questions that happen to use the same word. *Shelter* in a
tornado means the room that keeps debris out of your skull for ninety seconds. *Shelter* in a
February outage means the room that keeps your body above the temperature at which it stops working,
for four days. They are unrelated problems with unrelated solutions, and the only thing they share is
that both are decided in advance and both are free.

The unifying principle for the thermal half is **consolidate**. Do not condition a house. Condition a
room, and put the household in it.

---

### 23 · Safe room selection

**Prevents:** being in the wrong part of the house when the wind arrives.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 20 minutes, once · **Materials:** none
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 1 Tornado Alley.

You want the place with the most walls between you and the outside, on the lowest floor, with nothing
above it that can fall through. Usually a basement corner away from the windows, or an interior
bathroom, closet, or hallway.

Walk the lowest floor and count exterior walls in each candidate — you want zero. Look up, and rule
out anything under a piano, a water heater, a masonry chimney, or a car. Rule out rooms with windows;
a small high bathroom window is acceptable, a picture window is not. Then time the slowest person in
the house getting there. Over sixty seconds means pick a closer room. Say it out loud to everyone
tonight and write it on Form 4.

**What this does not do:** an interior room is not a shelter. It buys walls, not survival in a violent
tornado. In a mobile or manufactured home no room qualifies — your safe room is elsewhere, and you
leave on the watch, not the warning. See Protection 24.

### 24 · Above-ground safe rooms, and the standard to demand

**Prevents:** paying for a shelter that is not one.
**Cost:** $3,000–12,000 installed · **Time:** weeks · **Materials:** contractor
· **Regions:** Tornado Alley, Hurricane Coast.

If you live in a manufactured home, or in a slab-on-grade house in violent-tornado country, no
interior room will do and the honest answer is a purpose-built shelter — in-ground, garage-floor, or
a reinforced above-ground safe room.

The only thing that matters when buying one is the standard, and you should learn its name before you
call anybody. FEMA defines a safe room as a hardened structure built to its criteria to provide
**"near-absolute protection"** in extreme wind — meaning, in FEMA's own words, that occupants "will
have a very high probability of being protected from injury or death." The two documents are **FEMA
P-361** and the referenced consensus standard **ICC 500**, and the current editions are **FEMA P-361
(2021) and ICC 500-2020**. That is not a detail: FEMA's grace period allowing FEMA-funded prefabricated
residential safe rooms to comply with the older 2015/2014 editions **expired on 1 January 2024.**

So the sentence to say on the phone is: *"Is this unit tested, listed and labelled to ICC 500-2020, and
will you give me that documentation in writing?"* If the answer is vague, the answer is no.
«SRC: FEMA, "Safe Rooms" (Building Science) — near-absolute protection definition; FEMA P-361 (2021) and
ICC 500-2020 compliance requirement effective 1 January 2024. |
https://www.fema.gov/emergency-managers/risk-management/building-science/safe-rooms |
retrieved 2026-07-29» A steel box welded by a local fabricator with no design pedigree is a
purchase, not a protection.

Register it with your county emergency management office if they maintain a registry, so rescuers know
where to dig.

**What this does not do:** a shelter you cannot reach in time is decoration. If yours is detached from
the house, your trigger is the watch, not the warning — and that changes Protection 95, not this one.

### 25 · The helmet rule

**Prevents:** head injury from small, fast debris inside a room you have already chosen well.
**Cost:** $15–40 each · **Time:** none · **Materials:** bicycle or sports helmets
· **Regions:** Tornado Alley rank 2, Quake West, Hurricane Coast.

In CDC's review of all 338 deaths in the April 2011 southeastern outbreak, 95.6 percent of the
deaths were multisystem trauma and **21.9 percent of the death certificates noted a head injury.**
«SRC: "Tornado-Related Fatalities — Five States, Southeastern United States, April 25–28, 2011."
MMWR Morb Mortal Wkly Rep 2012;61(28):529–533. |
https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6128a3.htm | retrieved 2026-07-29» About one death in
five, then — not the majority, and this book is not going to inflate it into one.

CDC's recommendation on the back of that number is to keep **personal protection items, "e.g.,
blankets and helmets," in the safe room**, and that is exactly what this protection is. Keep them
in the room, not the garage: a helmet you have to find is a helmet you will not be wearing. Bicycle
helmets are adequate and already in most houses with children; buy for the adults.

The objection is always that it looks ridiculous. Fifteen dollars and thirty seconds of feeling
foolish against a one-in-five injury category is still an easy trade — but it is a trade at the
margin, on top of Protection 23, not instead of it.

**What this does not do:** **there is no evidence that helmets reduce tornado deaths.** CDC says so
plainly — *"currently, no data are available regarding the effectiveness of helmet use to prevent
head injuries during a tornado"* — and adds that *"choosing to use helmets to protect the head
should not be considered an alternative to seeking appropriate shelter."* A helmet does nothing
about structural collapse, nothing about large debris, and nothing about the 78 percent of these
deaths whose certificates did not mention the head. It is a cheap addition to a good room. Anyone
selling it to you as a substitute for the room is selling you something.

### 26 · Warm-room consolidation

**Prevents:** trying to heat a house you cannot heat.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Materials:** blankets, towels
· **Regions:** Ice & Snow North rank 3, Quake West at altitude.

Pick one room: interior, small, few windows, ideally with a south-facing window for daytime gain.
Everyone sleeps in it. Close doors to the rest of the house, and stuff towels along the bottoms.
Hang blankets over windows at night and open them to direct sun during the day. Cover bare floors.

Body heat is a real heat source in a small room — a family of four generates meaningful warmth, which
is why consolidating beats heating.

**What this does not do:** it does not stop pipes elsewhere in the house from freezing. Closing off
rooms accelerates that, which makes Protection 34 mandatory rather than optional whenever you do this.

### 27 · Window insulation

**Prevents:** losing through the glass what you are working to keep.
**Cost:** $15–60 per room · **Time:** an hour · **Materials:** film kit, or heavy blankets and tape
· **Regions:** Ice & Snow North, and any cold-climate rental.

Windows are the thinnest part of the envelope. An interior film kit costs little and works; heavy
blankets or quilts hung over the frame work nearly as well for a few nights and cost nothing if you
already own them. Bubble wrap misted onto the glass is the renter's version and is effective and
removable.

Do the warm room first, then the rest if there is time.

**What this does not do:** film does nothing for draughts around the frame — that is caulk and
weatherstrip, and it is a different job with a bigger return.

### 28 · Sleeping bags with a real rating

**Prevents:** discovering your bedding is decorative at 4am.
**Cost:** $40–150 each · **Time:** none · **Materials:** rated bags
· **Regions:** Ice & Snow North rank 8, Quake West.

A duvet is not rated for anything. A sleeping bag carries a temperature rating, and that rating is the
difference between an uncomfortable night and hypothermia in a house that has been unheated for two
days. Buy for the coldest realistic indoor temperature your house reaches without heat — which is
colder than people guess, because a house sheds heat for days.

Wool blankets are the low-cost alternative and work when layered. Hats indoors, because a large share
of heat loss is from the head and it is the cheapest correction available.

**What this does not do:** a warm bag protects a sleeping person. It does nothing for the waking hours,
which is what Protection 26 is for.

### 29 · The cooling room and the heat refuge

**Prevents:** the deadliest weather mechanism in the United States.
**Cost:** $0–120 · **Time:** an hour · **Materials:** fans, shading, a plan
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 1 in any heat-prone area.

Heat is, over the long run, the deadliest weather hazard in the country, and it kills indoors.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Extreme Heat" — **"extreme heat is responsible for the highest number of annual deaths among all weather-related hazards."** https://www.ready.gov/heat | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Identify the coolest room now — lowest floor, north side, fewest windows, best cross-ventilation. That
is where the household goes. Then identify, and write down, the nearest reliably air-conditioned
public building: library, mall, community centre, or the official cooling centre your county opens.
Know its hours and how you would get there without a car.

Fans help only up to a point, and the Weather Service names the point: **"Do not direct the flow of
portable electric fans toward yourself when room temperature is hotter than 90°F. The dry blowing air
will dehydrate you faster, endangering your health."** Above that threshold a fan is still useful — for
**exhausting hot air out of a room or drawing cooler air in** — but not for blowing on a person. That is
exactly the moment when people believe it is helping most.
«SRC: National Weather Service, "During a Heat Wave" — do not direct portable electric fans at yourself
above 90°F; use them to exhaust hot air or draw in cooler air. |
https://www.weather.gov/safety/heat-during | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Active cooling works: cool showers, wet cloths at neck and wrists, feet in cool water.

**What this does not do:** none of this replaces air conditioning for a frail or elderly person in a
prolonged heat wave. For them, relocation is the protection, and it should be decided on day one, not
day three.

### 30 · Reflective film and exterior shading

**Prevents:** the sun loading your house all afternoon.
**Cost:** $20–200 · **Time:** an afternoon · **Materials:** film, awnings, or shade cloth
· **Regions:** heat-prone regions, Drought regions.

Blocking sun *outside* the glass beats blocking it inside by a wide margin — an awning, an exterior
shade, or shade cloth on the sun-facing windows. Interior reflective film is the rental-friendly
version. Close blinds on the sun side and open the shaded side.

**What this does not do:** shading reduces gain; it does not remove heat already in the house. Pair it
with night flushing — opening up when the outside air finally drops below the inside air.

### 31 · Attic insulation and ventilation as heat defence

**Prevents:** a ceiling that radiates into your living space all evening.
**Cost:** $300–2,000 · **Time:** a weekend, or hire it · **Materials:** insulation
· **Regions:** heat-prone regions, Ice & Snow North.

An under-insulated attic is a heat battery in summer and a heat leak in winter, which makes it the
one upgrade that pays in both directions. Check your depth against the recommended level for your
climate zone before spending anything — many houses are far below it and the fix is cheap per unit of
return.

**Here are the actual targets**, from the federal ENERGY STAR retrofit guidance for existing wood-framed
houses. The column that applies to you depends on what is up there now.

| Climate zone | Uninsulated attic | Already has 3–4 inches |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | R30 | R25 |
| 2 | R49 | R38 |
| 3 | R49 | R38 |
| 4A and 4B | R60 | R49 |
| 4C, 5 and 6 | R60 | R49 |
| 7 and 8 | R60 | R49 |

Two things to notice before you call anyone. **The recommendation is R49 to R60 across most of the
country**, and R49 is roughly fourteen to eighteen inches of loose fill depending on material — so if you
can see the tops of your ceiling joists, you are not close. And **the number you add depends on what is
already there**, which is why the second column exists and why an honest contractor measures before
quoting.

This guidance tracks the 2021 International Energy Conservation Code, Table R402.1.3, which is also what
your local building department is most likely to be working from.
«SRC: US EPA / US Department of Energy ENERGY STAR, "Recommended Home Insulation R–Values" —
recommended insulation levels for retrofitting existing wood-framed buildings, attic floor: zone 1
R30/R25, zone 2 R49/R38, zone 3 R49/R38, zones 4A and 4B R60/R49, zones 4C, 5 and 6 R60/R49, zones 7 and 8
R60/R49 (uninsulated / already has 3–4 inches); guidance based on the 2021 International Energy
Conservation Code Residential Provisions Chapter 4 Table R402.1.3. |
https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/seal_insulate/identify-problems-you-want-fix/diy-checks-inspections/insulation-r-values | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**What this does not do:** insulation is a slow-payback comfort and cost measure, not an emergency
protection. It belongs in the sixty-day sequence only if the budget is there after the cheap items.

### 32 · Generator siting and carbon monoxide clearance

**Prevents:** the most common way a survived storm kills a family.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** a decided location
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 4 Hurricane Coast, rank 4 Ice & Snow North.

**A generator runs outdoors. Never in the garage — including with the door open. Never on a porch,
under an overhang, in a breezeway, or near an open window or vent.** Well clear of the building, with
the exhaust directed away from it.

Decide the exact spot now and mark it, because this decision otherwise gets made in the dark, in rain,
by someone exhausted, and it gets made badly. Run the cord from there, and make sure it is rated for
the load and the distance.

Carbon monoxide is colourless and odourless, its early symptoms are indistinguishable from
post-storm exhaustion, and it impairs the judgment you would use to recognise it. CDC's instruction is
unqualified: **never use a generator inside a home or garage, "even if doors and windows are open,"** and
place it **more than 20 feet from any window, door or vent** — plus a **battery-powered or battery-backup
CO detector** inside while it runs.
«SRC: CDC, "Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics" — never use a generator inside a home or garage, even
with doors and windows open; operate generators outdoors more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents;
use a battery-powered or battery-backup CO detector. |
https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**What this does not do:** correct siting does not remove the need for alarms. Wind shifts, and
enclosed spaces you did not think of connect to the house. Protection 47 is not optional alongside
this one.

### 33 · Space heater discipline

**Prevents:** trading hypothermia for a house fire.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** rules
· **Regions:** Ice & Snow North, all cold-climate households.

Electric space heaters: three feet of clearance from everything, plugged **directly into a wall
outlet** and never into an extension lead or power strip, never left running unattended or overnight,
and never used to dry clothing.

Fuel-burning heaters marketed as indoor-safe still consume oxygen and produce combustion products,
require the ventilation the manufacturer specifies, and require a working CO alarm in the room. If you
are unsure whether yours qualifies, treat it as outdoor-only.

**What this does not do:** a space heater does not heat a house and should not be asked to. It heats
the consolidated room from Protection 26.

### 34 · Pipe freeze prevention

**Prevents:** a burst pipe turning an outage into a renovation.
**Cost:** $10–120 · **Time:** an afternoon · **Materials:** pipe insulation, faucet covers
· **Regions:** Ice & Snow North rank 1. Also any southern state during an anomalous freeze.

Water expands as it freezes and splits the pipe; the damage happens on the thaw, when it starts
pumping into the structure. The correctable failures are all cheap.

Insulate pipes in unconditioned space — crawlspaces, garages, exterior walls, attics. Cover outdoor
spigots and disconnect hoses, because a connected hose holds water against the valve. Open cabinet
doors under sinks on exterior walls so room air reaches the pipes. Let a vulnerable tap drip, because
moving water resists freezing and a dripping tap relieves the pressure that actually splits the pipe.
And if the house will be empty in a hard freeze, either keep the heat on at a set minimum or drain the
system properly.

Some of the worst losses on record come from southern states during anomalous freezes, where houses
were never built for it and nobody knew where the shut-off was.

**What this does not do:** a drip is not a guarantee, and insulation slows freezing rather than
preventing it. If the house will be unheated for days in deep cold, draining is the real answer.

### 35 · Shut-off literacy

**Prevents:** twenty minutes of hunting while water or gas fills the house.
**Cost:** $0–20 · **Time:** 30 minutes, once · **Materials:** a labelled wrench
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 2 Ice & Snow North.

Three locations, learned once, by everyone in the house old enough: **the main water shut-off, the gas
shut-off, and the main electrical breaker.** Find them, label them, photograph them, and put the
photographs in the document vault. Hang the wrench that fits the gas valve next to the gas valve.

Then practise the water one — actually close it and open it, because valves seize and a valve you
discover is seized during a burst pipe is not a shut-off.

**On gas: shut it off only if you smell gas, hear it escaping, or are told to.** Once it is off, it
stays off until the utility restores it — never relight it yourself.

### 36 · Sump pump with battery backup

**Prevents:** the pump failing at the exact moment it is needed.
**Cost:** $150–600 · **Time:** an afternoon, or hire it · **Materials:** battery backup or water-powered
backup · **Regions:** Ice & Snow North rank 5, Hurricane Coast, all basement households.

A sump pump runs on grid power, and the storm that floods the basement is the storm that cuts the
power. A pump with no backup is a pump that works on every day except the one that matters.

Battery backup is the common answer; a water-pressure-driven backup is the maintenance-free
alternative where municipal pressure holds. Either way, test the primary quarterly by pouring a
bucket into the pit, and replace the backup battery on the schedule rather than on failure.

**What this does not do:** a sump pump handles groundwater seepage. It does not stop a sewer backup —
that is Protection 82 and a completely different device — and it will not keep up with a rate of
inflow beyond its capacity.
