# Part Three, Playbook 2 — Hurricane and Storm Surge

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

The lesson of the modern hurricane era is that the category number tells you almost nothing about how
you will be hurt.

**Katrina, 2005 — water, not wind.** The National Hurricane Center's report puts the toll at
**1,392 people: 520 direct deaths, 565 indirect, and 307 whose cause was never determined.** The
surge reached 15 to 19 feet in eastern New Orleans, St. Bernard and Plaquemines Parishes, and the
levees and floodwalls were overtopped and breached — most of the breaches from erosion on the *back*
side, caused by water pouring over the top. Louisiana reported that people over 60 were the majority
of its dead.
«SRC: National Hurricane Center, Tropical Cyclone Report: Hurricane Katrina (AL122005). |
https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL122005_Katrina.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**Harvey, 2017 — rain, hundreds of miles of it.** Harvey stalled over southeast Texas for four days
and dropped **60.58 inches near Nederland**, the largest tropical-cyclone rainfall total in United
States records. NHC attributes **68 direct deaths, all in Texas, 36 of them in Harris County — and
all but three of those deaths were caused by freshwater flooding, with none linked to the storm
surge.** For a category 4 landfall, the report calls that remarkable. It is the whole thesis of this
playbook in one sentence.
«SRC: National Hurricane Center, Tropical Cyclone Report: Hurricane Harvey (AL092017). |
https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL092017_Harvey.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**Sandy, 2012 — not technically a hurricane when it hit.** Sandy made landfall near Brigantine, New
Jersey as a **post-tropical cyclone** with 70-knot winds, and because of its sheer size it drove a
record storm tide into New York Harbor: **9.40 feet of surge above normal tide at the Battery, and a
storm tide of 14.06 feet — 4.36 feet above the previous record in a series going back to 1920.**
There were at least 147 direct deaths across the basin, 72 of them in the mid-Atlantic and Northeast.
«SRC: National Hurricane Center, Tropical Cyclone Report: Hurricane Sandy (AL182012). |
https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL182012_Sandy.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**Michael, 2018 — stronger than the plan people made.** Michael was at **110 knots at 1800 UTC on 9
October and 140 knots — category 5 — when the eye came ashore near Tyndall Air Force Base at 1730 UTC
on the 10th.** Thirty knots of intensification inside the last day, which is to say: whatever you
decided when you looked at the forecast the previous evening, you were planning for a different storm.
«SRC: National Hurricane Center, Tropical Cyclone Report: Hurricane Michael (AL142018). |
https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL142018_Michael.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Four storms, four completely different mechanisms of harm.

The forecasting truth underneath that is the one thing to take from this section: **the category is a
wind scale and nothing else.** It does not describe surge, it does not describe rainfall, it does not
describe the storm's size, and it does not describe how long it will sit on you. A large, slow
category 1 can be far worse for your household than a small, fast category 3.

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Killed by water, not wind**, and the margin is not close. The definitive study is Rappaport's
fifty-year accounting of US deaths directly caused by Atlantic tropical cyclones, 1963 to 2012 —
2,325 deaths for which a cause was identified. **Roughly 90 percent were water incidents, most of
them drownings.** Storm surge accounted for **49 percent.** Rainfall-driven freshwater floods and
mudslides accounted for **27 percent** — and rainfall-flood deaths occurred in more tropical cyclones
than any other hazard, which is the sentence to remember if you live inland. Between 100 and 150
people died near the shoreline in rip currents and large waves, and about the same number drowned
offshore within 50 miles of the coast. **Nontornadic wind caused 5 to 10 percent. Tornadoes caused
about 3 percent.**
«SRC: Rappaport EN. "Fatalities in the United States from Atlantic Tropical Cyclones: New Data and
Interpretation." Bull. Amer. Meteor. Soc. 2014;95(3):341–346. doi:10.1175/BAMS-D-12-00074.1 |
https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/bams/95/3/bams-d-12-00074.1.xml | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Read that list against the category scale and the mismatch is obvious. The number on the news
describes the fraction of the danger that kills five to ten percent of the people.

Then the category people forget: **indirect deaths after the storm**, which in some events exceed the
direct ones. Carbon monoxide from generators run indoors. Heat, in a powerless house in September, among
elderly people. Cardiac events during cleanup. Loss of dialysis, oxygen and refrigerated medication.
Electrocution in flooded structures. Chainsaws. Traffic at dead intersections.

These are the deaths this book can prevent most reliably, because they are all downstream of an outage
you can plan for.

**Costs:** wind damage to roof and openings, and then the far larger and far less insured category —
water. Standard homeowner policies exclude flood. The single most consequential financial fact in this
playbook is that a household in a rainfall-flooded neighbourhood with no flood policy is usually looking
at an uninsured loss.

## Before

Hurricanes are the one hazard in this book with days of warning. That changes the whole posture: nothing
here should be done during the warning, because the shops empty and the contractors vanish.

**Priority order for a Hurricane Coast household:**

- **95 — the trigger plan**, and here it is rank one. Written in advance: *if an evacuation order is
  issued for my zone, or if the forecast track puts the storm here within X hours, we leave — without a
  further discussion.*
- **73 — opening protection.** Rated shutters, impact glass, or plywood cut, labelled and drilled
  **now**, in the garage, with the fasteners in a bag taped to it. Plywood bought during a warning is
  plywood you do not get.
- **74 — garage door bracing.** The most common structural failure point, and the one that
  pressurises the house and takes the roof.
- **69 — a sealed roof deck** and **72 — hurricane straps**, timed to your next re-roof, which is when
  they cost a fraction of what they cost as standalone jobs.
- **103 — flood insurance**, bought **thirty days before** you need it. This is the protection with a
  clock on it and the one most people get wrong.
- **1 — water store**, **13 — no-cook pantry**, **39 — generator and transfer switch**, **32 —
  generator siting**, because the outage is the long part.
- **106 — two routes out** and **111 — three named destinations**, driven and confirmed in advance.
- **91 — know your evacuation zone**, by letter, from your county's own map. Not your flood zone; they
  are different things and people confuse them every year.
- **96, 100, 102 — document vault, video inventory, insurance audit**, with the audit specifically
  checking the hurricane deductible, which is often a percentage rather than a flat sum.

**Know which of the two houses you live in.** A house in a surge evacuation zone is a *leave* house, and
no amount of hardening changes that. A house outside the surge zones is a *stay and harden* house, where
the plan is riding out wind and then a long outage. Every decision below flows from which one you are,
and it is decided now, on a map, not later, in the dark.

## During

**Five days out.** Watch the forecast, and watch the *surge* and *rainfall* products rather than the
category. Fill prescriptions. Get cash. Get fuel. Check the generator actually starts, because a
generator that has sat since last season frequently does not.

**Three days out.** Install the opening protection — this takes longer than you think and it cannot be
done in wind. Bring in or tie down everything outside: furniture, bins, planters, the trampoline, the
grill, anything that becomes a projectile. Clear the gutters. Trim nothing at this point; you will not
dispose of the cuttings in time and loose branches on the ground are ammunition.

**Two days out, if you are leaving:** go. This is what the trigger plan is for. Traffic is the reason —
the roads work now and will not later, and contraflow, closures and fuel shortages all arrive together.
Take the go-bags, the document envelope, the pets, the medication, the five-minute and thirty-minute
lists. Shut the house down per Protection 113.

**One day out, if you are staying:** fill the tubs and every container (Protection 3). Freeze water
bottles to buy your freezer time. Charge everything. Turn the fridge and freezer to their coldest.
Position the go-bags and the safe room. Photograph the house and the property, inside and out — this
takes five minutes and it is the "before" half of your claim evidence.

**During the storm:** interior room, lowest floor, away from windows, and stay off the upper floor if
wind is the main threat. If water is rising, that judgment inverts — go up, and never into an attic
without a way out of it (Protection 114).

**The eye is the trap.** If the wind stops and the sky brightens, that is the middle of the storm, and
the second half arrives from the opposite direction, onto a structure that has already been weakened
and is now loaded the other way. Do not go outside. This kills people in every major landfall.

**If a window or door fails**, retreat and close doors behind you to compartmentalise the pressure. Do
not try to fight it, and do not open a window on the far side "to equalise pressure" — that is a myth
and it makes things worse.

## After

**Do not go back until the area is opened.** Roads are closed for live wires, gas, washouts and
unstable structures, not to inconvenience you.

**The killers are now indoor generators, heat and water.** Restated because this is where the indirect
deaths come from:

- **Generators outdoors only**, twenty feet from the house, away from windows and doors, never in a
  garage even with the door open (Protections 32 and 41). CO alarms on every level.
- **Never backfeed** a generator into a wall socket. It kills utility workers and it is why Protection
  39 specifies a transfer switch.
- **Heat**, in a house with no air conditioning, is a serious risk for elderly people and anyone on
  certain medications (Protection 64). Find a cooling centre if that is your household.
- **Assume the water is unsafe** until the boil notice is lifted (Protection 8).

**Floodwater is contaminated.** Sewage, fuel, chemicals, and whatever was in the neighbourhood's
garages. Boots and gloves, wash after every contact, and any wound that has met floodwater gets treated
seriously (Protection 65). Discard food that touched it, without negotiation (Protection 21), and
discard porous materials — carpet, mattresses, upholstery, insulation.

**Mould starts within a day or two.** Get water out, get air moving, get materials open. This is one of
the few places where speed genuinely changes the outcome of a repair.

**The claim.** Photograph everything before you move it. Log every call. Understand which policy is
paying: wind damage on the homeowner policy with its hurricane deductible, water intrusion from outside
on the flood policy if you have one, sewer backup on the endorsement if you bought it. Three products,
three claims, and a household that knows the difference gets paid faster.

**Contractor fraud is at its worst here.** Out-of-state trucks arrive within a day. Sign nothing on the
spot, never pay in full up front, verify licensing with the state, and be extremely careful with any
document that assigns your insurance benefits to a contractor.
