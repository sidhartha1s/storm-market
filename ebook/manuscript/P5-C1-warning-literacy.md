## Warning literacy

Every protection in this book is worthless if you do not find out in time.

That sounds obvious and it is where most households actually fail. Not through a lack of equipment,
but because the alert went to a phone that was on silent, or the siren was never audible indoors, or
the words "watch" and "warning" were understood backwards, or the hurricane cone was read as a map of
where damage happens.

This chapter is the cheapest in the book. Almost every protection in it costs nothing, takes an
evening, and is the difference between having a plan and executing one.

---

### 85 · Two independent alert paths

**Prevents:** a single point of failure between you and the warning.
**Cost:** $0–45 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **Materials:** a phone, and a NOAA weather radio
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 3 Tornado Alley.

You need two paths that fail differently, because every single path has a failure mode that is
routine rather than exotic.

**Wireless Emergency Alerts** reach your phone without an app or a subscription, and they are the
default path for most people. Their failure modes are ordinary: the phone is on silent, the phone is
dead, the network cell is congested or down, or the alert categories have been switched off in
settings — which many people do after being woken once. **Check those settings tonight** and leave the
emergency categories on.

**A NOAA weather radio** with battery backup fails differently. It does not depend on the cellular
network, the internet, or the power grid. It is the path that still works when everything else is
saturated, which is precisely when you need it. Get one with SAME programming and battery backup, and
put it where you sleep.

The point is not redundancy for its own sake. It is that the two paths do not share a failure.

**What this does not do:** neither path helps if nobody is awake to hear it. A phone face-down on a
charger in another room and a radio in a cupboard are both decorative. Placement is the protection.

### 86 · County and local alert enrollment

**Prevents:** missing the alerts that are most specific to you.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 15 minutes · **Materials:** your county's website
· **Regions:** all five. **The single most-skipped free protection in this book.**

Federal alerts cover broad hazards. The alerts that tell you *your* street is being evacuated, *your*
water is unsafe, or *your* neighbourhood is under a shelter order come from your county or city
emergency management office, and they almost always require you to sign up.

Almost nobody does. It takes a few minutes, it is free, and it is the alert most likely to be
specifically about you.

Do it tonight, for every adult in the household, on every phone, and add your work address and your
children's school addresses if the system allows multiple locations — because you are not always at
home when it matters.

Your state and county contacts are in Appendix B.

**What this does not do:** enrolling does not test the system. Many counties send an annual test —
when yours arrives, note whether it actually reached everyone, and treat a miss as a fault to fix
rather than a curiosity.

### 87 · NOAA weather radio, programmed to your county only

**Prevents:** a radio so noisy you turn it off.
**Cost:** $30–70 · **Time:** 20 minutes with the manual · **Materials:** a SAME-capable radio
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 4 Tornado Alley.

The reason people abandon weather radios is that an unprogrammed one alerts for a wide area, wakes
you for events fifty miles away, and gets switched off within a week.

**SAME** — Specific Area Message Encoding — fixes this. Each county has a numeric code, and a radio
programmed with your code alerts only for your county. Look up your code, program it, and the radio
becomes something you can actually leave on beside the bed.
«CITE: NOAA Weather Radio SAME county code lookup, and the programming procedure.»

Buy one with battery backup and a tone alert that will wake a sleeping adult. Test it. Replace the
batteries on a schedule.

**What this does not do:** the broadcast requires a transmitter in range, and there are genuine
coverage gaps in mountainous terrain. Check coverage for your address rather than assuming.

### 88 · Watch, warning, advisory — and what each asks of you

**Prevents:** the most consequential vocabulary error in weather.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 10 minutes · **Materials:** none
· **Regions:** all five.

Three words, routinely understood backwards, and the whole trigger plan depends on them.

A **watch** means conditions are favourable — be ready, and this is when you do the things that take
time. Fuel the cars. Charge everything. Fill the containers. Bring in the furniture. Brace the garage
door. **If you are in a manufactured home, a watch is when you leave**, not a warning.

A **warning** means it is happening or imminent — act now, and stop preparing. There is no time left
for tasks; there is only time for the plan.

An **advisory** means significant conditions that are inconvenient or hazardous but below warning
criteria. Not nothing. Not an emergency.

Some hazards also carry an enhanced tier of wording reserved for the most extreme, life-threatening
situations, and those phrasings are deliberately unambiguous when they are used.
«CITE: current NWS definitions of watch, warning and advisory, and the enhanced-wording products
including their trigger criteria.»

Write these on the trigger plan in your own words, with the action next to each, and go through them
once with everyone in the house.

**What this does not do:** knowing the definitions does not produce action. That is Protection 95,
and it is the one that matters.

### 89 · Reading a radar loop well enough to decide

**Prevents:** depending entirely on someone else's interpretation.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** an hour, then practice · **Materials:** a phone
· **Regions:** all five.

You do not need to forecast. You need three things.

**Is it coming toward me?** Watch a loop rather than a still image — motion is the entire signal, and
a single frame tells you nothing about direction.

**How long do I have?** Estimate from the loop's motion and the distance. Rough is fine; the answer
you need is "twenty minutes" versus "two hours".

**Is this the dangerous kind?** Intensity, and whether the storm shows the structural signatures that
matter. Learn what a hook or a distinct bounded structure looks like in your area's typical storms, and
learn what velocity data shows — most public radar apps expose it and almost nobody looks.

Practise on ordinary storms when nothing is at stake. That is the whole method, and after a season of
casual watching you will read a loop far better than most people.

**What this does not do:** radar shows precipitation and motion, not the ground. A radar signature is
not confirmation that a tornado is on the ground, and the absence of one is not proof of safety —
particularly at long range or in terrain, where the beam is looking well above the surface.

### 90 · The hurricane cone, and what it does not mean

**Prevents:** the most consequential misreading in weather communication.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 15 minutes · **Materials:** none
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast.

The cone is the most misunderstood graphic in public weather, and the misunderstanding is dangerous in
a specific way.

**The cone shows the probable track of the storm's centre. It does not show where the impacts are.**
Hazards extend far outside it, sometimes hundreds of miles — and being outside the cone is not being
out of danger. Rainfall, surge, and tornadoes routinely occur well beyond it, and the cone is drawn so that
**two-thirds** of historical forecast errors fall inside it — which means the centre itself goes outside
the cone about one time in three.
«SRC: NHC, "Definition of the NHC Track Forecast Cone" — the cone encloses circles sized so that two-thirds of official forecast errors over a 5-year sample fall within them, and it depicts the probable track of the centre only, not the extent of impacts. https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/aboutcone.shtml · retrieved 2026-07-29»

What to read instead: the **surge inundation maps**, which show water depth over land and are the
product most tied to fatalities, the wind arrival timing, and the rainfall forecast — which is
frequently the deadliest element and is unrelated to the category.

And read the **category** correctly: it describes wind only. It says nothing about surge or rain, which
is why lower-category storms have produced some of the worst water disasters.

**What this does not do:** none of these products replace your evacuation zone, which is Protection 91.
Zones are drawn from surge modelling, not from the forecast, and they are the instruction that
actually applies to you.

### 91 · Your evacuation zone, and the fire-weather products
**Prevents:** guessing whether the order applies to you.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **Materials:** your county's map
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, Wildfire West rank 3.

Evacuation orders are issued by **zone**, not by distance from the water or the treeline. Look up your
zone now, write it on the plan, and write it on the fridge. When an order comes it will name zones, and
you will not be in a state to work out which one you are in.

In wildfire country the equivalent is your **evacuation zone identifier** in whatever system your
county uses, plus the fire-weather products: a **Red Flag Warning** means conditions for rapid fire
growth are present, and it is a signal to be packed, fuelled and paying attention rather than to leave.

Know the difference between the two levels of order your jurisdiction uses — typically a warning or
"be ready" tier and a mandatory or "go now" tier — and know that in a fast-moving fire the first may
never arrive.

**What this does not do:** knowing your zone is not a decision. The decision is the leave-early
trigger in Protection 112, and the whole point is that it fires *before* the order in the cases where
the order comes too late.

### 92 · Flash flood: turn around, and your local gauge
**Prevents:** the way most flood victims actually die.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **Materials:** a stream-gauge bookmark
· **Regions:** all five.

The rule is short and absolute: **never drive or walk into water of unknown depth, and turn around.**
Twelve inches of rushing water carries away most cars; two feet carries away SUVs and trucks. Over half
of all flood-related drownings happen when a vehicle is driven into floodwater.
«SRC: NWS, "Turn Around Don't Drown" — 6 inches of fast-moving water knocks over an adult; 12 inches carries away most cars; 2 feet carries away SUVs and trucks. Cites CDC that over half of all flood-related drownings occur when a vehicle is driven into floodwater, with walking into or near floodwater the next largest share. https://www.weather.gov/safety/flood-turn-around-dont-drown · retrieved 2026-07-29»
«SRC: NWS, "Turn Around Don't Drown" — 6 in of fast-moving water knocks over an adult, 12 in carries away most cars, 2 ft carries away SUVs and trucks. https://www.weather.gov/safety/flood-turn-around-dont-drown · retrieved 2026-07-29»

Two specifics that save lives. **At night you cannot judge depth**, and the familiarity of your own
commute is a liability rather than an asset — the road you know is exactly the one you will
misjudge. And **barricades are not advisory**; they are placed because someone measured something.

Then the local skill: find your nearest stream or river gauge online and bookmark it. Learn what
number means water in your street. A household that knows its own gauge reading has better
information than any general forecast can give it.

**What this does not do:** a gauge is upstream history, not a forecast, and flash floods on small
catchments can outrun any gauge. In a burn scar or a canyon, the rule is to leave on the forecast, not
on an observation.

### 93 · Siren myths
**Prevents:** waiting for a sound that is not coming.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 5 minutes · **Materials:** none
· **Regions:** Tornado Alley, and anywhere with sirens.

Outdoor warning sirens are designed to alert people who are **outdoors**. They are not designed to be
heard inside a modern insulated house, over a television, in the rain, with the wind blowing the wrong
way. Many people believe the opposite and treat silence as safety.

Three things to know. **Sirens may not sound at all** for a hazard that warrants shelter, because
activation policy varies by jurisdiction and some do not sound for every warning. **A siren is not an
all-clear system** — most do not signal the end of anything. And **hearing one is not confirmation of
what the hazard is**, which is why it is a prompt to check your two alert paths rather than a source of
information.

Find out your own jurisdiction's activation policy. It is usually published, and it is usually
surprising.

**What this does not do:** none of this means sirens are useless. It means they are a supplement to
Protection 85, never the primary path.

### 94 · Space weather alerts
**Prevents:** being surprised by the one hazard with hours of notice and no siren.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 10 minutes · **Materials:** none
· **Regions:** all five, strongest at high latitude.

Geomagnetic storms are forecast and graded on published scales, and the alerts are public. The
practical consequence for a household is potential effects on the grid, on radio and satellite
navigation, and in the largest historical cases on long transmission infrastructure.
«CITE: NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center — geomagnetic storm scale, alert products, and documented
historical effects including the 1989 Quebec event.»

The action is modest and proportionate: know where to check, and treat a severe watch as a prompt for
Protection 120 — unplug the expensive electronics — and for the ordinary outage readiness you already
have.

**What this does not do:** this is not a doomsday hazard and this book does not treat it as one. It is
a real, forecast, graded phenomenon with real infrastructure effects, and the honest household
response is small.

### 95 · The trigger plan
**Prevents:** deciding under stress what you could have decided calmly.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** one evening · **Materials:** one sheet of paper
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 5 Tornado Alley. **The highest-leverage free protection in the book.**

This is the protection that everything in Part One points at.

People do not act on warnings. They seek to verify them — the window, a relative, the television, the
neighbours — and verification consumes exactly the time the warning bought. This was among the
findings of the National Weather Service assessment of the Joplin tornado, and it is not a failure of
character. It is what a lifetime of warnings that came to nothing trains you to do.
«SRC: NWS Central Region, *Service Assessment: Joplin, Missouri, Tornado — May 22, 2011* — timeline: initial 3-minute siren alert **5:11 pm CDT**; WFO Springfield tornado warning polygon #31 issued **5:17 pm**; initial touchdown **5:34 pm**; **second** 3-minute siren alert **5:38 pm**. As of the report date, **158 direct and 4 indirect fatalities**. Findings: the perceived frequency of siren activation "led the majority of survey participants to become desensitized or complacent to this method of warning"; and Finding #2d — "the majority of surveyed Joplin residents did not take protective action until receiving and processing additional, credible confirmation of the threat and its magnitude from a non-routine trigger", achieved by seeing the tornado, hearing on-air instruction to take cover, or hearing the second siren. Joplin Globe preliminary analysis of fatality location: **54% in residences, 32% in non-residential buildings, 14% in vehicles or outdoors**. https://www.weather.gov/media/publications/assessments/Joplin_tornado.pdf · retrieved 2026-07-29»

You cannot fix this by resolving to be braver. You fix it by **moving the decision to a moment when
nobody is frightened**, writing it down, and agreeing it out loud with the household. A trigger plan is
a set of pre-committed thresholds with an action attached to each:

> When a tornado warning includes our location — we go to the room. We do not look outside.
>
> When a hurricane watch is issued for our county — cars fuelled, documents in the car, garage braced.
>
> When an evacuation order is issued for our zone — we leave within thirty minutes, and we do not
> reopen the argument about whether it is necessary.
>
> When the AQI passes our number — windows shut, filtration on, and the children stay in.
>
> When a Red Flag Warning is issued — the go-bags go in the car.

Write between five and ten. Put it on the fridge and in the binder. Read it once at the start of each
season.

The mechanism is simple and it is why this works: it converts a judgment call made by a frightened
person into a rule already agreed by a calm one.

**What this does not do:** a trigger plan does not survive being written and forgotten. It has to be
said out loud to the people who will have to follow it, and rehearsed once, or it is a piece of paper
that makes you feel prepared.
