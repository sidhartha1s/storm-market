# Part Three, Playbook 19 — Electronics and Records Protection

Target: 8 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

There is no disaster in this playbook. There is a pattern, and every insurance adjuster and every
restoration contractor has seen it hundreds of times.

A household survives the event. The building is repairable. Nobody is hurt. And then, in the following
weeks, three things emerge.

**The photographs are gone.** Twenty years of them, on a hard drive in the basement that flooded, or on a
phone that was in a pocket in the rain, or on a laptop that was in the room the tree came through. There was
a backup, once, on a drive in the same room.

**Half the electronics are dead**, not from water but from the surge when the power came back — and the
household did not know that surge damage was a thing until the television, the router, the furnace control
board and the well pump controller all failed within two weeks.

**And the claim is smaller than it should be**, because nobody can prove what was in the house. The adjuster
is not being difficult. There is simply no evidence.

None of that is dramatic. All of it is permanent, and all of it is preventable for a small amount of money
and about two hours of work.

That is why this playbook exists as its own chapter: **the records are the recovery.** The gap from Part
One — an average federal award of a few thousand dollars against a statutory maximum many times larger —
is decided almost entirely by documentation, and documentation is the one part of your exposure that costs
nothing to fix.

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Nothing here kills.** This playbook is honest about being about money and memory.

**What it costs:**

**Electronics replacement**, which for an average household with a television, computers, a router, tablets,
a games console, a printer and smart devices is a four-figure sum, and which surge frequently takes all at
once. Add the invisible ones people forget until they fail: HVAC control boards, well pump controllers,
septic controllers, garage door openers, alarm panels, and the electronics inside modern appliances.

**Data**, which has no dollar figure and is the loss people actually grieve.

**The unclaimable contents**, which is the largest number of the three. A household with no inventory
routinely under-claims a contents loss by a wide margin, because reconstructing what was in a house from
memory produces a list that is short by a factor you would not believe until you tried it. The garage. The
loft. The linen cabinet. The tools. The children's things. The clothes.

**And the coverage basis**, which decides what any of it is worth. Contents settled on **actual cash value**
pay depreciated value: a ten-year-old television at a fraction of what a new one costs. Contents settled on
**replacement cost** pay what it costs to buy the equivalent new. That single line in the policy is
frequently worth more than every other protection in this playbook combined, and most households do not know
which one they have.

## Before

- **100 — the video home inventory.** Twenty minutes, narrated, every room, every drawer opened, the garage,
  the loft, the shed. This is the highest-value twenty minutes in this book and it is rank ten in every region
  for exactly that reason. Off the phone and into the vault the same night.
- **101 — serial numbers and receipts.** Photograph the plate on anything over a few hundred dollars. A
  dedicated email folder for digital receipts. High-value or unusual items may need an appraisal and a
  specific endorsement, because standard policies cap jewelry, art, collections and instruments far lower
  than owners expect.
- **102 — the insurance audit**, and in this playbook the specific line to find is **replacement cost versus
  actual cash value on contents**, plus the sub-limits by category.
- **123 — the off-site backup**, three copies, two places, one off-site, and **restored once** to prove it
  works. An untested backup is a belief.
- **96 — the document vault**, and **97 — the paper envelope**, because digital fails in the conditions you
  built it for.
- **46 — whole-home surge protection** at the panel, plus point-of-use protectors on what matters, plus
  protection on the coaxial and telephone lines, because surges arrive on data paths as readily as on the
  mains.
- **120 — unplug on warning**, the free version, applied habitually.
- **117 — battery packs and charging layers**, so the device holding your documents survives day two.
- **122 — the shielded box** with a spare drive holding the vault, for the readers who want the low-cost
  hedge.
- **81 — elevate the mechanicals**, which is the electronics protection nobody thinks of as one: the furnace,
  the panel and the air handler are the most expensive electrical assemblies in the building and they are
  usually in the wettest room.

**And the physical placement rule, which is free.** Do not store the irreplaceable in the basement. Not the
photographs, not the drives, not the documents, not the negatives, not the children's things. Basements
flood, from outside and from the plumbing above, and they are the first room lost in most of the hazards in
this book. Move the boxes up a floor this weekend.

**Then the annual habit**, which is what keeps all of it true: redo the video inventory once a year and after
anything significant arrives, refresh the vault, and check that the backup still runs.

## During

Short, because most of this is decided beforehand.

**On any warning with lead time:**

Unplug what you can spare (Protection 120), leaving in only what you need for the next hours. Charge
everything. Move electronics and irreplaceables **up and away from windows** — up a floor if water is the
threat, into an interior room if wind or hail is. Get the drives, the albums and the boxes off the basement
floor even if all you have time for is putting them on a table.

**Then photograph the house, inside and out, before the event.** Five minutes, every room, all four
elevations. This is the "before" half of the evidence pair, and almost nobody does it. If a claim later turns
on whether a roof was already damaged or whether a room already had a crack, this is what answers it.

**During an outage:** the surge risk is not while the power is off, it is when it returns. Leave unplugged
what you unplugged.

**If water is entering:** power off to the affected area if it is safe to do so, and then leave the
electrics alone. Do not wade into a flooded room to save a computer. The computer is not worth the risk and
it is almost certainly already finished.

## After

**The restoration surge.** Wait several minutes after power returns before plugging sensitive equipment back
in, then stage things back on rather than all at once.

**Check the invisible electronics**, because they fail quietly and sometimes days later: HVAC control board,
well pump controller, septic controller, thermostat, alarm panel, garage opener, router, and any appliance
with a circuit board. Replace any point-of-use surge protector that took a hit, because a protector that has
absorbed a surge is now a plain extension lead with a reassuring light.

**Anything submerged is finished, and this is the section to be firm about.** A submerged outlet, switch,
panel, furnace, air handler, water heater or appliance does not get dried out and reused. Water leaves
conductive residue and corrosion inside sealed assemblies, and the failure mode is a fire weeks later. This
is not caution for its own sake — it is why restoration contractors and electricians condemn wet equipment
as a matter of course.

**Wet data is a partial exception, and it is worth knowing.** A drowned hard drive should **not** be
switched on or dried out — powering it up is what destroys the platters. Keep it wet or sealed in a bag,
and take it to a professional data recovery service.

**Wet photographs and paper can frequently be saved, and the National Archives publishes how.** The
guidance was written after the 1993 Midwest floods for exactly this reader. The key move is the one nobody
guesses: **if wet materials cannot be dried immediately, stabilize them by freezing.** In the Archives'
words, a domestic or commercial freezer *"will not kill mold. It will, however, put the mold in a dormant
state until time and an appropriate treatment environment are available"* — after which manageable
quantities can be defrosted and treated at leisure. Mold thrives on high temperature, high humidity,
stagnant air and darkness, so the priority order is dry first, freeze if you cannot.

Two cautions from the same document. **Do not try to remove active mold** — the fuzzy or slimy kind — as
*"it may only spread or smear"*; dormant mold is dry and powdery. And anything of real monetary, historic
or sentimental value should be handled in consultation with a conservator, because some flood damage is
irreversible.
«SRC: National Archives and Records Administration, "Emergency Salvage of Flood Damaged Family Papers"
(August 1993) — stabilizing wet and moldy materials by freezing; freezing renders mold dormant rather than
killing it; do not attempt to remove active mold. |
https://www.archives.gov/preservation/conservation/flood-damage.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**Then document the loss properly.** Photograph everything before you move it. Photograph the serial plates
on everything destroyed. Build the contents list from the video inventory rather than from memory, which is
what makes the list complete. Keep the discarded items until the adjuster has seen them or released them in
writing.

**Then claim in the right order:** the homeowner policy for covered perils, the flood policy for water from
outside, the sewer endorsement for backups, and federal assistance separately. Ask specifically about the
food spoilage sub-limit and about emergency mitigation costs, both of which are frequently reimbursable and
frequently unclaimed.

**And rebuild the redundancy immediately**, because the second event is the one that finds you without a
backup: new backup drive, cloud backup running, vault refreshed, inventory redone in the new arrangement of
the house.
