# Part Three, Playbook 1 — Tornado

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

The sirens sounded in Joplin, Missouri at **5:11** on the afternoon of 22 May 2011. The tornado warning
followed at **5:17**. The tornado touched down at **5:34** and the city lost, by the count in the federal
assessment, **158 people directly and four more indirectly** — along with about a third of its built
area.
«SRC: NWS Central Region, *Service Assessment: Joplin, Missouri, Tornado — May 22, 2011* — timeline: initial 3-minute siren alert **5:11 pm CDT**; WFO Springfield tornado warning polygon #31 issued **5:17 pm**; initial touchdown **5:34 pm**; **second** 3-minute siren alert **5:38 pm**. As of the report date, **158 direct and 4 indirect fatalities**. Findings: the perceived frequency of siren activation "led the majority of survey participants to become desensitized or complacent to this method of warning"; and Finding #2d — "the majority of surveyed Joplin residents did not take protective action until receiving and processing additional, credible confirmation of the threat and its magnitude from a non-routine trigger", achieved by seeing the tornado, hearing on-air instruction to take cover, or hearing the second siren. Joplin Globe preliminary analysis of fatality location: **54% in residences, 32% in non-residential buildings, 14% in vehicles or outdoors**. https://www.weather.gov/media/publications/assessments/Joplin_tornado.pdf · retrieved 2026-07-29»

The service assessment written afterwards found something more useful than the meteorology, and it is
the reason this playbook opens here rather than with a bigger tornado. The warning worked. The lead
time was ahead of the national average. People heard it.

**And most of them did not immediately take shelter.** They went to a window. They opened a door. They
called somebody. They checked a television, a phone, a neighbour, the sky — looking for a second,
independent confirmation that the thing was real and was coming for them specifically. The assessment
named this behaviour, and it named the reason for it: a population that had heard many sirens for storms
that did nothing had learned, entirely rationally, to treat a siren as an invitation to look rather than
an instruction to move.
«SRC: NWS Central Region, *Service Assessment: Joplin, Missouri, Tornado — May 22, 2011* — timeline: initial 3-minute siren alert **5:11 pm CDT**; WFO Springfield tornado warning polygon #31 issued **5:17 pm**; initial touchdown **5:34 pm**; **second** 3-minute siren alert **5:38 pm**. As of the report date, **158 direct and 4 indirect fatalities**. Findings: the perceived frequency of siren activation "led the majority of survey participants to become desensitized or complacent to this method of warning"; and Finding #2d — "the majority of surveyed Joplin residents did not take protective action until receiving and processing additional, credible confirmation of the threat and its magnitude from a non-routine trigger", achieved by seeing the tornado, hearing on-air instruction to take cover, or hearing the second siren. Joplin Globe preliminary analysis of fatality location: **54% in residences, 32% in non-residential buildings, 14% in vehicles or outdoors**. https://www.weather.gov/media/publications/assessments/Joplin_tornado.pdf · retrieved 2026-07-29»

Seventeen minutes from warning to touchdown is enough time. It was not enough time to spend most of
it looking for a second opinion.

And Joplin was not an isolated case. The Weather Service's assessment of the 27 April 2011 outbreak —
**316 fatalities that day (234 in Alabama, 32 in Tennessee, 31 in Mississippi, 15 in Georgia, 4 in
Virginia) and more than 2,400 injuries** — reports that **every single tornado fatality occurred inside
a tornado watch and was preceded by a tornado warning.** Watch lead time averaged 2.4 hours. Mean
warning lead time was 22.1 minutes. Probability of detection was 89 percent. And among the contributing
factors the assessment lists by name: *"individuals in the affected areas who did not respond to
warnings until confirmed by more than one communication source"* and *"people in the paths of the storms
who waited for visual confirmation before taking protective action"* — with the storms moving at 45 to
70 mph, which shrank the window for anybody who waited.
«SRC: NWS, *Service Assessment: The Historic Tornadoes of April 2011.* |
https://www.weather.gov/media/publications/assessments/historic_tornadoes.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

There is one more number in that assessment worth sitting with, because it explains the behaviour rather
than condemning it: **the false alarm ratio was 49 percent.** About half of the tornado warnings issued
did not produce a tornado at that location. A person who has learned from experience that a warning is a
coin flip is not being stupid when they go to the window. They are being empirical. The problem is that
the coin flip is asymmetric — being wrong in one direction costs you ten minutes in a hallway, and being
wrong in the other direction costs everything.

That is the finding this book is built on, and it is why Protection 95 — the trigger plan, written down
in advance, that commits you to acting on the first alert rather than the third — is the highest-leverage
free thing in the book.

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Killed by:** trauma, overwhelmingly — and specifically *multisystem* trauma, the body struck in
several places at once. CDC reviewed all 338 deaths across the four days of 25–28 April 2011 — the 316
above are the 27th alone — and found that 95.6 percent
died of multisystem trauma and 21.9 percent of the death certificates noted a head injury. Tornado
deaths are a debris problem, not a wind problem: a wind that will not knock you over is carrying
material that will kill you, and in a violent tornado most of that material is the building you are in.

The number in that report that should change what you do is this one: **90.5 percent of the fatal
injuries happened indoors, and 37 percent of the bodies were recovered outside.** Being inside was not
the protection people assumed. Which room, and what that room is made of, was.

Two specific patterns account for a large share of deaths and are both addressable. **Mobile and
manufactured homes** fail structurally in winds that a site-built house survives. CDC lists
"sheltering in mobile homes" first among the known risk factors for tornado death, and in that
outbreak 26.6 percent of the dead were in mobile homes against 46.7 percent in single-family homes.
Almost a third of the dead (32.5 percent) were aged 65 or over, and 89.5 percent of the deaths came
from EF-4 and EF-5 tornadoes.
«SRC: "Tornado-Related Fatalities — Five States, Southeastern United States, April 25–28, 2011."
MMWR Morb Mortal Wkly Rep 2012;61(28):529–533. |
https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6128a3.htm | retrieved 2026-07-29»
**Vehicles** are the other: people who leave shelter to drive, or who are caught driving and stay in the
car.

The other pattern is timing. Deaths cluster at night, when people are asleep and the sky cannot be
read, and in the early part of the year in the South, where tornadoes arrive after dark and inside rain
so heavy that nothing is visible.

**Costs:** total loss or near-total loss in the damage path, and a much wider band of roof, window and
vehicle damage from the same storm's hail and straight-line wind. The insurance reality is that the
catastrophic cases are the visible ones and the volume of money is in the mundane periphery — which is
why Protections 100, 101 and 102 matter as much here as the safe room does.

## Before

**Priority order for a Tornado Alley household:**

- **23 — the safe room**, decided and cleared today. Interior, lowest floor, as many walls between you
  and the outside as possible, no windows. Not a corner of the basement under a piano.
- **24 — a rated above-ground safe room** if you have no basement and the budget exists. Demand
  documented compliance with the federal criteria rather than a salesman's assurance.
- **25 — the helmet rule.** Bicycle or sports helmets for every person, stored *in* the safe room. Head
  injury is the killer; a helmet is the cheapest life-saving object in this book.
- **85 — two independent alert paths**, one of which wakes you up.
- **87 — a NOAA weather radio** programmed to your county only, so it alerts for you and not for three
  counties you do not live in.
- **95 — the trigger plan.** Written. Signed by the household. The Joplin lesson made personal.
- **107 — go-bags**, stored in or beside the safe room, because you may be leaving a house that no
  longer exists.
- **74 — garage door bracing** and **69 — a sealed roof deck**, the two structural upgrades with the
  best return in this hazard.
- **43 — a torch and sturdy shoes at every bedside**, because the floor after a tornado is glass, nails
  and splintered timber, and the lights are out.
- **96, 100 — document vault and video inventory**, because a total loss makes proof impossible after
  the fact.

«SRC: NOAA Storm Prediction Center, "The Online Tornado FAQ" — a safe room is a reinforced small room in the interior of a home, in a basement or on the ground floor where there is none; an existing interior bathroom or closet can be fortified into one. **Register a safe room or shelter with the local fire department**, so rescuers know it is there if the entrance is blocked by debris. https://www.spc.noaa.gov/faq/tornado/ · retrieved 2026-07-29»
**The safe room, specifically.** Clear it now, not during the warning. It should hold, permanently: the
helmets, water, a torch, a whistle each, shoes, the go-bags, a charged battery pack, the weather radio,
and a heavy blanket or mattress you can pull over yourselves.

And one free action almost nobody takes: **register your safe room or shelter with the local fire
department.** If the entrance is buried in debris, that register is how rescuers know there are people
under it. If it is a bathroom, the tub with a
mattress over it is a genuinely good option. If it is a closet, empty the top shelf of anything heavy.

**The drill.** Twice a year, at a random moment, say the word and time it. Everyone, including the pets,
in the room with helmets on. If it takes more than ninety seconds, something in the route is wrong —
usually a door that does not open, an animal nobody can catch, or a child who does not know where to go.

**If you live in a manufactured home, the plan is different and it has to be made now.** No amount of
tying down makes it survivable in a strong tornado. Your plan is to leave for a specific identified
shelter — a community shelter, a relative's basement, a public building you have confirmed is open — and
your trigger has to fire at the *watch*, not the warning, because the drive takes time you will not have
later. Identify the place, confirm it is accessible at night, and write the trigger down.

## During

The decisions arrive in this order.

**On a watch** — conditions are favourable, hours ahead. Charge everything. Bring in loose objects.
Clear the safe room. Put the helmets and shoes in it. Fuel the car. If you are in a manufactured home,
this is your trigger: go now, to the identified place. If you have plans that would leave you in a car
or a public building this evening, change them.

**On a warning** — a tornado is occurring or imminent in your area. **Go. Now. Do not look.**

There is no version of this where confirming first is the better play. The whole content of the Joplin
finding is that the confirmation step is where the seventeen minutes between the warning and the
touchdown went. Take the helmets, take the
phones, take the animals, get in the room, close the door, get low, cover your head, and stay there
until the warning is cancelled — not until it goes quiet, because the quiet is frequently the middle.

**If you are caught in a vehicle:** if the tornado is visibly distant and the roads are clear and open,
drive at right angles away from it. If it is not — if you cannot see it, if it is at night, if traffic is
stopped, if you are inside heavy rain — abandon the vehicle for the lowest ground you can reach, lie
face down, cover your head with your arms. This is a bad option chosen over a worse one. Never shelter
under an overpass: it accelerates wind through a confined space, and on 3 May 1999 near Newcastle and
Moore, Oklahoma, two people were blown out from under bridges on I-44 and I-35 and killed, along with a
third in a truck parked under a bridge.
«SRC: NOAA Storm Prediction Center, "The Online Tornado FAQ" — on 3 May 1999 two people were killed and others injured when a violent tornado blew them out from under bridges on I-44 and I-35 near Newcastle and Moore, Oklahoma, and a third was killed in a truck parked under a bridge. Vehicles are described as notorious death traps; the guidance is to leave the vehicle for sturdy shelter or drive out of the path, and if neither is possible, park clear of traffic lanes and lie flat in a low spot away from the road. https://www.spc.noaa.gov/faq/tornado/ · retrieved 2026-07-29»

**If you are in a large building** — a shop, a school, a warehouse — get to an interior room or corridor
on the lowest floor, away from the wide-span roof. Long-span roofs over gymnasiums, supermarkets and
warehouses fail early. Avoid the glass frontage entirely.

**If you are outdoors with no building available**, a ditch or culvert away from vehicles and trees,
face down, arms over your head. Flying debris is the mechanism; getting below the debris stream is the
only available protection.

## After

**The first ten minutes.** Stay put until the warning is cancelled; more than one tornado from the same
system is common. Then, before you move, put shoes on. Check everyone. Do not use a lighter or a match,
because gas lines break.

**Getting out.** If the structure has failed around you, do not force your way through unstable debris —
make noise instead. A whistle carries further than a voice and lasts longer, which is why one lives in
the safe room per person.

**Utilities.** Assume every downed line is live. Smell for gas before entering anything, and if you
smell it, do not touch a switch and do not use a phone inside — Protection 129. Shut the gas off only if
you smell or hear a leak, or are told to.

**The injuries in the hours after** are cuts and punctures from debris and nails, and they get infected.
Protection 65 covers wound care; the practical rule is that a puncture wound from disaster debris needs
medical review and a tetanus check, and "it's only a nail" is how people lose weeks.

**Photograph before you touch anything** — Protection 105. Then tarp what is open, board what is broken,
keep every receipt, and start the log.

**The contractors will arrive before the adjuster.** Post-tornado contractor fraud is organised and it
targets exactly the people who have just lost a house. Sign nothing on the day. Verify the licence.
Never pay in full up front.

**Look after the household's minds, not just the building.** Protection 66 exists because children who
have been through this show it later rather than immediately, and because adults who are functioning
well in week one are frequently not functioning in month three. Losing a house is a bereavement, and
treating it as an administrative problem is how people fall over.
