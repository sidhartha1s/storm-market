# Part Three, Playbook 16 — Volcanic Ashfall

Target: 4 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

Mount St. Helens erupted on the morning of 18 May 1980 and killed **fifty-seven people.** The lateral
blast and the lahars — volcanic mudflows — did the killing close in. But the ash went east. USGS puts it
at **520 million tons of ash carried eastward across the United States, detectable over 22,000 square
miles**, falling **ten inches deep ten miles downwind, one inch at sixty miles, and half an inch three
hundred miles out** — and causing **complete darkness in Spokane, Washington, 250 miles from the
volcano.**
«SRC: US Geological Survey, "Mount St. Helens — From the 1980 Eruption to 2000," Fact Sheet 036-00. |
https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/2000/fs036-00/fs036-00.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

So the practical crisis for hundreds of thousands of people was not lava. It was gray powder, everywhere,
that stopped the vehicles, blocked the filters, loaded the roofs and could not be swept away because
there was nowhere to put it.

This playbook is deliberately short and deliberately narrow. If you live on the flank of an active volcano
in Washington, Oregon, Alaska or Hawaii, your hazard is lahars, pyroclastic flows and lava, and the
authoritative source is your local volcano observatory's hazard map — not a general preparedness book.

**What this playbook covers is the hazard that reaches ordinary households hundreds of miles away: ashfall.**
That is the version most American readers could plausibly experience, and it is genuinely manageable.

---

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Ash is pulverized rock, not soot.** That single fact explains everything below. It is abrasive, angular,
heavy, electrically conductive when wet, and it does not burn or decompose.

**Killed or injured by:** roof collapse under accumulated ash; respiratory distress in people with asthma,
COPD or heart disease; traffic accidents in near-zero visibility on roads that become extremely slippery;
and cardiac events during the clean-up, which is heavy, prolonged work in bad air.

**Ash is heavy, it packs, and unlike snow it never melts off.** Wet ash is substantially heavier than dry.
This book does not print a density figure or a collapse depth, because those depend on the ash and the
roof and a number here would be read as permission to wait and see.

What the federal guidance does say is where the danger to *you* is, and it is not where people expect:
**"Do not get on your roof to remove ash unless you have guidance or training."** Ash makes surfaces
slippery, and — the instruction that should stop anyone — *"be careful not to contribute additional weight
to an overloaded roof."* You are part of the load. Clear from the ground where you can, wear protective
clothing and a mask, and keep children out of the clean-up entirely.
«SRC: Ready.gov / FEMA, "Volcanoes" — 161 potentially active volcanoes in the United States; ash can
travel hundreds of miles; do not get on the roof to remove ash without guidance or training; do not
contribute additional weight to an overloaded roof; protective clothing and a mask for clean-up; children
should not help. | https://www.ready.gov/volcanoes | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**Costs:** roofs and gutters; vehicle engines and brakes, because ash destroys air filters and scores
cylinders; HVAC systems; water supplies where the ash lands in open reservoirs and tanks; electronics, from
conductive dust and blocked cooling; and the clean-up itself, which is expensive and slow because ash has to
be removed rather than washed away — into the storm drains it sets like concrete.

## Before

- **56 — the ashfall specifics** protection covers the kit: **N95 respirators** for everyone, and proper
  goggles rather than glasses, because ash abrades corneas and contact lens wearers suffer badly.
- **49 — HVAC filtration and recirculation**, plus knowing how to shut the system down entirely, which is
  the correct setting for heavy ashfall rather than recirculation with a loaded filter.
- **51 — sealing a room**, and here plastic sheeting and tape earn their place: ash gets through gaps that
  smoke does not seem to.
- **Plan the roof.** Know its slope and its condition, know whether it can be cleared safely from the
  ground with a roof rake, and know who you would call. Clear the gutters before the season if you are in an
  ashfall corridor.
- **1 — stored water**, covered, because ash contaminates open supplies and tanks and it is chemically
  active enough to change the water's taste and acidity. Cover water butts, disconnect rain capture, and
  cover pools and hot tubs.
- **Vehicle preparation:** a spare air filter and the knowledge of how to change it, and the acceptance
  that driving in ashfall damages the car and should be avoided.
- **Cover the electronics**, and plan to keep computers and equipment closed and covered rather than running
  during ashfall, because cooling fans pull conductive dust straight into them.
- **Sign up for your volcano observatory's notifications** if you are in a corridor, and know the alert
  levels.

## During

**Stay indoors while ash is falling.** That is the primary protection and it is nearly free.

Close every window and door. Shut down the HVAC entirely — do not run it on recirculate with ash in the
system. Close the fireplace damper. Seal gaps with tape and sheeting where ash is getting in.

**If you must go out: N95 and goggles**, long sleeves, and no contact lenses. Wet a cloth for the face only
as a last resort, because it is not a respirator.

**Do not drive.** Visibility collapses, roads become slippery, and ash destroys engines. If you are caught
out, drive slowly with the lights on and the ventilation closed to recirculate, and pull off safely to wait
it out. Then expect to change the air filter.

**Watch the roof, and act on accumulation rather than on the eruption ending.** This is the decision that
saves lives. If ash is accumulating to a few inches, and especially if rain is falling onto ash, the roof
needs clearing — and it needs clearing progressively rather than all at once at the end.

**Clear it symmetrically**, from both sides, so you do not unbalance the load on the structure. From the
ground with a roof rake wherever possible. If someone must go up, that is a harness-and-two-people job, on
a surface that is now extremely slippery, and it is a genuinely dangerous task — a professional is the
right answer for a large or steep roof.

**Protect the people who are vulnerable to it:** asthma, COPD and heart disease sufferers should be in the
sealed room with filtration running on battery or mains, with medication to hand.

**And keep pets and livestock in** if you can, out of the ash and with covered water and feed.

---

## After

**Clean-up is the dangerous phase**, and the main risks are inhalation, falls and cardiac events.

**Wet it lightly before moving it** — enough to stop it becoming airborne, not enough to make it heavier
than you can lift. This is a real balance and it matters.

**Never dry-sweep, and never use a household vacuum.** Ash destroys vacuum cleaners and the exhaust puts
the finest and most harmful fraction back into the air.

**Bag it and dispose of it where your municipality directs.** Do not wash it into storm drains or the sewer,
because it sets, and do not shovel it into the garden in quantity.

**Protective equipment throughout:** N95, goggles, gloves, long sleeves, sturdy footwear. Keep children and
anyone with respiratory disease out of it entirely.

**Take breaks and treat it as heavy exertion**, because the cardiac risk here is the same as snow
shoveling and the air is worse.

**Then the machinery.** Change the vehicle air filter before driving any distance, and change it again
sooner than the schedule says. Check the brakes, because ash acts as a grinding paste. Do not run HVAC
until the system and filters have been cleaned and replaced.

**Then the water.** Do not use rain-captured water that has received ash. Flush and check any tank that was
open. Follow the utility's guidance on the mains supply, and expect taste and treatment changes for a
while.

**And the honest note about ash indoors:** it takes multiple cleaning passes over weeks, it gets
re-suspended by ordinary movement, and it will come back in on shoes and clothes for months. Damp cleaning,
doormats, shoes off at the door, and filters changed repeatedly.
