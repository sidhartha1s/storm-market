# Part Four, Chapter Two — Places

House · apartment · manufactured home · the car · work and school

Target: 16 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## Why this chapter exists

Most preparedness writing assumes a detached house that the reader owns, with a basement, a garage, a garden
and permission to drill into anything.

A very large share of American households have none of that. Renters cannot bolt a house to its foundation.
Apartment dwellers have no basement and no roof of their own. Manufactured-home residents face a structural
reality that no amount of hardening changes. And every household spends a third of its life somewhere else
entirely — at work, at school, or in a car.

**A protection you are not allowed to install is not a protection.** So this chapter takes the same hazards
and asks what is actually available to you where you actually live.

---

## The detached house you own

This is the case the rest of the book is written for, so the note here is short and it is about sequence.

The structural protections in Part Two (69 to 84) are almost all **timed to something else**. The sealed roof
deck, the ring-shank renailing and the impact shingles happen at a re-roof. The hurricane straps happen when
the roof is open. The flood openings and elevated mechanicals happen during a renovation or a repair. The
seismic anchoring is the exception — it is a weekend and a hundred dollars of hardware, and it can happen this
month.

**So the plan for an owner is: do the free and cheap things now, and keep a written list of what to demand
next time a contractor is on the property for any reason.** That list is worth thousands of dollars and it
costs nothing to maintain. It goes in the trashder.

**And know your own building.** Where the water main, gas meter and breaker panel are. Which way the valves
turn. What the roof covering is and how old it is. Whether the crawl space floods. Where the sump discharges.
Which walls are load-bearing. Most owners cannot answer half of these, and every answer changes a decision
during an event.

## The apartment or condominium

**What you cannot do:** touch the structure, the roof, the openings, the mechanical systems or anything
outside your door. That removes about a third of this book.

**What you can do, and it is more than people assume:**

**Know which floor to be on for which hazard, because the answers conflict.** For tornado and high wind, the
lowest floor and an interior room — a bathroom or an interior corridor away from windows. For flooding, up.
For a hazmat release, usually up, because vapors pool low. For fire, down and out. Work these out for your
building now, in advance, because in an emergency you cannot reason through four conflicting rules.

**Learn the building's own plan**, which exists and which most residents have never read: the fire evacuation
routes, the assembly point, whether there is a designated shelter area, how the building's alarm system works,
what happens to the lifts and the water supply in a power outage, and whether there is a generator and what it
actually runs. Ask management directly, in writing if necessary.

**The lift is the single biggest practical fact.** In an outage it stops, and in a high-rise that means the
stairs, in the dark, possibly many floors, possibly carrying somebody. If anyone in your household cannot do
that, the plan has to be built around it — which means either leaving early on a warning or having a genuine
arrangement for assistance. Know how many floors, and count them once with the lights off and a flashlight.

**Water pressure** in many multi-story buildings depends on electric pumps, so an outage means no water on
the upper floors regardless of what the mains is doing. Store water accordingly (Protection 1), and fill
everything on a warning (Protection 3).

**Windows are your exposure.** You cannot install shutters, but you can move furniture away from glass, keep
heavy curtains closed during a wind event, know which room has the fewest windows, and use interior film if
your lease permits it.

**Renter's insurance** is the protection this section exists to insist on. It is inexpensive, it covers your
contents — the building is the owner's problem — it usually includes loss of use, which pays for you to live
somewhere while the building is repaired, and it usually excludes flood exactly like a homeowner policy, which
means ground-floor and basement apartments in flood-exposed areas need a separate flood policy.

**This book does not print a national take-up percentage for renter's insurance**, because the figures in
circulation come from insurers' own marketing surveys rather than from any regulator's count, and the
range between them is wide enough to be useless. What can be said without a number: **a renter's policy is
among the cheapest insurance products sold in the United States, and a renter without one has no coverage
for their possessions at all** — the landlord's policy covers the building and owes you nothing. If you
rent and you are not certain you have a policy in force, that is a ten-minute phone call and it is the
highest-return ten minutes in this chapter.

Ask specifically about **loss of use**, which pays for somewhere to live while the building is repaired
and is the part that turns a fire two floors below you from a catastrophe into an inconvenience.

**Then the neighbors, who matter more here than in a house.** In a building, an emergency is collective: the
stairwell, the alarm, the water, the lift and the exits are shared. Knowing three neighbors by name — and
knowing which of them is elderly, disabled or alone — is a genuine protection, and it is the one that gets
someone knocked on when the power has been off for two days.

**Balconies and shared spaces:** anything on a balcony is a projectile in high wind, and it becomes your
liability. Bring it in on a warning.

## The manufactured or mobile home

This section is written plainly because the data is plain, and a book that softened it would be doing its
readers harm.

**A manufactured home is not survivable shelter in a strong tornado or in hurricane-force wind.** The
weather service states the arithmetic without softening it: on average **72 per cent of all tornado
fatalities occur in homes, and 54 per cent of those are in mobile homes.** More than half. **In a mobile
home you are 15 to 20 times more likely to be killed than in a permanent home.**

**Three details make the case final.** First, the threshold is far lower than people assume: **an EF-1
tornado — or high-end severe thunderstorm winds, with no tornado at all — can completely destroy a mobile
or manufactured home.** Second, on anchoring: *"regardless of how well built a mobile or manufactured home
is built, anchor system failures are the primary cause of the majority of fatalities."* The tie-downs are
the failure point, not the answer. And third, *"even well-built manufactured homes can be destroyed if they
become airborne."*

**Then the timing instruction, which is the actionable part and the one most often got wrong: leave on the
WATCH, not the warning.** The weather service's reasoning: *"when a Tornado Warning is issued, it may be
too dangerous to take your evacuation route."* By the time the warning fires, the drive to shelter is
itself the hazard. The day before, when tornadoes are in the forecast, arrange where you are going — a
relative's house, a designated shelter, a reinforced-concrete or community building — and time the journey
so you know how long you actually need.
«SRC: NOAA National Weather Service, Jackson MS, "Severe Weather Preparedness Week: Mobile & Manufactured
Home Safety" — NOAA and FEMA recommend that mobile and manufactured home residents flee for sturdier
shelter before storms with tornadoes hit; on average 72 per cent of all tornado-related fatalities are in
homes and 54 per cent of those are in mobile homes; in a mobile home you are 15 to 20 times more likely to
be killed than in a permanent home; EF-1 tornadoes and high-end severe thunderstorm winds can completely
destroy mobile and manufactured homes; "regardless of how well built a mobile or manufactured home is
built, anchor system failures are the primary cause of the majority of fatalities"; even well-built
manufactured homes can be destroyed if they become airborne; evacuate when a Tornado Watch is issued
because by the time a warning is issued it may be too dangerous to take the evacuation route. |
https://www.weather.gov/jan/swpw_mhsafety | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**So the plan is different in kind, not in degree: your protection is leaving, and your trigger is earlier
than everybody else's.**

**Identify the place now.** A community storm shelter, a neighbor's or relative's basement, a public building
you have confirmed is accessible, a park office shelter. Confirm three things about it: that it is genuinely
open at night, that you can reach it in the time available, and that you know how to get in. Many
manufactured-home communities have a designated shelter and many residents have never located it.

**Then set the trigger at the watch, not the warning** (Protection 95). This is the crucial difference. A
site-built house can wait for a warning because sheltering takes ninety seconds. You need driving or walking
time, at night, possibly in rain, and a warning does not give you that. When a tornado watch is issued for
your area and conditions are serious, you go — and you accept that you will do this several times for nothing,
because that is what the arithmetic requires.

**If leaving at every watch is not possible**, do this, in this order.

- **Always leave on the worst days.** The Storm Prediction Center rates each day's risk from 1 to 5. Level 4
  is Moderate and Level 5 is High; both mean widespread severe weather or an outbreak is expected. A tornado
  watch that says "particularly dangerous situation" means forecasters are confident of multiple intense
  tornadoes. On any of these days, you leave at the watch.
  «SRC: NOAA Storm Prediction Center, "SPC Products" — categorical outlook levels 1-MRGL, 2-SLGT, 3-ENH,
  4-MDT ("an area where widespread severe weather with several tornadoes and/or numerous severe
  thunderstorms is likely") and 5-HIGH ("an area where a severe weather outbreak is expected from either
  numerous intense and long-tracked tornadoes ..."); a tornado watch carries the
  wording "particularly dangerous situation" whenever there is a high-confidence forecast of multiple
  intense (EF2–EF5) tornadoes. | https://www.spc.noaa.gov/misc/about.html | retrieved 2026-10-27»
- **Always leave at the watch if grandchildren or a slow walker are in the house.** No exceptions. They are
  the people who cannot be hurried when the warning comes.
- **Have three places, not one.** Write each down with its drive time. First, a neighbor's in-ground shelter:
  ask them now, not on the day. Second, a public building you have confirmed will be open. Third, your
  city's shelter registry, if it has one. Ask the park manager, in writing, whether the park has a shelter
  and who opens it. The weather service's own checklist says the same: plan more than one place, check
  with emergency management that shelters will open, and talk to family and friends about staying with them.
- **Time everyone to the car.** Do it once with the whole household, including the slowest person and the
  pet. That number, not a guess, is how early you must go.
  «SRC: NOAA National Weather Service, "Manufactured/Mobile Home Safety" one-page guide (2023) — every day:
  have a family plan with ideas for shelter spaces and emergency locations; know how long it will take you
  to evacuate your home during severe weather; day before: check with your community's emergency management
  on whether shelters will be open, and talk with family and friends about spending time at their home if
  it is safer; day of: if a tornado watch is issued and storms are headed toward your area, evacuate to a
  sturdier shelter, with your pets. | https://www.weather.gov/media/wrn/Mobile-Home-Safety-One-Pager-Final.pdf
  | retrieved 2026-10-27»

**Caught at home by a warning, with nowhere to go.** The weather service says a mobile home is not a safe
place to shelter, whatever kind it is. If the warning comes before you leave, it may already be too
dangerous to drive. Its last-resort options are two. Drive away, seatbelt on, to a sturdy building. Or move
to a protected place outside, shielded from flying debris and from flooding. If you are caught in the car,
it says either get down in the car and cover your head, or leave the car for a low spot such as a ditch.
These are last resorts. None of them is a plan.
«SRC: NOAA National Weather Service, "Manufactured/Mobile Home Safety" one-page guide (2023) — no matter what
type of manufactured home you live in, it is not a safe place to take shelter; if a Tornado Warning is issued
before you evacuate, it may already be too dangerous to evacuate; last resort options include driving away
(seatbelt on) to a sturdy structure or moving to a protected area outside shielded from potential debris or
flooding. | https://www.weather.gov/media/wrn/Mobile-Home-Safety-One-Pager-Final.pdf | retrieved 2026-10-27»
«SRC: NOAA National Weather Service, "What to do During a Tornado" — a mobile home is not safe; if you are

retrieved 2026-10-27»

**What this does not do:** none of this makes a mobile home safe. It gives you somewhere else to be.

**What is still worth doing to the home itself:**

- **Proper anchoring and tie-downs**, inspected and to your state's standard. This does not make it
  tornado-survivable. It genuinely helps in lower-grade wind, in hail and derecho events, and it is frequently
  required by law and by insurance.
- **Skirting secured**, because it protects the underbelly and the plumbing, which is the most freeze-vulnerable
  plumbing in American housing.
- **Pipe insulation and freeze protection** (Protection 34), which matters more here than in any other housing
  type because supply lines run in unheated space under the floor. Heat tape, insulation, and knowing where the
  shut-off is.
- **Smoke and CO alarms**, and an honest note: fire spreads extremely fast in a manufactured home and the
  escape time is short. Two exits, both clear, practiced — including the bedroom window as an exit, which
  should be openable by everyone who sleeps there.
- **Elevated and secured propane tanks**, and knowing how to shut the gas off.
- **In a flood zone**, a manufactured home floats and moves, which makes the leave-early rule apply to water as
  well as wind.

**And the insurance point:** manufactured homes are insured under different products from site-built houses,
with different structures and frequently actual-cash-value settlement. Read the policy against Protection
102's checklist, and be specific about wind and hail.

## The car

**The car is where a great deal of disaster death happens** — in flash floods, in tornadoes, in wildfire
evacuations, in dust storms, in blizzards. Protection 115 covers the rules. This section covers the vehicle
itself.

**What lives in it, permanently:**

- A go-bag or at least the core of one, especially during your region's season.
- Water and some food that tolerates heat.
- A flashlight, a phone charger and a cable, and a battery pack.
- A blanket, and in winter warm layers, hat and gloves for each regular occupant.
- Sturdy shoes, because people evacuate in whatever they were wearing.
- A first aid kit, and ideally a tourniquet if you are trained.
- **A window-breaking tool on the keyring** and a seatbelt cutter — the flash-flood escape from Protection
  115.
- A high-visibility item and a whistle.
- In winter: a shovel, a scraper, grit or sand, and traction aids.
- Paper maps of your region, because the phone is not reliable when you need it.

**Rotate the water and food twice a year**, because a car interior destroys both faster than a cupboard does.

**Maintenance is a preparedness activity here**, not a chore: tires with real tread, working wipers and
lights, a battery that is not on its last year, and the coolant and washer fluid appropriate to the season.
An evacuation is the wrong moment to find out about any of them.

**The half-tank rule** (Protection 110) in season, and the same for an electric vehicle with the added
knowledge of where the chargers are on both evacuation routes and how long a stop takes.

**Then the rules people forget.** Never leave a person or an animal in a parked car, in any weather that is
even mildly warm. Never park under a large tree during a storm season if you have an alternative. Do not shelter
in a car from a tornado if a building is available. And in an outage, the car is a power source and a phone
charger (Protection 44) — run outdoors, never in an attached garage.

## Work, school and away from home

**The plan that only works at home is a plan that works a third of the time.**

**At work**, find out the same things you found out about your apartment building: where the shelter area is,
what the evacuation route and assembly point are, what the alarm means, whether there is a plan for staying
overnight if the roads close, and who is responsible. Then keep a minimal personal kit in a drawer or a
locker: water, a snack, medication for a day or two, a flashlight, a phone charger, comfortable shoes, and a copy of
the household contact sheet. That is a small box and it covers the overwhelming majority of
stranded-at-work scenarios.

**If you work outdoors**, the heat, cold, smoke and lightning playbooks are your occupational safety
documents, and several states set enforceable employer requirements at defined thresholds. Know them.

**The reunification question is the one that actually matters**, and it has to be answered in advance because
in the event nobody will be able to reach anybody: **where does this household meet, and who collects the
children?**

Two locations. **One near home** — a specific neighbor's driveway, a named tree, a corner — for the case where
the house is unsafe but the area is fine. **One outside the area** — a named relative, a specific parking lot, a
named town — for the case where the whole area is evacuated. Both written down, both known by everyone
including the children, both reachable without a phone.

Then the out-of-area contact (Protection 112) as the hub, and the rule that everybody messages the hub rather
than trying to reach each other.

**And the standing agreement that prevents the worst outcome: nobody goes back for anybody.** Everybody goes
to the agreed place and waits. This is the hardest thing in this chapter to agree to and the most important,
because the deaths that happen after the event happen to people driving back into it.

**Travel.** When you are away from your region you are in someone else's hazard: hurricane season in a coastal
hotel, wildfire season in a national park, tornado season in a motel with no basement. Two minutes on arrival
answers it — what is the hazard here, what does the building's plan say, and where would we go. Hotel rooms have
an evacuation notice on the door that almost nobody reads.
