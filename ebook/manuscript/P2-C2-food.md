## Food

Food is the protection people over-buy and under-think.

The instinct when someone decides to get prepared is to buy a bucket — a sealed drum of freeze-dried
meals with a twenty-five-year date, which sits in a basement and is never opened, including during the
emergency, because nobody wants to eat it and nobody has practiced cooking it. Meanwhile the same
household loses four hundred dollars of food out of a freezer in a three-day outage for want of a
twelve-dollar thermometer and a bag of ice.

The order of value is the reverse of the order of expense:

1. **Not losing what you already have** — a thermometer and a protocol. Costs almost nothing.
2. **A two-week pantry of food you actually eat** — bought at normal prices on normal shopping trips.
3. **Cooking without power, safely** — which is mostly a carbon monoxide problem, not a food problem.
4. **Long-term stores** — genuinely useful, and last on the list.

This chapter is in that order.

### 13 · The fourteen-day no-cook shelf pantry

**Prevents:** needing power, water, or a working stove in order to eat.
**Cost:** $60–150 · **Time:** two normal shopping trips · **Materials:** shelf-stable food
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 7 Ice & Snow North.

The rule that makes this work: **every item must be edible with no power, no cooking, and no added
water.** The moment a plan requires simmering, it requires fuel and water, and both are constrained in
exactly the event you are planning for.

That points at an unglamorous and effective list: canned fish, meat, and beans; canned vegetables and
fruit in juice; peanut butter; nuts; crackers; shelf-stable bread products; dried fruit; instant
oats that hydrate in cold water; cereal; long-life milk; honey; jam; shelf-stable cheese; tinned
soups that are palatable cold; electrolyte powder; coffee or tea you can make with treated water; and
something sweet, because morale on day four is a real variable and not a joke.

Two rules of composition. **Buy what your household already eats**, because an emergency is a terrible
time to introduce a new food to a child or an anxious adult. And **buy calories, not tokens** — count
the actual calories per person per day rather than counting cans. An adult under stress, working
physically, in a cold house, needs more than they do on an ordinary day, not less.

Then note the openers. A can you cannot open is not food. Protection 18 exists because of this.

**What this does not do:** it does not cover anyone with a medically restricted diet, an infant, or a
severe allergy. Those are Protection 19 and they are not optional additions — for those households
they are the primary food protection.

### 14 · The thirty-day deep pantry, built in stages

**Prevents:** the choice between a large single purchase and no depth at all.
**Cost:** spread across normal grocery spending · **Time:** months, invisibly · **Materials:** shelves
· **Regions:** all five.

Depth beyond two weeks is worth having and does not require a special budget. The method is the
**deep pantry**: buy two of what you already use, eat the front one, put the new one at the back.
Nothing is stockpiled, nothing expires, and no single purchase is painful.

Give it dedicated shelf space with the oldest at the front, and once a season eat deliberately from
the back. The failure mode of a deep pantry is not spoilage; it is drift, where the back of the shelf
silently becomes a museum.

Add staples that carry a long time and stretch everything else — rice, pasta, dried beans, flour,
oil, salt, sugar, stock — while being honest that most of these need water and heat, which is why
they sit behind Protection 13 rather than in front of it.

**What this does not do:** a deep pantry is not a long-term food store. If your risk is a
months-long disruption rather than a weeks-long one, that is a different purchase and a different
conversation.

### 15 · The refrigerator and freezer outage protocol

**Prevents:** losing several hundred dollars of food, and eating something that will make you ill.
**Cost:** $10–25 · **Time:** ten minutes · **Materials:** two appliance thermometers, frozen bottles
· **Regions:** all five. Highest dollar-return item in this chapter.

This is the protection with the best financial return in the chapter, and it is a thermometer.

The mechanism is simple: food safety is determined by **temperature and time**, not by appearance or
smell. Without a thermometer inside the appliance you have no idea which side of the line you are on,
so you either throw away food that was fine or eat food that was not.

Buy two appliance thermometers, one for the refrigerator and one for the freezer, and know the numbers
before you need them. **The refrigerator should read at or below 40°F.** After an outage, food that has
been above 40°F for **two hours or less** is safe. **Frozen food that still holds ice crystals, or reads
40°F or below, can be refrozen or cooked** — check the appliance thermometer or a food thermometer in
individual packages rather than judging by feel. And the rule that settles every argument: **if you do
not know how long it was above 40°F, throw it out.**
«SRC: FDA, "Refrigerator Thermometers — Cold Facts about Food Safety": refrigerator at or below 40°F; food above 40°F for 2 hours or less is safe; frozen food still holding ice crystals or at 40°F or below may be refrozen or cooked; if the duration above 40°F is unknown, discard. https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/refrigerator-thermometers-cold-facts-about-food-safety | retrieved 2026-07-29»

The protocol, in order:

1. **Keep the doors shut, and know the numbers.** FDA's figures: **an unopened refrigerator holds food
   cold for about 4 hours. A full freezer holds temperature for about 48 hours — 24 hours if it is half
   full.** After power returns, discard any refrigerated perishable (meat, poultry, seafood, milk, eggs,
   leftovers) that has been above 40°F for 4 hours or more; frozen food that still has ice crystals or
   reads 40°F or below is safe to refreeze or cook. And the separate rule that catches people out:
   perishable food left at room temperature for 2 hours — **1 hour if it is above 90°F outside** — is
   discarded regardless of how it looks or smells. Fifty pounds of dry ice will hold an 18-cubic-foot
   full freezer for two days. Every opening costs you. The
   free move that buys the most time: **before the power goes, freeze water in sealed bags and put them
   in both the freezer and the fridge.**
   «SRC: FDA, "Refrigerator Thermometers — Cold Facts about Food Safety" — In Case of Disaster:
   pre-freezing water in quart bags, and keeping both doors closed as much as possible.
   https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/refrigerator-thermometers-cold-facts-about-food-safety | retrieved 2026-07-29»
   «SRC: FDA, "Food and Water Safety During Power Outages and Floods." |
   https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/food-and-water-safety-during-power-outages-and-floods | retrieved 2026-07-29»
2. **Fill the empty space now, before anything happens.** Water bottles frozen in the freezer's gaps
   are free thermal mass. They extend the hold, they become drinking water as they thaw, and they can
   be moved into the refrigerator to extend that too.
3. **Consolidate.** Move freezer contents together; a solid block holds cold far better than scattered
   packages.
4. **Move the refrigerator's high-risk items into the freezer** early — dairy, meat, leftovers.
5. **When the power returns, read the thermometer, not the food.** Above the threshold for longer than
   the safe window means it goes, and that judgment does not get relitigated because the food looks
   fine.

The rule that saves people: **never taste food to check it, and never rely on smell.** The organisms
that matter do not announce themselves.

**What this does not do:** partially thawed food that is still at or below the safe threshold may be
refrozen, with quality loss but not danger — but that determination requires the thermometer. Without
one, this whole protection collapses into guessing.

### 16 · Cooking without power — outdoors, always

**Prevents:** the most common fatal mistake made after a storm.
**Cost:** $0–90 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** an outdoor cooking method
· **Regions:** all five.

This protection is short because it is a rule, not a technique.

**Nothing that burns fuel is ever operated indoors.** Not a charcoal grill, not a gas grill, not a
camp stove, not a propane heater rated for outdoor use, not a generator, and not an oven with its door
open. Not in the garage, not in a breezeway, not next to an open window, not "just for a few minutes".

Charcoal is the most lethal item on that list because it produces enormous quantities of carbon
monoxide with no flame and no noise, and because it is the one people most often bring into a
sheltered space in cold weather.
«SRC: CDC, "Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics" — never use a generator inside a home or garage, even
with doors and windows open; operate generators outdoors more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents;
use a battery-powered or battery-backup CO detector. |
https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»

What to actually own: a propane camp stove with spare canisters, or a grill you already have, used
outdoors and away from the building. Cook once and eat twice — a hot meal costs fuel, so batch it.

The reason this rule is stated as an absolute with no exceptions is in Chapter One of Part One: the
exception is always invented at two in the morning by a cold, tired person whose judgment is already
impaired by the early stages of the poisoning.

**What this does not do:** outdoor cooking is not always possible. In a wildfire smoke event, an
ashfall, a chemical release, or a hurricane's wind field, going outside to cook is wrong — which is
why Protection 13 requires food that needs no cooking at all.

### 17 · Fuel storage and rotation

**Prevents:** a stove with no canisters and a generator with stale fuel.
**Cost:** $20–80 · **Time:** an hour · **Materials:** canisters or approved fuel containers
· **Regions:** all five.

Cooking fuel and generator fuel are both consumables that people buy once and then treat as
permanent. Propane canisters are stable for years and are the easy case. Gasoline is not — it degrades
over months, and degraded fuel is a leading cause of generators that will not start on the day they
are needed.

Store fuel outside the living space, in approved containers, away from ignition sources and out of
direct sun. Never store gasoline in the house. Use a stabilizer if you keep gasoline, label the container
with the date it was filled, and rotate it through a vehicle or mower rather than letting it sit.

**What this does not do:** fuel storage has a legal quantity limit in many jurisdictions and an
insurance implication in some. Check both rather than assuming, and never solve a fuel problem by
storing more of it indoors.

### 18 · Openers and redundancy

**Prevents:** a two-week pantry you cannot get into.
**Cost:** $5–15 · **Time:** five minutes · **Materials:** two manual can openers
· **Regions:** all five.

Two manual can openers, because they fail and because one will be missing. One lives with the pantry
and one lives in the go-bag. Test them both — a cheap opener that skips on a thick rim is not a tool.

The same logic covers anything in your plan that has exactly one failure point: a bottle opener, a
sharp knife, a lighter and matches both, a spare set of batteries in the size your flashlight actually
takes.

**What this does not do:** nothing. This is a five-dollar protection with no downside, which is why it
is in the free week of the sixty days.

### 19 · Special diets, infant feeding, and allergies

**Prevents:** a plan that works for everyone in the house except the one who needs it most.
**Cost:** varies · **Time:** an evening · **Materials:** the specific items
· **Regions:** all five.

For some households this is the most important protection in the chapter, and generic advice is
useless here — so this is a set of instructions rather than a list.

**Infants.** The critical insight is water dependence. Powdered formula requires safe water at a safe
temperature; ready-to-feed liquid formula requires neither. FDA's own instruction for flood and outage
conditions is exactly this: *"For infants, try to use prepared, canned baby formula that requires no
added water."* In an emergency where the water supply is uncertain, ready-to-feed is the safer form,
and a supply of it belongs in the store even if powdered is what you normally use.
«SRC: FDA, "Food and Water Safety During Power Outages and Floods." |
https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/food-and-water-safety-during-power-outages-and-floods | retrieved 2026-07-29» Bottles and teats need treated water for cleaning, which is a demand people
forget to count.

**Allergies.** Cross-contamination risk rises sharply when food comes from unfamiliar sources —
donations, shelters, neighbors. A household with a severe allergy needs its own labeled, sealed
supply and in-date emergency medication, and needs to assume that nothing offered to them is safe
without a label.

**Medically restricted diets.** Renal, diabetic, coeliac, low-sodium. Most emergency food is
extremely high in sodium, which is a genuine hazard for several of these. Build the store against the
restriction, not against a generic list.

**Elderly household members.** Chewing and swallowing difficulty is common and rarely planned for.
Soft, ready-to-eat, nutrient-dense items rather than crackers and nuts.

**What this does not do:** this protection cannot be delegated to a generic kit. Every pre-made
emergency kit on the market ignores all four of these cases.

### 20 · Pet and animal food

**Prevents:** the choice nobody should have to make on day five.
**Cost:** $20–60 · **Time:** one shopping trip · **Materials:** food, water, medication
· **Regions:** all five.

Two weeks of pet food, in a sealed container, rotated like everything else. Two weeks of any pet
medication. Water counted into the total from Protection 1, at a realistic rate rather than a
sentimental one.

Also: a manual can opener that works on the pet food, a spare bowl, and — the item everyone forgets —
vaccination records, because most shelters and most pet-friendly accommodation will ask for them and
will refuse without them. Those records belong in the document vault in Protection 96, not in a drawer.

Livestock and large animals are a different scale of problem and are outside this book; see the note under Protection 109.

**What this does not do:** food does not solve evacuation. That is Protection 109, and it needs
carriers, records, and a pre-built list of places that will actually take an animal.

### 21 · Food safety after floodwater

**Prevents:** eating something contaminated because it looked sealed.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** judgment, applied once
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, Ice & Snow North, all flood-exposed households.

Floodwater is not water. It is sewage, agricultural runoff, fuel, and whatever was in the buildings
upstream. Anything it touches is contaminated, and the rules are deliberately harsh because the
failure mode is serious illness in a period with no medical access.

**Discard** anything porous or imperfectly sealed that floodwater reached: cardboard, paper, cloth
packaging, screw-top and crimp-top bottles and jars, containers with pull tabs or peel-off tops, home
canning, and anything with a compromised seam. Home-canned goods go regardless of appearance, because
the seal cannot be verified.

**Undamaged commercially canned goods can be salvaged** — and so can retort pouches, the flexible
shelf-stable kind. FDA's procedure, in order:

1. Remove the labels if they are removable; they harbor dirt and bacteria.
2. Brush or wipe away dirt and silt.
3. Wash thoroughly with soap and water, hot if you have it.
4. Rinse with water that is safe to drink — residual soap and dirt reduce the effect of the chlorine.
5. Sanitize by immersion, one of two ways: **boil for 2 minutes**, or soak 15 minutes in **1 cup of
   unscented 5.25% household bleach per 5 gallons of water.**
6. **Air dry for at least an hour** before opening or storing.
7. Re-label with a permanent marker, including the expiration date.

Use reconditioned cans as soon as possible afterwards. Discard any can with swelling, leakage,
punctures, holes, fractures, extensive deep rusting, or denting severe enough to stop it stacking or
to stop a manual can opener working on it.
«SRC: FDA, "Food and Water Safety During Power Outages and Floods." |
https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/food-and-water-safety-during-power-outages-and-floods | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**What this does not do:** cleaning a can does not rescue its contents if the seal failed, and no
amount of washing makes porous packaging safe. When the judgment is close, the answer is discard —
this is the one place in the book where being wasteful is correct.

### 22 · Cooler and ice strategy

**Prevents:** a multi-day outage becoming a total loss.
**Cost:** $0–120 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** a cooler, frozen bottles
· **Regions:** all five.

A cooler is the bridge between a dead refrigerator and a working one, and it is also the container for
the item that matters most: **refrigerated medication**. That is Protection 60, and it is the reason
this protection is not merely about convenience.

Freeze water bottles now, in the gaps in the freezer. When the power fails, the coldest items and the
medication go into the cooler with the frozen bottles, the cooler goes somewhere cool and shaded, and
it stays shut.

Know where block ice is sold near you before you need it, because it lasts far longer than cubed, and
know that it sells out within hours of a regional outage. Dry ice is an option with real hazards —
never in a sealed space, never handled bare, never in a closed vehicle with people — and if you use
it, read the handling guidance first.

**What this does not do:** a cooler is a delay, not a solution. For refrigerated medication
specifically, know the actual temperature tolerances from the pharmacist and the manufacturer rather
than assuming "cold is cold" — some medications are damaged by freezing as well as by warmth.
