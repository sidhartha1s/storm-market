# Part Three, Playbook 10 — Extreme Heat

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

At the end of June 2021, a heat dome settled over the Pacific Northwest — a region where air
conditioning was uncommon because it had never been needed. Portland reached temperatures no reading in
its record had approached. Hundreds of people died in Oregon, Washington and British Columbia, most of
them indoors, most of them alone, most of them elderly, and a large majority of them in homes with no
air conditioning.
«CITE: Oregon/Washington/BC official mortality reviews of the late-June 2021 heat dome — death tolls, peak
temperatures, and the share of decedents living alone without air conditioning.»

Two things make heat the hazard this book takes most seriously despite its complete lack of drama.

**Extreme heat is responsible for the highest number of annual deaths among all weather-related
hazards.** More than tornadoes, hurricanes and floods — and it does it invisibly, one person at a time,
indoors, recorded as a cardiac or respiratory death.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Extreme Heat" — extreme heat is a period of high heat and humidity with temperatures above 90°F for at least two to three days, and **"extreme heat is responsible for the highest number of annual deaths among all weather-related hazards."** https://www.ready.gov/heat · retrieved 2026-07-29»

**And the deaths are concentrated in a completely knowable group**: people who are old, who live alone,
who are on certain medications, who have no working air conditioning, who cannot afford to run the one
they have, and who have nobody checking on them. That is not a forecast problem. That is a list, and it is
mostly a list of people somebody knows.

---

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**The mechanism** is a body that cannot shed heat fast enough. Sweating is the primary mechanism, and it
fails in high humidity, in the very old and very young, in dehydration, and in people whose medication
interferes with sweating or fluid balance — diuretics, some blood pressure drugs, anticholinergics, some
antidepressants and antipsychotics. The heart works harder to move blood to the skin, and in someone with
existing cardiac disease that is the fatal event.

**Heat exhaustion** is heavy sweating, cold clammy skin, weakness, nausea, headache, cramps, a fast weak
pulse. It is treatable where the person is: cool place, cool water, loosen clothing, cool cloths, sip
fluids.

**Heat stroke is a medical emergency and it looks different** — a body temperature above about 103°F,
skin that is hot and often dry rather than sweating, a fast strong pulse, confusion, slurred speech, and
collapse or seizure. **Call emergency services and start active cooling immediately** (Protection 64).
Cold water immersion where feasible, or ice packs to the neck, armpits and groin, and cool wet cloths
everywhere. Do not give fluids to someone who is confused or unconscious. Minutes matter — heat stroke
causes organ damage and death in proportion to how long the temperature stays up.

**Who dies:** adults over sixty-five, especially living alone; infants and young children; people with
heart disease, respiratory disease, diabetes, kidney disease, obesity or mental illness; people on the
medications above; outdoor workers; athletes; and people without air conditioning or without the money to
run it.

**The compounding failure that makes this a disaster rather than a discomfort** is heat plus grid failure.
A heat wave drives record electricity demand at exactly the moment when generation and transmission are
least efficient, and the outages arrive when air conditioning becomes the difference between survivable
and lethal. That is why this playbook and Playbook 17 are read together.

**Costs:** the direct costs are low — electricity bills, a hotel night, a portable unit — which is exactly
why households under-invest against the weather hazard with the highest annual death toll.

## Before

- **29 — the cooling room.** Pick it now, the way the winter plan picks a warm room. Lowest floor,
  north-facing or shaded, fewest windows, where the one air conditioner or the best fan setup lives, where
  the household sleeps during an event.
- **30 — reflective film and exterior shading.** Exterior shading beats interior shading by a wide margin,
  because it stops the heat before it enters the glass: awnings, exterior blinds, shutters, a tree on the
  west side. Reflective film on the worst windows is cheap and effective.
- **31 — attic insulation and ventilation**, which does more for a house's summer performance than most
  people expect, and which also pays in winter.
- **Blackout or thermal curtains** on the sun-facing windows, closed during the day, and the discipline
  to open the house at night when the outside air is genuinely cooler than the inside air — with a
  thermometer to decide, not a feeling.
- **Service the air conditioning before the season**, and if you do not have any, decide now what you will
  do — a portable or window unit for one room is far cheaper than central air and it makes a cooling room
  possible.
- **42 — the medical device power plan and the utility registry**, plus **60 — the refrigerated medication
  plan**, because an outage in a heat wave threatens insulin and other refrigerated drugs quickly.
- **1 — water store**, and the habit of drinking before being thirsty.
- **38 — a battery power station**, sized honestly. It will not run central air conditioning. It will run
  fans, a small window unit in some cases, and the medical equipment — which is the actual requirement.
- **Find your cooling centres now.** Libraries, community centres, malls, cinemas, and the
  officially-opened cooling centres your county announces. Know the hours, the transport and whether they
  take pets.
- **The check-in agreement**, and in this hazard it is the single most effective protection in the
  playbook — and it is what the federal guidance itself asks of you.
  «SRC: NWS, "Heat Safety Tips and Resources", citing *The Impacts of Climate Change on Human Health in the United States: A Scientific Assessment* — infants, young children and older adults are among the groups particularly vulnerable, and the guidance is explicitly to **check in on friends and relatives in those groups, especially if they do not have air conditioning**. https://www.weather.gov/safety/heat · retrieved 2026-07-29»
  Every elderly or isolated person in your circle gets a named person who contacts them **daily**
  during a heat event — and a physical visit if there is no answer, because heat stroke leaves people
  unable to answer a phone. The Northwest death toll was overwhelmingly people nobody checked on.

## During

**Watch the heat index and the overnight low, not the daytime high.** The overnight low is the
underappreciated number: a night that does not drop below about eighty degrees does not let a body or a
building recover, and consecutive hot nights are what turns a heat wave into a mass-casualty event.
Warnings are issued against combined heat-and-humidity thresholds that vary by region, because what is
dangerous in Seattle is normal in Phoenix.

**Cool the person before the building.** If the house cannot be cooled, go somewhere that can be — a
cooling centre, a mall, a library, a relative's. Hours in genuine air conditioning provide real
protection that persists after you leave. This is the most effective thing an unaircondtioned household
can do and it costs nothing.

**Manage the house on a schedule.** Windows and curtains shut through the day on the sun side. Everything
that generates heat off: the oven, the tumble dryer, the incandescent lights, the games console. Cook
outdoors or eat cold. Then, if and only if the outside air is genuinely cooler, open up at night and move
air through.

**Fans do not cool air, they move it, and above about body temperature they can make things worse** —
blowing air hotter than skin over a person who is no longer sweating effectively adds heat. Use them with
water: a damp cloth on the neck, a cool shower, a wet shirt, a basin for the feet. Evaporative cooling
does the work; the fan only speeds it.

**Drink steadily** rather than in large amounts occasionally, and remember that people on fluid
restrictions have been told that for a reason and need their clinician's advice rather than this book's.
Alcohol and large amounts of caffeine work against you.

**Shift anything physical to the early morning**, or do not do it. This is the hazard where "I'll just
finish the garden" produces the ambulance call. Outdoor workers need water, rest and shade on a schedule
rather than on demand, and several states set enforceable requirements for that.

**Never leave anybody in a parked car**, not for a minute, not with the windows cracked. A car's interior
rises above lethal temperatures within minutes even on a moderately warm day, and children die in
American cars every summer. Same for animals. Make it a household habit to check the back seat every
single time.

**Watch the children and the elderly rather than asking them.** Children do not self-regulate and will
play through it. Older adults frequently do not feel thirsty and do not perceive how hot the room has
become, and a house at ninety-five degrees can feel normal to someone acclimatised to it over three days.

**And if the power fails during a heat wave**, that is an escalation rather than an inconvenience: go
somewhere cooled, take the medication and the medically-dependent person with you, and do not wait to see
how long the outage lasts.

## After

**Heat waves do not have a dramatic end and their harm is counted afterwards.** Excess deaths in a heat
event are frequently identified weeks later, in the cardiac and respiratory statistics, which is why the
hazard has no cultural weight.

**Check on people again after it breaks.** People who got through the event dehydrated, who missed
medication, who did not eat, or who suffered a small cardiac event they attributed to the heat are at risk
in the following days.

**Look at what failed.** In this hazard the after-action is more valuable than in most, because heat waves
recur predictably and the fixes are permanent: the room that could not be cooled, the window with no
shade, the air conditioner that could not keep up, the attic with no insulation, the person who did not get
checked on. Every one of those is a summer project with a known return.

**The medication and the fridge.** If the power went, follow Protection 60 for anything refrigerated —
insulin and similar drugs have specific tolerances and specific published guidance, and the answer is
your pharmacist's rather than a guess.

**The financial part**, briefly, because it is real: heat drives the largest electricity bills of the
year, and the households most at risk are the ones who ration cooling because of the bill. Every state
has a low-income energy assistance programme and many utilities are prohibited from disconnecting
customers during declared heat events or offer medical-necessity protections. That information is worth
having before the bill arrives, and it belongs in the state directory at the back of this book.
