# Part Three, Playbook 17 — Grid Failure and the Long Outage

Target: 8 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

Every hazard in this book converges here.

Tornado, hurricane, ice storm, derecho, heat wave, wildfire, earthquake, deep freeze, geomagnetic storm —
whatever the cause, the shared consequence is that the electricity stops for longer than the household is
set up for. And in most of the events in this book, **the outage hurts more people than the event did.**

The 2003 Northeast blackout is the clean example because the causes were mundane. The joint US–Canada
task force found **four groups of causes, none of them exotic**: a utility that had not assessed the
voltage instability of its own Cleveland–Akron system, inadequate situational awareness in its control
room, inadequate management of the trees under its transmission lines, and a reliability coordinator
without adequate tools to see what was happening. The result took out **an estimated 50 million people
and 61,800 megawatts of load across Ohio, Michigan, Pennsylvania, New York, Vermont, Massachusetts,
Connecticut, New Jersey and Ontario** — in minutes.
«SRC: US–Canada Power System Outage Task Force, "Final Report on the August 14, 2003 Blackout in the
United States and Canada: Causes and Recommendations," April 2004. |
https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/oeprod/DocumentsandMedia/BlackoutFinal-Web.pdf |
retrieved 2026-07-29»

Nobody's tree-trimming budget was the villain in a movie. That is the point: the largest blackout in
North American history came out of ordinary maintenance decisions and ordinary gaps in what a control
room could see, which is why the household-level answer is not to hope for a better grid.

That is what makes this the highest-value playbook in the book despite being about nothing in particular.
**The failure mode is generic, the protections are generic, and they pay out no matter which hazard arrives.**

The other reason it matters: outages are getting longer, and the EIA's own numbers separate out exactly
why. The metric is SAIDI — the average minutes a customer spends without power in a year. **Counting all
events, the US figure went from 236 minutes in 2014 to 663 minutes in 2024** — from about four hours to
about eleven. **Strip out "major event days" and the same decade runs 114 minutes to 132.** Ordinary
Tuesday reliability barely moved. Essentially the whole increase is storms: in 2024, major event days
accounted for roughly 530 of those 663 minutes.
«SRC: US Energy Information Administration, Electric Power Annual, Table 11.1, "Reliability Metrics of
U.S. Distribution System" (IEEE method, 2014–2024). |
https://www.eia.gov/electricity/annual/html/epa_11_01.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»

That is the single most useful fact in this playbook for deciding how much to spend. You are not
preparing for a degraded grid. You are preparing for the handful of days a year when the weather takes
it away, and those days are getting longer while everything else stays the same.

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Carbon monoxide is the first killer, and it is not close.** Generators run indoors, in garages, in
crawl spaces, under windows or on porches. Charcoal grills brought inside. Camp stoves. Gas ovens as heaters.
Cars idling in attached garages. Every winter and every hurricane season produces the same deaths in the
same way.

**Temperature is the second.** Cold indoors in a winter outage, heat indoors in a summer one. Both
concentrate among elderly people living alone, and both are covered at length in Playbooks 9, 10 and 11.

**Medical dependency is the third**, and it is the quietest. Oxygen concentrators, dialysis, CPAP,
refrigerated insulin, powered mobility, feeding pumps, nebulisers. A household that depends on any of these
is on a clock from the moment the power goes, and the clock is frequently short.

**Then the rest:** falls in the dark, candle fires, food-borne illness from a freezer that was reopened
hopefully, electrocution during restoration, and traffic collisions at dark intersections. Backfeeding a
generator into a wall socket electrocutes utility workers and is a real and recurring event.

**Costs:** spoiled food, hotel nights, lost income, spoiled medication, burst pipes if the heat went out in
winter, and dead electronics on restoration. Most homeowner policies cover food spoilage from a covered
power failure up to a modest sub-limit, and many do not cover it at all when the outage originated
off-premises — which is worth knowing before you throw a freezer's contents away without photographing them.

## Before

This is the outage stack, and it is the same list regardless of what caused the outage.

- **37 — the power priority list**, written before you need it. Which loads matter, in what order, for your
  household: medical equipment, refrigeration, heating or cooling, water pump, communications, light. This
  is the document that decides what size battery or generator you actually need, rather than what the
  salesman suggests.
- **42 — the medical device power plan**, and the utility's medical registry. Do this first if it applies to
  you, because it changes everything else. Talk to the equipment supplier about battery options and to the
  clinician about what happens if the machine stops.
- **38 — a battery power station**, sized against the priority list. Quiet, indoor-safe, no fuel, no CO. For
  most households a battery plus a plan covers the first day or two of an outage better than a generator
  does, and it is what you will actually use.
- **39 — a generator with a transfer switch**, if the outage duration in your area justifies it. The
  transfer switch is not optional: it is what prevents backfeed, and it is what lets you run the furnace and
  the well pump at all.
- **32 — generator siting.** Outdoors, well away from the building, never in a garage even with the door
  open, never under a window, never on a porch. **47 — CO alarms** on every level, in date.
- **40 — fuel storage and rotation**, stabilized, stored safely, in an amount matched to a realistic run
  time rather than to an afternoon.
- **41 — small-scale solar**, where it genuinely pays.
- **43 — lighting layers**: headlamps for tasks, lanterns for rooms, flashlights at bedsides, and no candles.
  A headlamp is a better outage purchase than almost anything at the same price.
- **44 — the car as a power source**, and its limits.
- **46 — surge protection**, because restoration damages electronics.
- **15 — the fridge and freezer protocol**: a thermometer in each, ice blocks or frozen bottles filling the
  freezer's air space, and the four-hour rule known in advance.
- **1, 13 — water and no-cook food**, sized to two weeks if you can, and the sizing done in Part Two rather
  than guessed.
- **98 — cash in small bills**, because terminals and ATMs die with the grid.
- **117, 119 — battery packs and a radio that is not a phone.**
- **53–55 — sanitation**, because a long outage means no water pressure and no sewer pumping in many places,
  and this is the protection people are least willing to think about and most grateful for.

**Then the two conversations.** Anyone in your circle who is elderly, alone or medically dependent needs a
named person checking on them physically. And your household needs to know, in advance, what threshold sends
you elsewhere — a temperature, a duration, a medical device's battery running out — because deciding that
during hour thirty is how people get hurt.

## During

**The first ten minutes.** Confirm the outage is not just you: check the breaker panel and look at the
street. Report it to the utility, because in many systems the outage map is built from customer reports.
Then note the time — the fridge and freezer decisions all depend on it.

**Then hold the cold.** Fridge and freezer stay shut. Every opening costs hours. A full freezer holds
roughly twice as long as a half-empty one, and a blanket over a freezer helps. Move fridge items into the
freezer if there is room. Eat from the fridge first, then the freezer, then the shelf pantry — in that
order, which is Protection 15's whole logic.

**Then power the priority list**, not everything. Medical equipment first, then refrigeration in cycles
rather than continuously, then heating or cooling, then light and communications. Charge the phones and packs
early, while you have options.

**Generator rules, restated because they are the deaths:** outdoors only, twenty feet from the building,
downwind of doors and windows, on a level surface, never refueled while hot or running.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Winter Weather" — Generator Safety: generators and fuel are used outdoors and **at least 20 feet from windows, doors and attached garages**; install working CO detectors on **every level**; keep the generator dry; connect appliances with heavy-duty cords; **let it cool before refuelling**. Same page: only use generators and grills outdoors, and **never heat a home with a gas stovetop or oven**. https://www.ready.gov/winter-weather | retrieved 2026-07-29» Extension leads
sized for the load and rated for outdoor use. Never plugged into a wall socket. If your CO alarm sounds,
everybody goes outside immediately.

**Then manage the building's temperature** using the relevant playbook: consolidate into a warm room in
winter, into a cooling room in summer, and understand that the ambitious option — heating or cooling a whole
house on backup power — is usually not available and not necessary.

**Water.** If you are on a well, no power means no water at all, which is why Protection 3's pre-fill
protocol exists. If you are on mains, pressure can be maintained for a while and then fail, and pressure
loss frequently triggers a boil notice — so fill containers early in the outage rather than late.

**Sanitation.** If the water is off or the sewer is not pumping, do not keep flushing. Go to the twin-bucket
system, keep hand hygiene up with alcohol gel and the no-water routine in Protection 55, and be
disciplined about it, because a gastrointestinal outbreak in a household with no water is a genuinely
serious event.

**Information, not rumor.** The battery radio, the utility's outage map while you have data, and the
county's channels. Restoration estimates are frequently wrong early and better later.

**Then the check-ins.** Make them. This is the single most useful thing an unaffected neighbor does in an
outage, and it is how the elderly people in the mortality statistics would not have been in them.

**Do not touch anything utility-side.** Downed lines are live, and a line that is not sparking is not safe.
Stay away, keep children and animals away, and report it.

## After

**Restoration is when electronics die.** Leave sensitive equipment unplugged for several minutes after power
returns, then bring loads back in stages. Expect voltage swings and brief re-cycling.

**Reset and check:** the HVAC, the water heater's pilot if it has one, the well pump, the sump pump, the
freezer's temperature, the GFCI outlets, the alarm system, and every clock and timer that now says
midnight — including the medical equipment's settings.

**The food decision is made with a thermometer, not with your nose.** If the fridge was above the safe
threshold for more than a few hours, it goes. If the freezer still has ice crystals and reads at or below
freezing, it is generally safe to refreeze. Photograph what you discard and keep a list with approximate
values before it goes in the trash, because a food-spoilage claim needs evidence and the sub-limit is
frequently worth claiming.

**Water.** Follow any boil notice until it is formally lifted, and if you are on a well that lost power and
pressure, treat the supply as suspect (Protections 8 and 9).

**Then re-stock immediately.** Batteries, water, fuel, food, and whatever you discovered you did not have.
Outages cluster — the same weather pattern comes back, and the same weakened infrastructure fails again.

**And do the honest after-action**, because this is the playbook where it pays the most. How long did it
last? What ran out first? What did you improvise, and what should be bought instead? Who could not manage,
and what do they need next time? Did the generator start on the first pull? Was every CO alarm in date?
Write it down on the annual review sheet, because the next outage is the same outage.
