## Air and sanitation

These two subjects share a chapter because they share a timeline. Both are invisible on day one, both
are the dominant problem by day three, and both are cheap to solve in advance and impossible to solve
in the moment.

Air kills faster. Sanitation kills more slowly and affects everyone.

### 47 · Carbon monoxide alarms, placement and expiry

**Prevents:** the poisoning that kills whole households after the storm has passed.
**Cost:** $20–45 each · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Materials:** CO alarms
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 9 Ice & Snow North, and non-negotiable anywhere a generator exists.

One alarm on every level of the house and one outside each sleeping area. Follow the manufacturer's
placement instructions rather than intuition — they are specific, and guesses about height and
distance from appliances are usually wrong.

**The thing nobody knows: CO alarms expire.** The sensing element degrades and the unit has a service
life printed on the back, typically counted from manufacture rather than installation. An alarm past
its date may sit silently through a poisoning. CDC's instruction is *"replace your CO detector following
the manufacturer's instructions or every 5 years"* — a much shorter interval than the ten years people
carry over from smoke alarms, and the single most common maintenance failure in this protection.

**And the test button does not test what you think it tests.** The federal product-safety regulator is
explicit: pressing it *"tests whether the circuitry is operating correctly, not the accuracy of the
sensor."* A unit can chirp obediently every month for years with a dead sensor. That is exactly why the
date matters more than the monthly test. Newer alarms carry an **end-of-life signal** that sounds when
the unit needs replacing — learn what yours sounds like, because most people hear it, assume a low
battery, change the battery, and put a dead alarm back on the ceiling.

Check the date on every alarm in your house tonight, and write the replacement year on the front of the
unit in marker where you can see it from the floor.
«SRC: US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, "Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics" — install
battery-operated or battery back-up CO detectors near every sleeping area; "replace your CO detector
following the manufacturer's instructions or every 5 years." |
https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»
«SRC: US Consumer Product Safety Commission, "Carbon Monoxide Fact Sheet" and "CO Alarms" — using the
test button "tests whether the circuitry is operating correctly, not the accuracy of the sensor";
alarms have a recommended replacement age; newer alarms have an end-of-life signal; CO alarms on each

Combination smoke-and-CO units are acceptable and reduce the number of things to maintain. Ten-year
sealed-battery units remove the dead-battery failure mode entirely and are worth the premium.

«SRC: CDC, "Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics" — the most common symptoms are **headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion**, often described as "flu-like". Breathing a lot of CO can cause loss of consciousness or death, and **people who are asleep or intoxicated can die before they ever have symptoms**. https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Know the response: alarm sounds, everyone goes outside immediately, then call 911 or the gas utility
from outside. Do not open windows and wait — you will disperse the evidence and stay in the building.

If anyone sleeps without hearing aids, use a CO alarm with a bed-shaker or strobe (see 48).

propane, or smoke unless it is a combination unit — three different sensors, three different jobs.

### 48 · Smoke alarms

**Prevents:** the disaster that follows the disaster.
**Cost:** $15–40 each · **Time:** an hour · **Materials:** sealed 10-year alarms
· **Regions:** all five.

Post-disaster house fires are a recurring pattern — candles, space heaters, damaged wiring, generators
too close to the structure, restored power energizing a damaged circuit. The alarms that would catch
that are the ones already in your ceiling, and they expire too.

One in every sleeping room, one outside each sleeping area, one on every level. Interconnected units,
so that one sounding sounds all of them, which matters enormously in a house where people are sleeping
in a consolidated room far from the origin. Sealed ten-year lithium units, which cannot be disabled by
removing a battery.

**Replace the unit itself ten years from the manufacture date**, not ten years from when you put it up
— the fire administration's phrasing is *"they need to be replaced 10 years from the manufacture
date,"* and the date is printed on the back. A 9-volt unit gets a monthly test and a fresh battery at
least once a year; a sealed ten-year lithium unit gets the monthly test and then the whole unit is
replaced.

**One more choice that most households get wrong by default.** There are two sensing technologies,
ionization and photoelectric, and they detect *different kinds of fire* — fast flaming fires versus
smoldering ones. Because nobody can predict which kind will start, the federal recommendation is that
every sleeping area have **both types, or dual-sensor alarms that contain both.** If you are buying
alarms anyway, that is the same money spent better. Alarms with strobes and bed-shakers exist for people
with hearing loss and belong in the plan for anyone who would not wake to a sounder.
«SRC: US Fire Administration, "Smoke Alarms" — install in every bedroom, outside each separate
sleeping area and on every level including the basement; on the ceiling or high on the wall;
interconnected so when one sounds they all sound; replaced 10 years from the manufacture date; test
monthly, replace 9-volt batteries at least annually; USFA recommends both ionization and photoelectric,
or dual-sensor alarms, in every place people sleep; strobe/vibrating alarms for people with hearing
loss. | https://www.usfa.fema.gov/prevention/home-fires/prepare-for-fire/smoke-alarms/ | retrieved
2026-07-29»

**What this does not do:** a smoke alarm buys escape time. It does not buy firefighting time, and it
does not detect CO.

### 49 · Wildfire smoke: filtration and HVAC recirculation

**Prevents:** breathing the fire from a thousand miles away.
**Cost:** $20–60 per filter · **Time:** 20 minutes · **Materials:** a high-efficiency furnace filter
· **Regions:** Wildfire West rank 4, and now genuinely all five.

Smoke travels across the continent, and the reason it matters is particle size. EPA's account of PM2.5
is that these particles *"can get deep into your lungs, and some may even get into your bloodstream,"*
and that exposure has been linked to **premature death in people with heart or lung disease, nonfatal
heart attacks, irregular heartbeat, aggravated asthma, decreased lung function, and increased
respiratory symptoms** — coughing, airway irritation, difficulty breathing.
«SRC: US EPA, "Health and Environmental Effects of Particulate Matter (PM)." |
https://www.epa.gov/pm-pollution/health-and-environmental-effects-particulate-matter-pm |
retrieved 2026-07-29»

Read that list again and notice what is on it. Two of the six outcomes are cardiac. Smoke is filed
mentally as a lung problem, and for the people most likely to die of it, it is a heart problem — which
is a large fraction of any neighborhood.

Two actions. Fit the highest-efficiency filter your system is rated to accept — commonly MERV 13, but
**check your equipment's tolerance**, because a filter too restrictive for your blower reduces airflow
and can damage the system. And set the system to **recirculate** rather than drawing outside air.

Then learn to read the air quality index for your area and set a household threshold at which windows
close and the filtration goes on. Write it into the trigger plan.

**What this does not do:** a furnace filter only cleans air that passes through the furnace, which
means it does nothing in a house with no ducted system, and nothing while the power is out. That is
what Protection 50 is for.

### 50 · The box-fan filter build

**Prevents:** needing to buy an air purifier you cannot afford or find.
**Cost:** $30–90 · **Time:** 15 minutes · **Materials:** box fan, four filters, tape
· **Regions:** Wildfire West rank 5, and all five.

A box fan with high-efficiency filters taped to it makes a genuinely effective room air cleaner for a
fraction of the price of a commercial unit, and this is not folk wisdom — EPA tested it in a chamber
against the commercial machines. **The finding: DIY air cleaners were as effective as a small
commercial air cleaner at reducing PM2.5.** Adding a **cardboard shroud** to block air recirculating
past the fan blade tips improved performance at no cost. Multi-filter designs moved more air and were
more cost-effective than single-filter ones. Independent chamber testing of a single-filter MERV-12
build measured a **clean air delivery rate of about 150 cfm for smoke-sized particles** on high speed.
«SRC: US EPA, "Research on DIY Air Cleaners to Reduce Wildfire Smoke Indoors" and EPA Science Matters,
"Do-It-Yourself Air Cleaners: Making Cleaner Air More Accessible." |
https://www.epa.gov/air-research/research-diy-air-cleaners-reduce-wildfire-smoke-indoors |
retrieved 2026-07-29»
«SRC: Illinois Institute of Technology, "Portable Air Cleaner Test Report — Box Fan Filter," March
2021 — CADR 150.2 cfm (0.09–1 µm), 203.1 cfm (0.5–3 µm), 285.6 cfm (5–11 µm) at fan speed 3 with a
MERV-12 filter. | https://www.built-envi.com/wp-content/uploads/IIT-CADR-Testing-March-2021-Box-Fan-Filters.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**Two findings from that research change how you use it, and both are non-obvious.**

First, on the fire question, which is the objection everybody raises: Underwriters Laboratories' Chemical
Insights Research Institute, working with EPA, tested five commercial box fan models in the standard
one-filter configuration — with clean filters, with filters deliberately loaded with smoke and dust,
and with one or both sides of the fan obstructed. **None of the tested conditions presented a burn or
fire risk.** The recommended practice is to use a **box fan from 2012 or newer**, which will have a
fused plug and a thermal cutoff.

Second, and this is the one people get wrong: **a DIY cleaner with dirty, loaded filters was almost
completely ineffective.** Not degraded — ineffective. A unit running all week on a gray filter is
theater. Change the filters.

Build it correctly: filters oriented with airflow arrows pointing **into** the fan, joints sealed with
tape so air cannot bypass the filters, and the cardboard shroud on the outlet side.

Figure 9 shows the build exploded out — orientation and the shroud are the two parts people get backwards.

«FIG: box-fan-filter»

Run it in the room people are in — usually the same consolidated room from Protection 26 — and keep
that room's door closed.

**What this does not do:** it removes particulate. It does not remove gases, smoke odor, or carbon
monoxide, and it is not a substitute for leaving an area under evacuation order.

### 51 · Sealing a room against smoke, ash, or a release

**Prevents:** the outside air becoming the inside air.
**Cost:** $10–30 · **Time:** 20 minutes when needed · **Materials:** tape, plastic sheeting, towels
· **Regions:** Wildfire West, hazmat-exposed households, volcanic regions.

One room, chosen in advance: interior, few windows, and ideally with a bathroom attached. Close and
lock windows, because locking seals better than closing. Towels along door bottoms. Tape plastic
sheeting over vents and window frames where the seal is poor. Turn off anything that draws outside air
— bathroom and kitchen extract fans, the fresh-air intake, the clothes dryer.

Take the box-fan filter and a radio in with you.

This is the same room and the same technique used for a chemical release, which is why the hazmat
chapter cross-references here rather than repeating it.

**What this does not do:** sealing is measured in hours, not days — a sealed room accumulates carbon
dioxide with people in it. It also does nothing against carbon monoxide, which is generated inside.
For a prolonged event the answer is leaving, not sealing harder.

### 52 · N95 supply and fit

**Prevents:** the respiratory injury nobody counts.
**Cost:** $15–40 · **Time:** 10 minutes · **Materials:** N95 or P100 respirators
· **Regions:** Wildfire West rank 6, volcanic regions, all five for debris cleanup.

Buy N95 or better, per person, and store them where they will be found. They matter for three
distinct situations: wildfire smoke, volcanic ash, and post-event debris clearing, which produces
silica, fibreglass, mold spores, and pulverized building material.

**Fit is the whole protection**, and NIOSH explains why in a sentence worth memorizing: a respirator
**"can only filter particles that try to pass through the filter material."** A tight-fitting respirator
seals to the face and *forces* the air through the filter. A loose-fitting mask does not, and **"if gaps
exist between your face and the respirator or mask, some small particles that you cannot see can enter
your nose or mouth through those gaps."** NIOSH names the usual leak points: **the bridge of the nose,
the sides, and facial hair.** Because faces differ, you may need to try a few to find one that seals —
which is an argument for buying them before you need them.

**On cloth and surgical masks, NIOSH's objection is precise and it is not "they don't work."** It is
that **"some masks, like many disposable face masks and cloth masks, are not made to a standard. They
provide unknown levels of protection."** A NIOSH Approved N95 meets the most rigorous standard and states
its filtration level. An unstandardized mask states nothing, and for smoke, ash and silica that is the
same as no information. Buy the thing with a number on it.
«SRC: CDC/NIOSH, "Community Respirators and Masks" — filtration and fit; NIOSH Approved respirators meet
the most rigorous standards and report their filtration level; disposable and cloth masks not made to a
standard "provide unknown levels of protection"; leak points at the nose bridge, sides and facial hair. |
https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/ppe/php/community-respirators-masks/index.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Children's faces do not seal on adult respirators. Buy sized ones or accept that their protection is
staying indoors with the filtration running.

**What this does not do:** a particulate respirator does nothing for gases or vapors — not carbon
monoxide, not chemical releases. Those require a cartridge respirator and training, or evacuation.

### 53 · The twin-bucket toilet

**Prevents:** the health emergency inside your own house on day three.
**Cost:** $25–60 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **Materials:** two 5-gallon buckets, snap-on seat, bags,
absorbent · **Regions:** all five.

When water or sewer fails, the toilet stops. It does not stop being needed.

The system is two buckets kept separate, which is the entire trick: **one for liquid, one for solid.**
Separating them is what controls odor and volume, and it is why this works where a single bucket
becomes unusable within a day. Line the solid bucket with a heavy bag, and after each use cover with
an absorbent — sawdust, wood pellets, cat litter, peat, or shredded paper. Snap-on toilet seats for
buckets are inexpensive and make the difference between a system people will use and one they will
avoid.

Keep it in the bathroom, for privacy and because that is where people already go.

**Never flush a toilet if the sewer is backing up or you have been told the system is compromised** —
you are pumping into your own house.

**What this does not do:** it does not compost, it does not treat, and it is not a septic solution. It
contains waste until it can be disposed of properly, which is Protection 54.

### 54 · Waste containment and lawful disposal

**Prevents:** a solved sanitation problem becoming a contamination problem.
**Cost:** $10–30 · **Time:** ongoing · **Materials:** heavy bags, a lidded outdoor trash can
· **Regions:** all five.

Double-bag the solid waste, seal it, and store it in a lidded container outside, away from the house
and away from any standing water or drinking water source. It stays there until municipal service
resumes and you are told how to dispose of it.

Do not bury it near a well or a garden, do not put it in a storm drain, and do not burn it. If the
outage is long, your county will publish disposal instructions — that instruction is the one to
follow, and it is another reason the state directory in Appendix B exists.

Liquid waste can be poured into a functioning sewer or a soakaway well away from any water source, if
your local rules allow it. Check before you assume.

**What this does not do:** containment is temporary. Beyond a week or two of accumulation this becomes
a genuine problem, and that is when relocation gets weighed against staying.

### 55 · Hand hygiene with no running water

**Prevents:** the illness that spreads through the household while nobody can reach a doctor.
**Cost:** $10–25 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** alcohol-based sanitiser, wipes, soap
· **Regions:** all five.

Gastrointestinal illness spreading through a household in a period with no water, no laundry, and no
medical access is one of the most likely bad outcomes of any long outage, and hand hygiene is the
control.

Alcohol-based sanitiser at a sufficient concentration is the low-water answer and belongs at every
point of use — the bucket toilet, the food area, the door. Soap and a small poured stream of treated
water is better when hands are visibly soiled, because sanitiser does not work well on dirty hands.
A faucet-fitted water container makes a two-liter bottle last for many washes.

Note the priority conflict honestly: this is one of the few non-drinking uses that justifies treated
water, because the alternative is illness in the worst possible circumstances.

**What this does not do:** sanitiser is not effective against every organism, notably some of the ones
that cause the diarrhoeal illness you are most worried about. Soap and water wins whenever it is
available.

### 56 · Ashfall specifics

**Prevents:** treating volcanic ash like dust.
**Cost:** $0–40 · **Time:** as needed · **Materials:** respirators, vehicle filters
· **Regions:** Pacific Northwest, Alaska, Hawaii.

Ash is pulverized rock — abrasive, heavy, and unlike ordinary dust in three ways that matter.

**It loads roofs.** Wet ash is dramatically heavier than dry, and accumulation is a structural load
problem. Clear it before it gets wet, from the ground where possible.

**And read FEMA's instruction on this before you climb anything**, because it is more cautious than most
people's instinct: *"Do not get on your roof to remove ash unless you have guidance or training,"* ash
makes surfaces slippery, and *"be careful not to contribute additional weight to an overloaded roof."*
Your own weight is part of the load. Protective clothing and a mask for the clean-up, and children do not
help with it.

This book does not print a depth at which a roof fails, because that depends on the roof, on the ash, and
on whether it has rained — and a number here would be used as permission to wait.
«SRC: Ready.gov / FEMA, "Volcanoes" — 161 potentially active volcanoes in the United States; volcanic
ash can travel hundreds of miles; "do not get on your roof to remove ash unless you have guidance or
training"; "be careful not to contribute additional weight to an overloaded roof"; wear protective
clothing and a mask when cleaning up; children should not help with clean-up. |
https://www.ready.gov/volcanoes | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**It destroys engines and HVAC.** It clogs air filters fast and scores cylinder walls. Change vehicle
and furnace filters early and keep spares. Avoid driving in it at all, both for the engine and because
it destroys visibility and makes roads slick.

**It injures lungs and eyes.** N95 or better, and sealed eye protection, and long sleeves, because it
is abrasive on skin. Contact lenses out.

Seal the house per Protection 51, and do not run the HVAC while ash is falling.

**What this does not do:** an N95 handles the particulate, not the volcanic gases that may accompany
an eruption. And clearing a roof is dangerous work in slick conditions — that is a hire-it job for
most households.
