## Power

Almost everyone who buys a generator buys the wrong size, and almost everyone who does not buy one
overestimates what they will lose without it. Both errors come from the same omission: nobody writes
down what actually needs electricity.

So that is where this chapter starts, and it is free.

---

### 37 · The power priority list

**Prevents:** buying the wrong equipment, and spending your last battery on the wrong thing.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Materials:** a sheet of paper
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 1 of this chapter everywhere.

Write down every electrical thing in your house and sort it into four tiers.

**Tier 1 — life-sustaining.** Oxygen concentrator, CPAP where prescribed as essential, powered
wheelchair, infusion pump, refrigeration for medication. If anything is in this tier, it changes every
downstream decision in this chapter and it also means Protection 42 is mandatory.

**Tier 2 — health and safety.** Refrigerator and freezer, sump pump, well pump, one heat source,
lighting, a way to charge a phone.

**Tier 3 — comfort and function.** Internet, television, most cooking appliances, laundry.

**Tier 4 — everything else.**

Write the running wattage next to each Tier 1 and Tier 2 item, off the label rather than off a guess.
Total the tiers separately. That total is the only number that tells you what to buy, and it is
usually far smaller than what people end up purchasing.

**What this does not do:** the list does not account for **starting** loads, which are much higher
than running loads on anything with a motor — well pumps, compressors, refrigerators. That is
Protection 39, and ignoring it is the single most common generator mistake.

### 38 · Battery bank and power station sizing

**Prevents:** overbuying, and buying something that cannot do the one job you needed.
**Cost:** $80–1,500 · **Time:** an hour of arithmetic · **Materials:** a battery power station
· **Regions:** all five.

A lithium power station is the right answer for most households whose Tier 1 is empty and whose Tier 2
does not include a well pump. It is silent, it works indoors, it produces no exhaust, and it charges
from a wall outlet, a car, or a panel.

Size it against watt-hours, not against a marketing headline. Take the device wattage, multiply by the
hours you need it, and add margin. A phone is trivial. A CPAP overnight is modest. A refrigerator for
a day is substantial. A well pump is out of range for most units.

Buy the capacity for your Tier 1 and Tier 2 list from Protection 37, plus a margin, and stop.

**What this does not do:** a power station will not run a house, a heat pump, an electric water heater,
or most well pumps. It also degrades if stored fully discharged, so charge it every few months whether
you used it or not.

### 39 · Generator sizing, transfer switch, and the rule that keeps linemen alive

**Prevents:** an undersized generator, a house fire, and electrocuting a utility worker.
**Cost:** $500–5,000 plus $500–1,500 for the switch · **Time:** days, with an electrician
· **Materials:** generator, interlock or transfer switch · **Regions:** all five.

Two things to get right, and the second is a safety absolute.

**Sizing.** Total your Tier 1 and Tier 2 running watts, then add headroom for the largest *starting*
load in the set, because motors draw several times their running current for the first seconds. A
generator sized to running watts alone will stall every time the well pump or the compressor kicks in.

**Connection.** A generator is connected to a house through a **transfer switch or an interlock kit,
installed by a licensed electrician** — or it is not connected to the house at all, and you run
appliances directly off it with proper cords.

**Never back-feed through a dryer outlet or any other receptacle.** It energizes the utility line
outside your house, it can kill the lineman restoring your power, and it bypasses every protective
device in your panel. This is illegal in most jurisdictions and it is the one item in this book where
the person your mistake kills may be a stranger.

Cords matter: rated for the load and the length, undamaged, uncoiled while running, and never run
under a rug or through a doorway that closes on them.

**What this does not do:** a generator does not solve carbon monoxide. See Protection 32, which is not
advice but a condition of ownership.

### 40 · Fuel storage, shelf life, and stabilizer

**Prevents:** a generator that will not start on day one.
**Cost:** $30–120 · **Time:** an hour · **Materials:** approved containers, stabilizer
· **Regions:** all five.

Gasoline degrades over months and degraded fuel is a leading cause of no-start. Store only what you can
rotate, in approved containers, outside the living space, away from ignition and out of the sun. Label
each container with its fill date. Add stabilizer if it will sit. Rotate through a car or a mower
rather than letting it age.

Propane is dramatically easier — stable for years, no stabilizer, no degradation — which is a real
argument for a dual-fuel generator even at a small price premium. Diesel sits in between and has its
own gelling problem in deep cold.

Run the generator under load for a short period a few times a year. A generator that has not run in
two years is a heavy object.

**What this does not do:** fuel quantity is limited by local code and sometimes by your insurance
policy. Check both. And no amount of stored fuel matters if the generator has not been started. If you
live alone and your generator has not started this year, it is not a plan.

### 41 · Small-scale solar that is worth the money

**Prevents:** buying a panel that charges nothing.
**Cost:** $80–600 · **Time:** an afternoon · **Materials:** portable panel matched to the station
· **Regions:** all five, best in sun-reliable regions.

The honest scope: a portable panel is for **recharging batteries and devices**, not for running a
house. Matched to the power station from Protection 38, it turns a finite battery into an indefinite
one for phones, lights, radios, and a CPAP — which is a genuinely large improvement in a long outage.

Match the panel to the station's input specification, and set expectations against real conditions
rather than the rating: cloud, angle, and winter sun all cut output substantially.

**What this does not do:** rooftop solar without battery storage **shuts down in a grid outage** by
design, for the safety of line workers. Many homeowners with panels do not know this and discover it
during the first blackout. If outage resilience is the goal, the battery is the purchase, not the
panels.

### 42 · Medical device power plan and the utility registry

**Prevents:** a life-sustaining device with a dead battery and no priority.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** an hour of phone calls · **Materials:** the phone
· **Regions:** all five. Mandatory if Tier 1 is not empty.

Three calls, and they are among the most valuable hours in this book for the households that need
them.

**Call the utility** and ask to be added to the medical or life-support registry. Most service
territories maintain one; it can mean advance notification of planned outages and priority
consideration in restoration. It is not a guarantee, and it is free.

**Call the equipment supplier** and ask three questions: what is the device's battery runtime, what is
the manufacturer's recommended backup, and can they supply a spare battery.

**Call the clinic** and ask what their outage protocol is and where their patients are directed.

Then write the answers on the form in Part Six, because the value is in the household knowing them,
not in you having asked.

**What this does not do:** a registry is not a promise of uninterrupted power. For a
genuinely power-dependent household, the plan must include relocation to a facility with power, and
the trigger for that decision belongs in Protection 95.

### 43 · Lighting layers, and the no-candles rule

**Prevents:** the fall, the cut, and the house fire.
**Cost:** $20–70 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **Materials:** a headlamp per person, batteries, lanterns
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 4 Quake West.

**A headlamp per person**, not one shared flashlight, because hands are needed for everything else and
because a household that shares one light moves as a clump. A lantern for the consolidated room, so
people can function rather than aim. And a light plus real shoes at each bedside, because the second
injury wave in Part One happens to people walking through wreckage in the dark in what they sleep in.

Standardize on one battery size across your devices so one spare pack covers everything.

**Candles are not emergency lighting.** Open flame in a house where people are exhausted, where
children are frightened, where pets are moving, and where furniture has been rearranged, after an
event that may have disturbed gas lines. The federal fire administration's own outage advice is one
line: *"use flashlights, not candles, if you have a power outage."*

The reason is what its casualty data shows about *how* candle fires start. Across three years of
national incident data, an estimated **23,600 residential candle fires a year**, causing about 165
deaths, 1,525 injuries and $390 million in property loss — and the mechanism is not carelessness in
the usual sense. **Twenty-one per cent involved someone unattended or unsupervised**, another 12 per
cent started when the user fell asleep, and the leading things ignited were furniture, curtains,
cabinetry and bedding. More than a third begin in a bedroom. Read that as a description of the second
night of an outage: tired people, a candle near soft furnishings, somebody asleep.

**What this does not do:** lighting is not signaling. If your plan involves being found, that is a
whistle and a radio, not a flashlight.

### 44 · The car as a power source, and its limits

**Prevents:** overestimating the outlet in your dashboard.
**Cost:** $25–150 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** a modest inverter
· **Regions:** all five.

A car will charge phones, run a small light, and top up a power station. With a modest inverter it
runs low-wattage devices. It will not run a refrigerator, a heater, or a well pump, and attempting it
either trips the inverter or damages something.

Two absolutes. **Never run the engine in an attached garage**, including with the door open — this is
the same mechanism as Protection 32 and it kills people who are sheltering from cold. And never leave
a running car unattended with occupants asleep inside it.

Some newer vehicles include real power-export capability, which is a genuinely useful feature. Check
your own manual rather than assuming either way.

**What this does not do:** running the engine to charge burns fuel you may need to evacuate with. In a
long outage, fuel is the escape route, not the generator.

### 45 · Refuel and recharge cadence

**Prevents:** running everything down at the same time.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** ongoing · **Materials:** a written schedule
· **Regions:** all five.

In a long outage, energy management becomes a daily routine and should be written rather than improvised.
Set a rule: the phones charge at a fixed time each day, the power station recharges whenever the sun or
the car allows, the generator runs in scheduled blocks rather than continuously — which stretches fuel,
reduces noise, and reduces theft exposure — and the vehicle never goes below half a tank.

Half a tank is the discipline that matters most. It is Protection 110, and in a long event fuel becomes
the scarcest thing in the region within a day.

**What this does not do:** scheduling does not create energy. If the Tier 1 list has anything in it, the
schedule bends around that item and everything else waits.

### 46 · Surge protection, whole-home and point-of-use

**Prevents:** losing every appliance in the house when the power comes back.
**Cost:** $250–600 installed, plus $20–60 per point · **Time:** an electrician visit
· **Materials:** service-entrance surge protective device, point-of-use protectors
· **Regions:** all five, especially lightning-prone regions.

Damage frequently happens at **restoration**, not at the outage — a surge as the grid comes back, or a
nearby strike coupling into the wiring. The two layers work together: a service-entrance device
installed at the panel handles the large events, and point-of-use protectors at the expensive
electronics handle what gets past it.

Point-of-use protectors have a finite absorption capacity and wear out silently. Replace them
periodically and after any known large event; a protector with a dead indicator is a power strip.

The free version of this protection is Protection 120: unplug the expensive things when a storm is
forecast, or when a geomagnetic storm watch is issued.

**What this does not do:** no surge device protects against a direct lightning strike to the structure,
and none protects a device that is plugged in through an unprotected path — a network cable, a coaxial
line, or a phone line all carry surges too.
