# Part Four, Chapter One — People

Children · older adults · disability and medical dependency · animals

Target: 18 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## Why this part exists

Everything up to here has been written for a generic household, and there is no such thing.

The mortality statistics in every hazard in this book point at the same handful of groups, over and over.
Older adults living alone. People whose bodies cannot regulate temperature. People who depend on a machine, a
refrigerated drug or a dialysis chair. Children, who cannot make the decisions and cannot leave on their own.
And the households who would not evacuate without an animal.

None of that is a reason for a separate book. It is a reason for a chapter that says: **take the plan you have
built and check it against the actual people in your house.**

---

## Children

**The two things that go wrong** are that children are not where you are when it happens, and that children
are not consulted about a plan they are expected to execute.

**School and childcare, resolved in advance.** Find out, in writing, what the school actually does: whether
it shelters in place or evacuates, where it takes children, who is permitted to collect them, and what
identification is required. Then put every adult who might collect your child on the authorized release form
— the grandparent, the neighbor, the childminder — because a school following its own policy will refuse to
release a child to someone not on the list, and it will be right to.

Ask specifically what happens if the event lands at the end of the day, or during a bus journey, because
those are the awkward cases that plans skip.

**Then tell the children the plan, in the words they use.** A child who knows where the safe room is, what
the siren means, who is coming for them and where the family meets is dramatically safer than one being
managed. Age-appropriate does not mean vague — a six-year-old can learn "if the alarm goes, we go to the
bathroom and put on the helmet," and can practice it as a game.

**What goes in a child's go-bag**, beyond the standard contents: a change of clothes in the current size,
which means checking twice a year; comfort items **chosen by them** rather than by you; something to do,
because boredom in a shelter is its own problem; a card in their pocket with their name, your name, two phone
numbers and any medical information; and for a younger child, a recent photograph of the family including the
pets, which is how a separated child gets reunited.

**For infants specifically**, the supply list is unforgiving: formula, and enough of it, in a form that does
not need clean water if possible; bottles that can be cleaned without a faucet; diapers and wipes in quantity;
a means of keeping a baby warm without heating a house. Protection 19 covers the reserve, and the honest note
is that an infant's supply cannot be improvised in the way an adult's can.

**Then the drill, made a game rather than a threat.** Twice a year, timed, everyone including the children,
with the helmets. Children who have practiced do not freeze.

**And afterwards, expect the reaction to be delayed and behavioural rather than verbal.** Regression, clinging,
sleep problems, bedwetting, new fears, irritability, an obsessive interest in the event, or apparent
indifference that changes months later. The interventions are unglamorous and they work: routine restored
quickly, honest and simple answers rather than reassurance that turns out to be false, limits on repeated
media coverage of the event, and letting them help with something concrete. Protection 66 covers this, and
the threshold for getting professional help is lower than most parents assume.

## Older adults

**This is the group the mortality data points at hardest**, in heat, in cold, in outages, and in evacuations.
The reasons are consistent and none of them are about frailty of character.

Temperature regulation declines with age, and the perception of temperature declines with it — an older
person in a house at fifty-five degrees or ninety-five degrees frequently does not feel it as dangerous.
Thirst perception declines, so dehydration arrives without a signal. Many common medications interfere with
temperature regulation and fluid balance. Mobility limits the ability to leave. And **living alone removes the
person who would have noticed.**

**So the protection that matters most is a person, not a purchase.** Every older adult living alone should
have a named individual who contacts them **daily** during any event — heat wave, cold snap, outage, storm
warning — and who goes there physically if there is no answer. Not a vague "we'll keep an eye out." A name, an
agreement and a phone number, written down on both sides.

**Then the practical adaptations to the plan you already have:**

- The safe room and the shelter route have to work for the actual mobility involved, which sometimes means the
  plan is different from the rest of the household's.
- Medication becomes the central logistical fact: the thirty-day buffer (Protection 59), an accurate written
  list, and a pill organizer that can be grabbed rather than reassembled.
- Hearing and vision: spare glasses, spare hearing aid batteries, and the recognition that an alert which is
  audible-only may not reach someone. A bed-shaker alarm or a visual alert is a real protection.
- A written medical summary in the document envelope, because an older person in an emergency is frequently
  unable to give a complete history and the receiving clinician needs one.
- Cognitive impairment changes everything. If there is dementia in the household, an unfamiliar environment is
  itself a hazard, disorientation and wandering are real risks in a shelter, and identification on the person
  — a bracelet, a card in a pocket — is essential. The evacuation destination should favor familiarity and
  quiet over convenience.

  **When they will not get in the car.** Plan for this before the day. Practice the evacuation with the
  family, including the drive. Pack a favorite item in the car, such as a photo book or a pillow. On the
  day, keep the words short and concrete, then reassure: "We're going to see your son." Approach from the
  front, use their name, and speak calmly and low. Do not argue or correct. Do not leave them alone while
  you load the car. Leave as early as you can. Tell the county registry the household has two people who
  need help, not one (Protection 111).

- Falls: the flashlight and shoes at the bedside (Protection 43) matter more here than anywhere, and clutter in the
  route to the safe room is a genuine hazard rather than untidiness.

**On evacuation, the honest and difficult point.** Moving frail older people has a measurable cost, and it
has been measured.

A study of **36,389 nursing-home residents exposed to four Gulf hurricanes** — Katrina, Rita, Gustav and
Ike — compared them with residents of the same homes in the two preceding non-hurricane years. Exposure to
the storms produced **277 extra deaths and 872 extra hospitalizations at thirty days**, and **579 extra
deaths at ninety days.** Then the part that matters for your decision: isolating the effect of *evacuation
itself*, the authors found it **raised the probability of death at ninety days from 2.7 per cent to 5.3 per
cent** — roughly doubling it — and hospitalization from 1.8 to 8.3 per cent, independent of other factors.
Their conclusion: *"among residents exposed to hurricanes, evacuation significantly exacerbated subsequent
morbidity/mortality."*

**Do not read that as "never move anybody."** It is a study of the frailest population in the country,
long-stay nursing-home residents, moved under blanket policies adopted after Katrina and under external
pressure to evacuate regardless of individual circumstances. The finding is not that evacuation is wrong;
it is that **for a very frail person, moving is itself a medical event with its own risk**, and it belongs
on the same side of the ledger as the storm.

Which means the calculation is real in both directions and it is sometimes still the right call.

**This study is about nursing-home residents moved in groups.** If you live in your own home in a surge
zone or a fire zone, it is not a reason to stay: leave early, on your trigger (Protection 95).

That decision belongs to the household and the clinician together, made in advance where possible, and the
worst version is making it at midnight with no plan. If the answer is "we stay," then the staying has to be
properly resourced: power for the equipment, temperature control, water, and somebody present.

**And if the older adult is a caregiver** — a grandparent raising children, or a spouse caring for a partner
— plan for their incapacity. Who takes over, and do they know it, and can they get in?

## Disability and medical dependency

**The general rule for this section is that the reader knows their own needs better than this book does.** So
what follows is a checklist to test a plan against, not instruction.

**Power dependency** is the sharpest cliff in this book. If anything in your household needs electricity to
keep somebody alive or well — oxygen concentrator, ventilator, CPAP or BiPAP, suction, feeding pump,
nebuliser, powered wheelchair, refrigerated medication — then the outage is not an inconvenience, it is a
clock starting.

The protections, in order: **register with the utility** as a medical-necessity customer, which affects
restoration priority and notification in many jurisdictions but is **not** a guarantee and should never be
treated as one. Then talk to the equipment supplier about **battery options and run times**, and get the
actual figures rather than an assurance. Then size backup power against those figures (Protections 38 and 39),
and know the vehicle option (Protection 44) as a bridge. Then identify the **named facility** you would go to
— a hospital, a clinic, a relative with power — and the transport that would take you, and confirm it is real.

**Dialysis** needs its own arrangement made with the clinic now: the backup unit, the emergency diet
guidance for a missed session, and the transport. Clinics have emergency plans; ask for yours in writing
(Protection 61).

**Mobility and evacuation.** If a household member cannot walk out, cannot use stairs, or needs equipment
that does not travel easily, the evacuation plan needs to be specific: who lifts, what equipment comes, which
vehicle, and which route. Enroll in the county's voluntary assistance registry (Protection 111) — and then
arrange a named human being as well, because a registry is a list of intentions and a neighbor is a person
who arrives. Know that shelters are legally required to be accessible, and know that the reality varies,
which is why a named alternative destination matters.

**Service animals** go where you go, by law, including into shelters that exclude pets — and it is worth
having documentation to hand not because it is required but because it ends arguments quickly.

**Communication.** If a household member is deaf or hard of hearing, alerting cannot be audible-only:
wireless emergency alerts on the phone, a bed-shaker, and visual alarms. If someone is blind or has low
vision, the go-bag and the safe room need a tactile logic and a spare cane, and the household's plan needs to
account for a changed and debris-strewn environment. If someone is non-speaking or has a cognitive or
communication disability, a written card describing needs and how to communicate is the single most useful
item they can carry.

**Autism and sensory sensitivity** deserve a specific note, because shelters are the worst possible sensory
environment: crowded, loud, bright, unpredictable. Plan around a shelter if you can — a relative, a quiet
hotel — and if you cannot, bring the noise-canceling headphones, the familiar items and the sensory
supports, and tell shelter staff what is needed rather than hoping.

**Mental health conditions** are a real dependency too: medication continuity matters, disruption is
destabilizing, and the plan should name the clinician and the medication rather than treating this as
separate from "medical."

**And the item that covers everything:** a written, one-page description of the person's needs — equipment,
medication, allergies, how they communicate, what helps, what makes things worse, who to call. In the
document envelope, in the go-bag, and on the person. It replaces a conversation nobody will have time for.

## Animals

**People die going back for animals**, and the whole of this section exists to make sure nobody in your
household has to.

**The plan has to be made before, because the constraint is legal and logistical rather than emotional:
many emergency shelters do not accept pets.** Find out now which shelters in your area are pet-friendly, which
boarding kennels and veterinary practices take evacuees, which hotels on your evacuation routes accept
animals, and which of your out-of-area contacts would take an animal. Three phone numbers, written down
(Protection 109).

**The kit**, per animal: a correctly sized carrier stored where it can be reached without moving furniture; a
week of food and water plus bowls; medication; a leash and harness; waste bags or litter and a tray; and a
copy of the vaccination records, because a facility that takes your animal will ask for them.

**The two things that fail on the day.** An animal that has never been in a carrier costs you the fifteen
minutes you do not have — so put the carrier out and make it ordinary long before. And an animal that bolts
when the sirens start is why the safe room drill includes the pets.

**Identification, done properly.** A microchip with **current** registration details, because a chip pointing
at a phone number from three houses ago is useless. A collar and tag as well. And a photograph of each animal
with a household member in the frame, which is how ownership is settled at a reunification point when
hundreds of animals have been collected.

**During the event**, animals come into the safe room or the sealed room with you. In a smoke or hazmat
event they stay indoors with covered water and stored feed rather than pasture. In a heat event, remember that
pavement burns paws, that cars are lethal within minutes, and that flat-faced breeds fail in heat far faster
than others.

**If you genuinely cannot take them** — and this is the situation the book should address rather than
moralize about — leave them indoors, not tied up, with the largest available supply of water in containers
that cannot be knocked over, and food; put a notice on the door stating how many animals are inside, what
they are and your contact number; and tell your local animal control or emergency management. Do not leave
them loose outside, which is how animals are lost permanently.

**Livestock is a different problem and needs a different plan**, made with your veterinarian and your
neighbors. The key facts: large animals move days ahead rather than hours, transport is the binding
constraint and it has to be arranged in advance, identification should be permanent, and in fire and flood
the decision is frequently between moving them early or opening the gates and giving them the chance to move
themselves. Standing water, downed fencing and contaminated feed are the after-event problems. This book does
not pretend to cover it in depth; it says only that if you have livestock, this plan exists and is written
with a professional.

**Afterwards**, animals show stress the way children do: hiding, aggression, house-soiling, appetite loss,
escape attempts. Familiar routine, a confined safe space, and patience. And the practical hazard — after any
flood or fire, keep animals off the ground you have not checked, because broken glass, ash, contaminated water
and displaced wildlife are all out there.
