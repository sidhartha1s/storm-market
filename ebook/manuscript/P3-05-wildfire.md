# Part Three, Playbook 5 — Wildfire

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

The town of Paradise, California burned on the morning of 8 November 2018. The fire started upslope
before dawn, driven by a dry offshore wind, and it moved into a community of tens of thousands with an
effective warning time measured in tens of minutes. It burned 153,336 acres over seventeen days, and its cause was
power lines.
«SRC: CAL FIRE incident record, Camp Fire — started 08 Nov 2018 at 6:33 am at Pulga Road and Camp Creek Road near Jarbo Gap, Butte County; cause **power lines**; **153,336 acres**; active 17 days, contained 25 Nov 2018. https://www.fire.ca.gov/incidents/2018/11/8/camp-fire/ | retrieved 2026-07-29»
**Eighty-five people died.** California's official after-action report puts the destruction at **13,972
residences, 528 commercial buildings and 4,293 other structures** — nearly nineteen thousand buildings.

And then the sentence in that report which should govern how you think about wildfire for the rest of
your life: **"Most of the destruction occurred within the first four hours of the wildfire."**

Four hours. Not four days of a siege. The report also notes that weather agencies **could not forecast
the fire's direction**, and that the wind gusts *"affected evacuation routes, blocked exit routes, and
eventually closed cities."* Every protection in this playbook is calibrated to that timescale, which is
why the go-decision items rank above the hardening items even though the hardening items cost more.
«SRC: California Governor's Office of Emergency Services, *2018 Camp Fire After Action Report* —
153,336 acres; 13,972 residences, 528 commercial and 4,293 other structures destroyed; 85 confirmed
fatalities; three fire personnel injured; contained 25 November 2018; "most of the destruction occurred
within the first four hours." |
https://www.caloes.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/Preparedness/Documents/FINAL-AAR-2018-Camp-Fire-508-Clean-Copy-11.17.25.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Then Lahaina, in August 2023, did the same thing to a coastal Hawaiian town in a hurricane-driven wind.
**101 fatalities** — the deadliest fire in North America in a hundred years.

Hawai'i's Attorney General commissioned an independent three-phase investigation, and the conclusion of
its second report is the sentence to take from it: **"no single factor led to the devastation."** Weather
and its impacts, low perception of risk, decades-old infrastructure, organizational structure, incident
management and coordination — 84 findings and 140 recommendations. In the investigators' own words, *"the
conditions that made this tragedy possible were years in the making."* The first of the three areas
flagged for immediate attention was **"perception of risk and preparedness"**: the low perception of
wildfire risk not matching the growing threat.
«SRC: Hawai'i Department of the Attorney General / Fire Safety Research Institute (UL Research
Institutes), *Lahaina Fire Comprehensive Timeline Report* (Phase One, 17 April 2024) and *Lahaina Fire
Incident Analysis Report* (Phase Two) — 101 fatalities; timeline from 2:55 p.m. HST 8 August 2023 to
8:30 a.m. 9 August; "no single factor"; 84 findings, 140 recommendations. |
https://ag.hawaii.gov/maui-wildfire-investigation-resources-page/ | retrieved 2026-07-29»
«SRC: USFA/FEMA, "Lahaina Hawaii Fire Timeline Report" (2 May 2024). |
https://www.usfa.fema.gov/blog/lahaina-hawaii-fire-timeline-report/ | retrieved 2026-07-29»

That last finding is the one a household can act on tonight, and it is the reason this playbook is in a
book aimed at people who do not think of themselves as living in fire country.

Two facts from these events govern this whole playbook.

**Wind-driven fire does not arrive only as a wall of flame. It arrives as embers** — carried well ahead
of the front, landing in gutters, vents, mulch beds, on decks and under eaves.

A note on how this book states that, because you will see a confident percentage elsewhere. The figure
usually quoted — that some large share of homes are lost to embers rather than flame — could not be
traced to a source that actually measures it, so it is not in this book. What the fire research
institute that runs the controlled experiments does say is that homes ignite through **"a complex mix of
ember attack and direct flame exposure,"** and that consequently **"no single hardening measure can fully
protect a building"** — which is why their guidance, and this chapter, is built as a two-tiered system
rather than a list of purchases.
«SRC: Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety, "Wildland Fire Embers and Flames: Home Mitigations
That Matter" (17 October 2025). |
https://ibhs.org/wildfire/wildland-fire-embers-and-flames-home-mitigations-that-matter/ |
retrieved 2026-07-29»

The practical consequence is unchanged and it is the thing to hold: **you are defending against small
burning objects landing on your house for hours, and against the house next door burning down.** Both
are addressed by the same work.

**Evacuation is a road-capacity problem, and it fails early.** A community on one or two roads cannot
move in twenty minutes what takes an hour to move on a normal day. Waiting for certainty means joining
the line at the worst moment, and cars in line in a fire is precisely the Paradise fatality pattern.

Together, they set the two priorities: harden the house so embers cannot start it, and go absurdly
early.

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Killed by:** entrapment while evacuating late — in vehicles, on roads, at the edge of the fire — and
by staying too long in a structure that then burns. Smoke and superheated air kill before flame in most
structure cases. Elderly and mobility-limited residents are hugely over-represented, because they need
help and the help needs time that a late evacuation does not have.

**Costs:** total loss, overwhelmingly. Wildfire is the hazard with the least partial damage in the book —
a house either survives largely intact or it is gone to the foundations. That has two consequences worth
planning around: the video home inventory (Protection 100) is the only evidence that will exist, and the
insurance question is whether your dwelling limit reflects what rebuilding costs today, because
everybody in the burn scar is rebuilding at once with the same contractors.

The other financial reality of this hazard, stated plainly: in high-risk areas, insurers are
non-renewing and withdrawing, and households are being pushed to state-backed plans of last resort.
Documented mitigation — defensible space, ember-resistant vents, a Class A roof — is increasingly what
keeps a policy available at all, which changes the calculus on Protections 76 and 77 from
saving money to keeping cover.

## Before

Priority order for a Wildfire West household, and note that the first two are structural and free-to-
cheap:

- **76 — ember-resistant vents and Zone 0.** The first five feet around the building is the highest-value
  ground in this hazard. Nothing combustible in it: no bark mulch, no firewood stack, no plastic bins, no
  door mat that will hold an ember, no shrubs against the wall, nothing stored under the deck. Screen
  every vent with fine metal mesh — soffit, gable, foundation, dryer — because a vent is a hole into the
  attic that embers find.
- **77 — defensible space, zones one and two.** Out to thirty feet, then to a hundred. Remove ladder
  fuels that carry fire from the ground into the tree canopy, space the trees, get branches off the roof
  and away from the chimney, keep the grass down.
- **78 — gutters cleared**, which is a Zone 0 problem in disguise: a gutter of dry needles is a fuse
  running along your roof edge.
- **91 — Red Flag Warnings and your evacuation zone**, by name, from your county's own map, with alerts
  enabled.
- **49, 50, 52 — filtration, the box-fan filter, and N95 supply**, because the smoke will reach you
  whether the fire does or not (Playbook 6).
- **107 — go-bags, in the vehicle** through the season, not by the door.
- **106 — two routes out**, both driven, with the honest assessment applied: a single road through
  unbroken fuel is not an escape route.
- **108 — the five- and thirty-minute lists**, which matter more in this hazard than in any other,
  because your warning may genuinely be five minutes.
- **100 — the video inventory**, done properly, this month. In a total loss it is the only record.
- **95 — the trigger plan**, and for wildfire it fires at the *warning* stage, not the order.

**The roof, if you are re-roofing anyway:** Class A rated assembly, and boxed-in eaves. This is the
largest structural determinant of survival after Zone 0.

**Then the neighbor conversation**, which is genuinely part of the protection here and not a nicety.
Structure-to-structure ignition means your neighbor's woodpile is your risk and yours is theirs. In
dense wildland-urban areas, a block that has all done Zone 0 survives at a completely different rate
from a block with one hardened house in it.

**And know who needs help to leave.** Make the arrangement now — the neighbor who does not drive, the
relative on oxygen, the person who will not leave without the animals. Late evacuations are
disproportionately people waiting for help.

## During

**Red Flag Warning or extreme fire weather.** This is your cue, and it is the whole game. Vehicles
fueled and parked facing out. Go-bags in the car. Phones charged. Sleep with clothes, shoes and keys to
hand. Do not start anything that produces a spark outdoors — no mowing dry grass, no grinding, no
burning, and check nothing is dragging under a trailer.

**A fire starts nearby, or an evacuation warning is issued.** Leave. Now, at the warning, not at the
order.

This is the single most important sentence in this playbook and it is worth being blunt about why: a
warning means "be ready", an order means "go", and the difference between them is the difference between
open roads and the Paradise traffic jam. Nobody has ever regretted a wildfire evacuation that turned out to be
unnecessary. Take the five-minute list, the people, the animals, the medication and the documents.

**If you have thirty minutes and it is safe**, work the thirty-minute list and the shutdown sequence in
Protection 113: windows and doors closed, curtains down or pushed clear of glass, furniture away from
windows, gutters flushed if there is a hose to hand, garden furniture and door mats moved away from the
building, gas cylinders moved out and away. Leave exterior lights on. Then go.

**Do not leave sprinklers or hoses running** — it drains the system pressure that firefighters need, and
a wet lawn does not stop an ember in a gutter.

**If you are trapped in your car by the fire**, this is a survival situation with poor options. A car
is poor shelter. Do these six things, in the order CAL FIRE publishes them:

1. Stay calm.
2. Park in an area clear of vegetation.
3. Close all the windows and vents.
4. Cover up with a wool blanket or jacket.
5. Lie on the floor of the car.
6. Call 911 on your cell phone.

Keep a wool blanket in the car through fire season, so step 4 is possible.
«SRC: CAL FIRE, Ready for Wildfire, "Go! Evacuation Guide" — Tips if trapped, in your vehicle: stay calm;
park in an area clear of vegetation; close all vehicle windows and vents; cover up with a wool blanket or
jacket; lie on the vehicle floor; use your cell phone to call 911. |
https://www.readyforwildfire.org/prepare-for-wildfire/go-evacuation-guide/ | retrieved 2026-10-27»

**If you use oxygen**, ask your supplier now, before fire season: what do I do with my oxygen if flames
surround the car? Write the answer on the card in your go-bag. Oxygen makes fire start more easily and
burn faster and hotter.
«SRC: NFPA, "Educational Messages Desk Reference," 2026 edition, message 11.3 — medical oxygen creates an
oxygen-enriched environment, which can cause fires to ignite more easily and burn faster and hotter. |
https://www.nfpa.org/-/media/project/storefront/catalog/files/home-fire-safety/emac/educationalmessagesdeskreference.pdf
| retrieved 2026-10-27»

**What this does not do:** it does not make a car safe in a fire front. It is what to do when leaving
early did not happen.

**If you cannot leave a house**, close everything, move to the side of the building furthest from the
approaching fire, stay away from windows, keep everyone together, and expect to move again once the front
has passed — because the danger afterwards is your own house burning from the inside from embers already
in it.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Wildfires" — evacuate immediately when told to; **if trapped, call 911, give your location and turn on lights to help rescuers find you**; use an N95 against smoke; designate a room closed off from outside air with a portable air cleaner; use high-efficiency filters in central air and set the system to **recirculate with the outdoor intake damper closed**. https://www.ready.gov/wildfires | retrieved 2026-07-29»

## After

**Do not return until it is officially opened.** Burned ground hides holes, hot ash pockets, downed
lines, and trees with root systems burned away that fall without wind.

**Ash is hazardous.** It contains everything the fire consumed: asbestos from older buildings, heavy
metals, treated timber residue, plastics. Do not sweep it dry and do not use a household vacuum on it.
N95 at minimum, gloves, boots, long sleeves, eye protection. Wet it lightly before moving it and bag it.
Keep children and pets out of it entirely.

**Do not assume the water is safe.** This is documented, not theoretical. EPA: *"After the 2017 Tubbs
Fire and the 2018 Camp Fire in California, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were found in the drinking
water of the impacted towns. Tests of the water revealed elevated levels of several VOCs, such as
benzene, in water mains, service connections, and building fixtures."*

The mechanism is worth understanding because it explains why flushing the faucet for ten minutes is not the
answer. When system pressure drops — broken mains, or heavy demand for firefighting — **contaminated air
and water are drawn into the pipes**, and the contamination can travel to parts of the system the fire
never reached. Worse, **VOCs permeate certain plastics such as polyethylene, and rubber, and then leach
back out slowly** — accumulating again in standing water. EPA's own conclusion: the potential for
recontamination persists until all the contaminated material is dealt with.

So: follow the notice, and if there is any doubt, do not drink or cook with the faucet until the utility has
confirmed it — Protection 8. A "do not drink" notice after a fire is a different and more serious animal
than a boil-water notice, because **boiling does not remove benzene.**
«SRC: US EPA, "Addressing Contamination of Drinking Water Distribution Systems from Volatile Organic
Compounds (VOCs) After Wildfires" (2021). |
https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2021-09/addressing-contamination-of-drinking-water-distribution-systems-from-volatile-organic-compounds-after-wildfires_508.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**Hot spots persist for weeks.** Stumps and root systems smolder underground, and structures reignite.
Walk the property, watch for smoke, keep a hose or an extinguisher available.

**Then the aftermath hazard specific to this playbook: burn-scar debris flow.** Fire changes the ground.
Vegetation cover is gone, and fire **increases hydrophobicity in the soil** — chemically, and by working
ash into the soil column — so rain that would have soaked in runs off instead, picking up ash, soil, rock
and burned trees. The result is not a flood. It is a moving slurry with boulders in it.

USGS states the consequence plainly: the enhanced runoff **"permits the initiation of floods and debris
flows even during relatively minor rainstorms."** The agency's two illustrations of what that means:
**25 December 2003**, a high-intensity storm on the Grand Prix and Old burn areas **killed 16 people**
near San Bernardino; **6 February 2010**, debris flows from the Station fire scar overtopped
sediment-retention basins and **damaged or destroyed 46 homes** in La Crescenta. Both storms, USGS notes,
had **recurrence intervals under five years.** Not freak rain. Ordinary rain on ground that had changed.
«SRC: Staley DM, *Emergency Assessment of Post-Fire Debris-Flow Hazards for the 2013 Springs Fire,
Ventura County, California*, USGS Open-File Report 2014-1001. |
https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2014/1001/pdf/of2014-1001.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

If you live in or below a burn scar, that changes your rain response permanently for a while: treat a
heavy-rain forecast as an evacuation question rather than an inconvenience, know the county's debris-flow
warning language, and do not wait to see what happens. Protections 92 and 114 apply, and the trigger
should be lower than you would set for ordinary rain.

**The claim, in a total loss.** Your video inventory is the claim. Expect to produce a contents list
running to hundreds of items, and expect that the process takes months. Ask specifically about
additional living expenses coverage and its time limit, because in a mass-loss event rebuilding takes
longer than the policy period frequently allows, and about extended replacement cost, because everyone
in the burn scar is bidding for the same builders and the same materials at once.
