# Part Three, Playbook 5 — Wildfire

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

The town of Paradise, California burned on the morning of 8 November 2018. The fire started upslope
before dawn, driven by a dry offshore wind, and it moved into a community of tens of thousands with an
effective warning time measured in tens of minutes. It burned 153,336 acres over seventeen days, and its cause was
power lines.
«SRC: CAL FIRE incident record, Camp Fire — started 08 Nov 2018 at 6:33 am at Pulga Road and Camp Creek Road near Jarbo Gap, Butte County; cause **power lines**; **153,336 acres**; active 17 days, contained 25 Nov 2018. https://www.fire.ca.gov/incidents/2018/11/8/camp-fire/ · retrieved 2026-07-29»
Eighty-five people died, most of them in or beside their vehicles or in their homes, and the great
majority of the town's structures were destroyed.
«CITE: the fatality count and structures-destroyed count for the Camp Fire — the CAL FIRE incident page
carries acreage, cause and dates but not casualties; use the Butte County Sheriff / CAL FIRE final
damage inspection figures.»

Then Lahaina, in August 2023, did the same thing to a coastal Hawaiian town in a hurricane-driven wind,
with a comparable death toll and the same central mechanism.
«CITE: official investigation into the Lahaina fire — fatalities, the wind conditions, and the
evacuation findings.»

Two facts from these events govern this whole playbook.

**Wind-driven fire does not arrive as a wall of flame. It arrives as embers, first, and in enormous
quantity** — carried a mile or more ahead of the front, landing in gutters, vents, mulch beds, on decks
and under eaves. Most homes lost in a wildfire are ignited by embers, not by the fire front touching
them. The house next door burning down is then the second-largest threat you face.
«CITE: research finding on the proportion of structures ignited by embers versus direct flame contact.»

**Evacuation is a road-capacity problem, and it fails early.** A community on one or two roads cannot
move in twenty minutes what takes an hour to move on a normal day. Waiting for certainty means joining
the queue at the worst moment, and cars in a queue in a fire is precisely the Paradise fatality pattern.

Together, they set the two priorities: harden the house so embers cannot start it, and go absurdly
early.

---

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Killed by:** entrapment while evacuating late — in vehicles, on roads, at the edge of the fire — and
by staying too long in a structure that then burns. Smoke and superheated air kill before flame in most
structure cases. Elderly and mobility-limited residents are hugely over-represented, because they need
help and the help needs time that a late evacuation does not have.

**Costs:** total loss, overwhelmingly. Wildfire is the hazard with the least partial damage in the book —
a house either survives largely intact or it is gone to the foundations. That has two consequences worth
planning around: the video home inventory (Protection 100) is the only evidence that will exist, and the
insurance question is whether your dwelling limit reflects what rebuilding costs today, because
everybody in the burn scar is rebuilding at once with the same contractors.

The other financial reality of this hazard, stated plainly: in high-risk areas, insurers are
non-renewing and withdrawing, and households are being pushed to state-backed plans of last resort.
Documented mitigation — defensible space, ember-resistant vents, a Class A roof — is increasingly what
keeps a policy available at all, which changes the calculus on Protections 76 and 77 from
saving money to keeping cover.

## Before

Priority order for a Wildfire West household, and note that the first two are structural and free-to-
cheap:

- **76 — ember-resistant vents and Zone 0.** The first five feet around the building is the highest-value
  ground in this hazard. Nothing combustible in it: no bark mulch, no firewood stack, no plastic bins, no
  door mat that will hold an ember, no shrubs against the wall, nothing stored under the deck. Screen
  every vent with fine metal mesh — soffit, gable, foundation, dryer — because a vent is a hole into the
  attic that embers find.
- **77 — defensible space, zones one and two.** Out to thirty feet, then to a hundred. Remove ladder
  fuels that carry fire from the ground into the tree canopy, space the trees, get branches off the roof
  and away from the chimney, keep the grass down.
- **78 — gutters cleared**, which is a Zone 0 problem in disguise: a gutter of dry needles is a fuse
  running along your roof edge.
- **91 — Red Flag Warnings and your evacuation zone**, by name, from your county's own map, with alerts
  enabled.
- **49, 50, 52 — filtration, the box-fan filter, and N95 supply**, because the smoke will reach you
  whether the fire does or not (Playbook 6).
- **107 — go-bags, in the vehicle** through the season, not by the door.
- **106 — two routes out**, both driven, with the honest assessment applied: a single road through
  unbroken fuel is not an escape route.
- **108 — the five- and thirty-minute lists**, which matter more in this hazard than in any other,
  because your warning may genuinely be five minutes.
- **100 — the video inventory**, done properly, this month. In a total loss it is the only record.
- **95 — the trigger plan**, and for wildfire it fires at the *warning* stage, not the order.

**The roof, if you are re-roofing anyway:** Class A rated assembly, and boxed-in eaves. This is the
largest structural determinant of survival after Zone 0.

**Then the neighbour conversation**, which is genuinely part of the protection here and not a nicety.
Structure-to-structure ignition means your neighbour's woodpile is your risk and yours is theirs. In
dense wildland-urban areas, a block that has all done Zone 0 survives at a completely different rate
from a block with one hardened house in it.

**And know who needs help to leave.** Make the arrangement now — the neighbour who does not drive, the
relative on oxygen, the person who will not leave without the animals. Late evacuations are
disproportionately people waiting for help.

## During

**Red Flag Warning or extreme fire weather.** This is your cue, and it is the whole game. Vehicles
fuelled and parked facing out. Go-bags in the car. Phones charged. Sleep with clothes, shoes and keys to
hand. Do not start anything that produces a spark outdoors — no mowing dry grass, no grinding, no
burning, and check nothing is dragging under a trailer.

**A fire starts nearby, or an evacuation warning is issued.** Leave. Now, at the warning, not at the
order.

This is the single most important sentence in this playbook and it is worth being blunt about why: a
warning means "be ready", an order means "go", and the difference between them is the difference between
open roads and the Paradise queue. Nobody has ever regretted a wildfire evacuation that turned out to be
unnecessary. Take the five-minute list, the people, the animals, the medication and the documents.

**If you have thirty minutes and it is safe**, work the thirty-minute list and the shutdown sequence in
Protection 113: windows and doors closed, curtains down or pushed clear of glass, furniture away from
windows, gutters flushed if there is a hose to hand, garden furniture and door mats moved away from the
building, gas cylinders moved out and away. Leave exterior lights on. Then go.

**Do not leave sprinklers or hoses running** — it drains the system pressure that firefighters need, and
a wet lawn does not stop an ember in a gutter.

**If you are caught in a vehicle**, the honest answer is that this is a survival situation with poor
options. Do not drive into smoke you cannot see through. Park in the largest clear area available, away
from vegetation, engine off, headlights and hazards on, windows up, vents closed, on the floor below the
windows, covered with a wool blanket or jacket, and stay in the vehicle while the front passes. A car is
poor shelter and it is dramatically better than being outside it.

**If you cannot leave a house**, close everything, move to the side of the building furthest from the
approaching fire, stay away from windows, keep everyone together, and expect to move again once the front
has passed — because the danger afterwards is your own house burning from the inside from embers already
in it.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Wildfires" — evacuate immediately when told to; **if trapped, call 911, give your location and turn on lights to help rescuers find you**; use an N95 against smoke; designate a room closed off from outside air with a portable air cleaner; use high-efficiency filters in central air and set the system to **recirculate with the outdoor intake damper closed**. https://www.ready.gov/wildfires · retrieved 2026-07-29»

## After

**Do not return until it is officially opened.** Burned ground hides holes, hot ash pockets, downed
lines, and trees with root systems burned away that fall without wind.

**Ash is hazardous.** It contains everything the fire consumed: asbestos from older buildings, heavy
metals, treated timber residue, plastics. Do not sweep it dry and do not use a household vacuum on it.
N95 at minimum, gloves, boots, long sleeves, eye protection. Wet it lightly before moving it and bag it.
Keep children and pets out of it entirely.

**Do not assume the water is safe.** Fires can damage water systems and draw contaminants into
distribution networks, and benzene contamination of municipal supplies has been documented after major
urban-interface fires. Follow the notice and, if there is any doubt, do not use the tap for drinking or
cooking until the utility has confirmed it — Protection 8.
«CITE: documented post-wildfire drinking water contamination — the specific incident and the contaminant
found.»

**Hot spots persist for weeks.** Stumps and root systems smoulder underground, and structures reignite.
Walk the property, watch for smoke, keep a hose or an extinguisher available.

**Then the aftermath hazard specific to this playbook: burn-scar debris flow.** Fire changes the ground.
Vegetation is gone and the soil in many burned areas becomes water-repellent, so rain that would have
soaked in runs off instead, picking up ash, soil, rock and burned trees. The result is not a flood — it
is a moving slurry with boulders in it, and it can be triggered by a rainfall intensity that would be
unremarkable in an unburned watershed. The USGS assesses this risk after major fires and issues
guidance, and the elevated period lasts for years, not months.
«CITE: USGS post-fire debris-flow hazard assessment programme — the rainfall thresholds used and the
duration of elevated risk after a fire.»

If you live in or below a burn scar, that changes your rain response permanently for a while: treat a
heavy-rain forecast as an evacuation question rather than an inconvenience, know the county's debris-flow
warning language, and do not wait to see what happens. Protections 92 and 114 apply, and the trigger
should be lower than you would set for ordinary rain.

**The claim, in a total loss.** Your video inventory is the claim. Expect to produce a contents list
running to hundreds of items, and expect that the process takes months. Ask specifically about
additional living expenses coverage and its time limit, because in a mass-loss event rebuilding takes
longer than the policy period frequently allows, and about extended replacement cost, because everyone
in the burn scar is bidding for the same builders and the same materials at once.
