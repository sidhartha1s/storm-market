## The timeline of a disaster

Knowing what kills people is half of it. The other half is knowing when — and when turns out to be
the part almost everyone gets wrong, because a disaster does not feel like a disaster for most of
its duration.

It has six phases. Each one has a dominant mechanism, a characteristic mistake, and a decision that
belongs in it and nowhere else. Most preparedness advice collapses all six into a single image: the
peak of the event, wind and water, the dramatic middle. That image covers about thirty minutes of a
three-week problem.

Here is the whole clock.

---

## Phase one · The warning window
### Days to minutes before. Dominant mechanism: none yet. This is the only phase where you have options.

Everything cheap happens here, and almost nobody uses it.

The warning window is not one length. It is a spectrum: days for a hurricane, a day or two for a
major winter storm, hours for a flood watch, ten to twenty minutes for a tornado, and none at all
for an earthquake. What matters is that the *decision quality available to you* collapses as the
window shortens, while the *number of decisions demanded* rises. At day three of a hurricane
forecast you can calmly choose a route, a destination, and a departure time. At hour one you are
choosing between bad options in traffic.

The characteristic mistake of this phase has a name in the research literature, and it is the single
most consequential behaviour in this book: **confirmation-seeking.**

When people receive a warning, they do not act on it. They seek to verify it. They look out the
window. They call a relative. They turn on the television. They check whether the neighbours are
doing anything. This is not stupidity — it is a completely rational response to a lifetime of
warnings that came to nothing, and it is nearly universal. The problem is that verification consumes
exactly the resource the warning was purchased with.

This is what the National Weather Service found in its assessment of the Joplin tornado: that most
residents did not take shelter on the first siren, and that a widespread habit of treating sirens as
one signal among several — formed by years of warnings that ended in nothing — was a major factor in
the death toll.
«SRC: NWS Central Region, *Service Assessment: Joplin, Missouri, Tornado — May 22, 2011* — timeline: initial 3-minute siren alert **5:11 pm CDT**; WFO Springfield tornado warning polygon #31 issued **5:17 pm**; initial touchdown **5:34 pm**; **second** 3-minute siren alert **5:38 pm**. As of the report date, **158 direct and 4 indirect fatalities**. Findings: the perceived frequency of siren activation "led the majority of survey participants to become desensitized or complacent to this method of warning"; and Finding #2d — "the majority of surveyed Joplin residents did not take protective action until receiving and processing additional, credible confirmation of the threat and its magnitude from a non-routine trigger", achieved by seeing the tornado, hearing on-air instruction to take cover, or hearing the second siren. Joplin Globe preliminary analysis of fatality location: **54% in residences, 32% in non-residential buildings, 14% in vehicles or outdoors**. https://www.weather.gov/media/publications/assessments/Joplin_tornado.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

You cannot train yourself out of confirmation-seeking by deciding to be braver. What you can do is
move the decision out of this phase entirely, which is the entire purpose of Protection 95: a
written trigger plan. Not a general intention to be careful. A specific, pre-committed threshold and
the specific action attached to it, written on paper, agreed with the household when nothing was
happening.

*When a tornado warning includes our location, we go to the room. We do not look outside.*

*When a hurricane watch is issued for our county, the cars are fuelled and the documents are in the
car.*

*When an evacuation order is issued for our zone, we leave within thirty minutes, and the argument
about whether it is necessary does not happen.*

A trigger plan works because it converts a judgment call made under stress by a frightened person
into a rule already agreed by a calm one. That is the whole mechanism. It is free, it takes an
evening, and it is the highest-leverage item in the first week of the sixty-day sequence.

**The decision that belongs in this phase:** go or stay, and when. Nothing else. Every supply
decision should have been made weeks ago; if you are shopping in the warning window you have already
lost, and you will be shopping in a store that has been stripped of exactly the items you need.

## Phase two · The violent window
### Zero to thirty minutes. Dominant mechanisms: trauma, drowning.

This is the phase everyone pictures, and it is the shortest.

There is nothing to decide here, and that is the point. Every outcome in this window was determined
by decisions made in the previous phase or months earlier: which room, whether the helmets were in
it, whether the garage door held, whether the roof deck was sealed, whether you were already
somewhere else.

The only useful behaviours are reflexes: get low, protect the head, get behind as many walls as
possible, and stay off the phone. Phones fail in this window precisely because everyone reaches for
them at once, and a battery radio does not.

The characteristic mistake is going to look. At a window, on a porch, in a doorway. It kills people
in tornadoes, it kills people in surge, and it kills people in flash floods. The second
characteristic mistake is moving during it — driving out of a tornado's path with insufficient
information about where the path is, or crossing water because the far side looked shallow.

**The decision that belongs in this phase:** none. If you are deciding in this phase, phase one
failed.

## Phase three · The isolation window
### Thirty minutes to six hours. Dominant mechanism: delayed care.

The wind stops. The water crests. And the phase that kills the injured begins.

This is the window where the emergency system is most thoroughly overwhelmed, because demand arrives
all at once and capacity has just been reduced by the same event. Roads are blocked. Networks are
congested. Crews are triaging hundreds of calls in a system sized for dozens. If someone in your
household is bleeding, having a cardiac event, or trapped, this is the window in which the outcome
is decided, and it is decided largely by whoever is standing there.

It is also the window of the second injury wave, which is entirely preventable and consistently
ignored: people walking into a wrecked building in the dark. Broken glass, exposed nails, splintered
framing, downed lines, unstable structure, gas. The shoes you sleep in are not shoes for this. That
is why Protection 43 puts a headlamp and a pair of real shoes at the bedside rather than in a
cupboard — a light you have to find in the dark is a light you do not have.

Three specific hazards live in this window and each has its own aftermath section later in the book:
gas leaks after an earthquake, live conductors on the ground after wind, and floodwater that is
electrically energized or biologically contaminated or both.

**The decisions that belong in this phase:** who is hurt and how badly, whether to attempt
self-transport or wait, whether the building is safe to be in, and whether the utilities need to be
shut off. Protection 35 exists so that the last one takes thirty seconds instead of twenty minutes
of hunting for a wrench.

## Phase four · The degradation window
### Six hours to three days. Dominant mechanisms: thermal failure, toxic atmosphere, sanitation.

The disaster is over. The consequences have barely started.

Power is out and the reason it matters compounds hour by hour. The refrigerator crosses out of safe
temperature. The house drifts toward the outside temperature. Water pressure fails or the water
becomes unsafe. Toilets stop working, which is an inconvenience for a day and a genuine health
hazard by day three. Phones die. Cash becomes the only functioning payment method, if any store is
open at all.

This is where the toxic atmosphere mechanism does its work, because this is when people bring
combustion indoors — generators into garages, grills into kitchens, camp stoves into bedrooms — and
where every fatal carbon monoxide cluster after a storm comes from. It is also where thermal failure
begins its slow work on the old and the isolated, in houses that are merely uncomfortable on day one
and dangerous on day two.

The characteristic mistake of this phase is trying to run the household normally. Conditioning the
whole house instead of one room. Opening the freezer to check. Using water for things that do not
need it. Burning fuel indoors because it is only for a few minutes.

**The decisions that belong in this phase:** consolidate to one room, ration what is finite, get the
generator sited correctly and outdoors, protect the medication that needs cold, and start
documenting damage before anything is cleaned up or thrown away — because the insurance conversation
in phase six is decided by photographs taken in phase four.

## Phase five · The decompensation window
### Three days to two weeks. Dominant mechanism: medical decompensation.

The news trucks leave. The official death toll is published. And the deaths continue.

This is the phase that almost no preparedness material covers and that kills a great many people. It
is where the fifth mechanism operates at full strength: the dialysis appointments missed, the oxygen
concentrators without power, the insulin that spent four days at the wrong temperature, the
medications that ran out on day six, the cardiac patients whose stress and heat and exertion and
missed doses converge.

It is also the phase of exhaustion-driven injury. People who have slept badly for a week climb ladders,
run chainsaws, clear debris and work on roofs. This is not an inference: when the National Hurricane
Center itemised the 43 indirect deaths from Hurricane Michael, the causes it named were **"falls during
the post-storm clean up, traffic accidents, and medical issues compounded by the hurricane."** Falls
first. Not the hurricane — the tidying up afterwards.
«SRC: National Hurricane Center, Tropical Cyclone Report: Hurricane Michael (AL142018) — 43 indirect
deaths, all in Florida; causes included falls during post-storm clean-up, traffic accidents, and medical
issues compounded by the hurricane. | https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL142018_Michael.pdf |
retrieved 2026-07-29»

And it is when the official numbers and the real numbers diverge. The direct toll counts wind and
water. The excess-mortality studies conducted months later count everyone who died who otherwise
would not have — and that second figure is frequently a multiple of the first.
The National Hurricane Center's own accounting is the simplest demonstration. **Katrina: 520 direct
deaths out of 1,392 total. Michael: 16 direct out of 59.** The indirect column — cardiovascular events,
falls during clean-up, traffic accidents on dead intersections, medical conditions the storm made worse —
was larger than the direct column in both.
«SRC: National Hurricane Center, Tropical Cyclone Reports for Hurricane Katrina (AL122005) and Hurricane
Michael (AL142018) — Katrina: 520 direct, 565 indirect, 307 indeterminate, 1,392 total; Michael: 16
direct, 43 indirect. | https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL122005_Katrina.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

The characteristic mistake is believing it is over because the weather is fine.

**The decisions that belong in this phase:** get every dependent person's support restored or
relocated, refill before running out rather than after, accept help, and slow down on the physical
work that is producing the injuries.

---

## Phase six · The recovery window
### Two weeks to two years. Dominant mechanisms: money, mould, and the mind.

The last phase is the longest by an enormous margin, and it is the one where the damage becomes
permanent — financially and psychologically.

Financially, this is where the documentation from phase four either exists or does not. Claims are
filed, adjusters visit, aid applications are submitted, appeals are won or abandoned. The gap between
the average federal award and the statutory maximum is decided here, and it is decided almost
entirely by proof. A household with a video walkthrough, a document vault, receipts, and dated
damage photographs is in a different conversation than a household relying on memory.

Physically, this is mould, which begins within days in wet building materials and becomes a health
problem and a remediation cost that dwarfs the original water damage if the drying is not done
properly and quickly.

Psychologically, this is where the real toll lands, particularly on children, and where the
mechanism is not dramatic at all — it is displacement, disrupted schooling, financial stress, and
the exhaustion of a recovery measured in months. It deserves naming in a preparedness book because
it is the most common long-term outcome of a disaster by a wide margin, and because the households
that fare best are the ones that expected a long recovery rather than a short one.

**The decisions that belong in this phase:** document before cleaning, dry aggressively, file
carefully, appeal rather than accept, and plan for months.

## The clock, in one table

| Phase | When | Dominant mechanism | The mistake | Belongs here |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Warning | Days to minutes before | None yet | Seeking confirmation instead of acting | Go or stay, and when |
| Violent | 0–30 min | Trauma, drowning | Going to look; moving | Nothing — reflexes only |
| Isolation | 30 min–6 hr | Delayed care | Walking into wreckage unshod, in the dark | Who is hurt; is the building safe; utilities off |
| Degradation | 6 hr–3 days | Thermal, toxic atmosphere, sanitation | Running the household normally | Consolidate, ration, site the generator, document |
| Decompensation | 3 days–2 weeks | Medical decompensation | Believing it is over | Restore support; refill early; slow the physical work |
| Recovery | 2 weeks–2 years | Money, mould, the mind | Cleaning up before documenting | Document, dry, file, appeal, plan for months |

## Why the clock matters more than the hazard

Two things follow from this chapter, and they shape everything after it.

**First: the phases are not equally prepared for, and the imbalance is backwards.** Almost all
public preparedness attention goes to phases one and two — the warning and the violence. Almost all
of the deaths beyond the first hours occur in phases four and five, where attention has already moved
on. If you have finite effort, and everyone does, the marginal hour is better spent on the
prescription buffer, the carbon monoxide alarms, and the one-room plan than on anything else.

**Second: preparation is phase-shifted from the event by design.** Every useful thing you can do sits
in a phase before the one where it pays. The room is chosen in ordinary time and pays in phase two.
The helmets are bought in ordinary time and pay in phase two. The generator siting is learned in
ordinary time and pays in phase four. The video walkthrough is made in ordinary time and pays in
phase six, eighteen months later, in an adjuster's spreadsheet.

Which is a formal way of saying the obvious thing: **there is no phase in which you can prepare, and
there is no phase in which preparation does not decide the outcome.** The only window available is
the one you are in right now, when nothing is happening.

That is what the sixty days in Part Seven are for. And it is why the next part of this book is not
about hazards at all — it is about the six things every household needs regardless of which hazard
is coming, because those six carry you through phases three, four and five of all twenty-three.
