# Part Three, Playbook 13 — Earthquake

Target: 10 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

## The event

At half past four in the morning of 17 January 1994, a fault nobody had mapped ruptured beneath the San
Fernando Valley. USGS's account: a **magnitude 6.7** shock on a **"blind" or buried thrust fault** that had
not previously been identified, which **raised the mountains and the northern San Fernando Valley by about
a foot.** Some **10 million people** were woken by it.

**Fifty-seven people died. More than 9,000 were injured. More than 20,000 were displaced from their homes.
More than 1,600 buildings were later red-tagged as unsafe.** Estimated losses: **20 billion dollars** — at
the time, the most costly earthquake in United States history.
«SRC: US Geological Survey, "Response to an Urban Earthquake — Northridge '94," Open-File Report 96-263 —
magnitude 6.7; 57 dead, more than 9,000 injured, more than 20,000 displaced, more than 1,600 buildings
red-tagged; estimated losses 20 billion dollars. |
https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1996/ofr-96-0263/introduc.htm | retrieved 2026-07-29»
«SRC: US Geological Survey, Open-File Report 94-214, "Northridge California Earthquake 1994" (Educator's
Guide summary) — blind/buried thrust fault, previously unidentified; uplift of about one foot. |
https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1994/0214a/report.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Hold on to the fault detail, because it is the whole argument of this playbook: **the fault that did this
was not on the map.** No warning, no season, and no list of safe addresses.

Three things about it define this playbook.

**It was over before anyone could react.** Twenty seconds is not time to make a plan; it is only time to
execute one that already exists. Every protection in this playbook is therefore pre-event, and the "during"
section is four words long.

**Most injuries were not caused by the building failing.** They were caused by things inside buildings that
moved — falling objects, toppling furniture, breaking glass, and people cut on debris while trying to move
during the shaking.

**And the fault was unmapped.** Earthquakes are the one hazard in this book with no warning of any kind and
no season. The Pacific Northwest has the Cascadia subduction zone, capable of a magnitude nine and a
tsunami; the central US has the New Madrid zone, where a major earthquake would affect a region with almost
no seismic building standards; and moderate damaging earthquakes are possible in many states that consider
themselves safe.

**How many states?** The 2023 fifty-state update of the USGS National Seismic Hazard Model — the model
that building codes are written from — concluded that **nearly 75 per cent of the United States could
experience damaging earthquake shaking.** Not "will," and not soon: the map is a hundred-year probability of
shaking strong enough to do damage. But three-quarters of the country is a very different picture from the
two or three states most people would name.

**And the Cascadia numbers are now published with probabilities**, which is more useful than "overdue" —
a word this book will not use, because the seismic record does not support it. USGS's 2025 assessment for
the Pacific Northwest gives, for the next fifty years: a **10 per cent chance of a full-margin magnitude
~9 rupture** on a time-independent model, or **15 per cent** on a time-dependent model that accounts for the
last one having been in 1700; an **~30 per cent chance of a magnitude 8-or-greater event in southern
Cascadia**; an **85 per cent chance of a deep intraslab earthquake in the Puget Sound region** — the type
that produced the 2001 Nisqually event; and a **17 per cent chance of a magnitude 6.5-or-greater shallow
crustal fault earthquake around Puget Sound.**

Read that list in the right order. The magnitude nine is the one that gets written about, and it is the
least likely of the four. **The everyday earthquake risk in the Pacific Northwest is the deep one at 85 per
cent in fifty years**, and the protections in this playbook are the same for both.

(2024), on the 2023 50-state update to the National Seismic Hazard Model — nearly 75% of the United States
could experience damaging earthquake shaking; the congressionally requested update is used in building
codes. |
https://www.usgs.gov/news/national-news-release/new-usgs-map-shows-where-damaging-earthquakes-are-most-likely-occur-us | retrieved 2026-07-29»
«SRC: US Geological Survey, "Earthquake probabilities and hazards in the U.S. Pacific Northwest," Fact

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Killed by:** structural collapse, which is dominated by specific building types — unreinforced masonry,
soft-story buildings with parking or shopfronts under living space, and older non-ductile concrete. If you
live or work in one of these, that is the single most important fact in this playbook and it is knowable
today.

**Injured by, in vastly greater numbers:** falling objects and toppling furniture. Bookcases, wall units,
televisions, water heaters, mirrors, hanging light fittings, and the contents of upper kitchen cabinets.
Then cuts to feet and hands from broken glass, which is why Protection 43 puts shoes and a flashlight under every
bed — and an earthquake at four in the morning is the specific reason.

**Then fire.** Post-earthquake fire is historically the largest secondary killer and the largest secondary
loss: gas lines break, electrical faults arc, and the fire service arrives late because roads are blocked,
water mains are broken and every call comes at once. In some historic earthquakes the fire caused more
destruction than the shaking.

**Costs, and here the insurance position is stark:** earthquake damage is **excluded from standard
homeowner policies** and requires separate cover.

**California's own law tells you how few people buy it, by how hard the state has to work to make them
consider it.** Every insurer writing residential property cover in California **must offer you earthquake
insurance in writing, every other year.** The offer has to state the limits, the deductible and the
premium; you have **thirty days to accept; and if you do not reply, you have rejected it.** A statutory
biennial reminder exists because the default behavior is to not buy.

**Then the deductible, which is the part that changes the calculation.** This is not a dollar deductible.
The California Earthquake Authority offers **5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 per cent** — a percentage of your
dwelling limit. And for two categories the floor is higher: a home worth over a million dollars, or **a
home built before 1980 on a raised or other non-slab foundation that has not been verified as seismically
retrofitted**, can get no better than **15 per cent.** On a $600,000 dwelling limit that is a $90,000
deductible. The policy also excludes landscaping, pools, fences, masonry and separate buildings.

**Notice what that pre-1980 rule is actually telling you.** The state's own insurer prices an unretrofitted
raised-foundation house as a category of its own, and offers up to a **25 per cent premium discount** for
older homes that have been properly retrofitted — bolting the frame to the foundation, bracing cripple
walls, the work in Protection 83. The insurance market has already scored the retrofit for you.

«SRC: California Department of Insurance, "Earthquake Insurance" consumer guide — insurers writing
residential property insurance in California must offer earthquake insurance in writing every other year,
stating limits, deductible and premium, with 30 days to accept and non-reply constituting rejection; CEA
offers deductibles of 5%, 10%, 15%, 20% and 25%, with a 15% floor for homes valued over $1 million and for
homes built before 1980 on raised or other non-slab foundations not verified as seismically retrofitted
(state law only requires insurers to offer a 15% deductible); older homes may qualify for a discount of up
to 25% if properly retrofitted; policies do not cover landscaping, pools, fences, masonry or separate
buildings; $10,000 building code upgrade coverage available separately. |
https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/03-res/eq-ins.cfm | retrieved 2026-07-29»

That means the realistic financial plan for most readers is not insurance — it is retrofit plus the
protections that reduce the damage. Which is why the structural protections in this playbook are the money
chapter for anyone west of the Rockies.

## Before

Priority order for a Quake West household:

- **83 — anchoring what will otherwise fall on you.** The highest-value, lowest-cost protection in this
  playbook and the one that prevents most injuries. Straps and brackets on tall furniture into studs, not
  drywall. Latches on cabinets, especially upper kitchen cabinets over where people stand. Televisions and
  monitors strapped. Nothing heavy or glass hung above a bed or a sofa. Heavy items on low shelves. A
  weekend and a hundred dollars of hardware buys most of the injury reduction available to you.
- **The water heater specifically**, because it deserves naming separately: strapped top and bottom to the
  studs with proper seismic strapping. An unstrapped water heater walks, breaks its gas line and its water
  line, and gives you a fire and a flood in the same second. It is also forty to eighty gallons of drinking
  water (Protection 4) if it survives.
- **84 — gas flex connectors and automatic shutoff.** Flexible connectors on appliances so a small movement
  does not shear a rigid pipe. An excess-flow or seismic shutoff valve on the service is worth costing —
  understand that it must be permitted and that it has consequences for restoration.
- **35 — shut-off literacy**, gas and water, wrench attached, at least two people who know it.
- **43 — a flashlight and sturdy shoes at every bedside**, tied to the bed so they do not become part of the
  debris. Non-negotiable.
- **1 — water store**, and here for longer than most hazards: a major earthquake breaks distribution mains,
  and municipal water can be out or unsafe for weeks. This is the hazard where a two-week store is a
  genuinely serious number rather than a cautious one.
- **57, 58 — the trauma kit and bleeding-control training.** Crush injuries, lacerations and fractures, with
  EMS overwhelmed and hospitals damaged. Protection 67 — when EMS cannot reach you — is written for this
  playbook more than any other.
- **112 — the out-of-area contact**, because local networks will be down and long-distance frequently works
  when local does not.
- **107 — go-bags**, and a **spare set outside the house**: in the car, at work, at a relative's. If the
  house is unsafe to enter you do not get what is inside it.
- **68 — spares**: glasses, medication, hearing aid batteries, mobility aids. People spend weeks without
  these after an earthquake.
- **A structural assessment** if your building is one of the vulnerable types. Foundation bolting and
  cripple-wall bracing on older wood-frame houses is a well-defined retrofit with a well-defined cost and it
  is the single most effective structural spend available to a homeowner. Many cities have grant or subsidy
  programs for exactly this work.
- **Sign up for ShakeAlert**, the USGS earthquake early warning system, if you are in **California, Oregon
  or Washington** — those are the three states it is built to cover. It delivers seconds, sometimes tens
  of seconds, which is enough to get under a table, stop a surgeon's hand, or halt a train.

  **Then calibrate your expectations, because the honest numbers are smaller than the headlines.** USGS's
  own study of expected warning times in the Pacific Northwest concluded that for the shallow crustal and
  intraslab earthquakes that will produce most alerts, *"users will only have a few seconds of warning
  before strong shaking begins"* — and that this *"remains true even during infrequent, offshore great
  (magnitude about 8) megathrust earthquakes,"* where times *"generally range from seconds to tens of
  seconds,"* with the longest warnings of **50 to 80 seconds possible only for users far from the
  epicenter.** And there is a zone near every epicenter that gets **no warning at all**, because detection
  itself takes seconds.

  So plan for *a few seconds*. That is enough for drop, cover, hold on, and it is not enough to go
  anywhere. A household that expects half a minute will use the alert badly.
  «SRC: J.J. McGuire, D.E. Smith, A.D. Frankel, E.A. Wirth, S.K. McBride and R.M. de Groot, "Expected
  Warning Times from the ShakeAlert Earthquake Early Warning System for Earthquakes in the Pacific
  Northwest," USGS Open-File Report 2021-1026 — a few seconds of warning for shallow crustal and
  intraslab events, seconds to tens of seconds for offshore great megathrust events, longest times of
  50-80 seconds only at considerable distance from the epicenter, and a no-warning zone near every
  epicentre. | https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/2021/1026/ofr20211026.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»
  «SRC: US Geological Survey, "ShakeAlert" — the system is being developed to cover California, Oregon
  and Washington. | https://earthquake.usgs.gov/data/shakealert/ | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**Then practice the response**, because twenty seconds only works as muscle memory. Drop, cover, hold on, in
the actual rooms you actually use, twice a year, including at night.

## During

**Drop, cover, hold on.**

Drop to your hands and knees. Get under a sturdy table or desk if one is within reach, and if not, get
beside an interior wall away from windows and cover your head and neck with your arms. Hold on to whatever
is protecting you, because it will move.

**Do not run.** Do not run outside, do not run for a doorway, do not run downstairs.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Earthquakes" — if inside, **stay inside and do not run outside; avoid doorways**; in bed, turn face down and cover head and neck with a pillow; in a car, pull over and set the parking brake; outdoors, stay in the open away from buildings, trees, streetlights and power lines. After: expect aftershocks, do not enter damaged buildings, and **if trapped, send a text or bang on a pipe and use a whistle rather than shouting**. https://www.ready.gov/earthquakes | retrieved 2026-07-29» Most injuries happen to
people moving during shaking, and the most dangerous place in an earthquake is immediately outside a
building, where the glass, the cladding and the parapets land.

**The "triangle of life" advice is not the recommended guidance** and contradicts what agencies and
engineers advise for the building stock this book's readers live in. Drop, cover, hold on.

**In bed:** stay there, turn face down and cover your head and neck with a pillow. Getting up in the dark
onto a floor of broken glass is worse.

**In a wheelchair or unable to get down:** lock the wheels, lean forward, cover your head and neck.

**Outdoors:** move to an open area away from buildings, walls, power lines and trees, and stay there.

**Driving:** pull over clear of overpasses, bridges, signs and power lines, stop and stay in the vehicle
with the seatbelt on until the shaking stops.

**Near the coast:** the shaking **is** the tsunami warning. If a strong earthquake lasts more than about
twenty seconds and you are in a coastal zone, the moment it stops you move inland and uphill on foot without
waiting for an official alert. Playbook 14.

**Then expect aftershocks**, immediately and for months. Each one arrives at a structure that is already
damaged, which is why the next section matters as much as this one.

## After

**In the first minute, before you move: shoes.** Then check yourself, then the people with you.

**Smell and listen for gas.** If you smell or hear it, get everyone out, shut the valve at the meter if you
can safely, and call from a distance. **Do not use switches, do not use a phone inside, do not light
anything.** But do not shut the gas off as a precaution when there is no sign of a leak — restoration after
a regional earthquake takes the utility weeks (Protection 129).

**Assume the electrics may be damaged.** If you see sparking, smell burning insulation or see damaged
wiring, shut the main breaker off if you can reach it safely and dry.

**Expect fire, and deal with small ones fast.** This is the largest secondary threat and the fire service
will not be coming quickly. Know where the extinguishers are and check the building for smoke, including
the roof space.

**Then decide whether the building is safe to be in**, and be conservative. Get out and stay out if you see
a shifted foundation, cracks in the foundation or exterior walls that pass through, a wall out of plumb, a
sagging roof or floor, a chimney that has cracked or moved — chimneys fail early and fall predictably, in
both directions. Do not use a fireplace until the chimney and flue have been inspected, because a cracked
flue vents combustion products into the structure.

**Do not go back in for possessions** during aftershocks. This is where people die after the earthquake.

**Water.** Assume the supply is unsafe and follow any boil or do-not-drink notice; mains break and pressure
loss draws contamination in. The water heater and toilet tanks are reserves (Protection 4). Sanitation
becomes an immediate problem if the sewer is broken — do not flush into a broken line, and go to the
twin-bucket system in Protection 53, which was written for this scenario.

**Communicate by text**, not voice, and use the out-of-area contact as the hub.

**Then the money, honestly.** Photograph everything before touching. If you carry earthquake cover, note the
percentage deductible and understand what it means before you assume there is a claim. Register for federal
assistance, which is where the gap from Part One is at its widest, and keep the log. If your area has a
post-disaster building inspection program, get the placard, because it determines occupancy and it matters
for both aid and insurance.

**And plan for the aftershock sequence rather than a single event.** For weeks the correct posture is that
another one is coming: nothing heavy stacked high, the go-bag stays packed, the shoes stay by the bed, and
anyone sleeping in a building with unrepaired damage should not be.
