# Part Three, Playbook 8 — Lightning

Target: 8 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

There is no famous lightning disaster, and that is precisely the problem.

Lightning killed an average of **27 Americans a year over the last measured decade** and injured
roughly **243 a year**, and it does it one or two at a time, on golf courses and beaches and building
sites and in back gardens, in weather that nobody thought was dangerous.
«SRC: NWS, "How Dangerous is Lightning?" — from NWS Storm Data, the US averaged **43 reported lightning fatalities a year over 1989–2018** and **27 a year over 2009–2018**, with roughly **243 injuries a year** estimated. **Only about 10% of people struck are killed, leaving 90% with varying degrees of disability.** Lightning can cause cardiac arrest at the time of injury. https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-odds · retrieved 2026-07-29»

The recurring pattern in the fatality reports is not people caught out in a violent storm. It is people
who had time. The thunder had been audible for some minutes. The activity was nearly finished. The
shelter was two hundred yards away. Someone decided to play the last hole, get the last load in, finish
the last field.

The other half of the pattern is the survivors, who are the far larger group and get almost no
attention. **Only about one in ten people struck by lightning is killed** — which leaves ninety percent
living with some degree of disability: nerve damage, chronic pain, memory and concentration problems,
sleep disruption.

**So this playbook is short and it is one instruction: when thunder roars, go indoors.** Everything else
is detail.

---

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Killed by:** cardiac arrest, immediately, in most fatal cases. The current interrupts the heart's
rhythm and the person collapses. That is why the intervention is CPR and why it works: a person struck by
lightning who receives immediate CPR has a genuine chance, and a person who waits eight minutes for an
ambulance usually does not.

**How the current reaches people**, in rough order of how many it affects:

**Ground current**, which spreads through the earth from the strike point and is the largest killer —
this is why sheltering under a tree is so dangerous and why proximity matters more than being hit.
**Side flash**, where current jumps from a struck object to a person nearby. **Conduction**, through
plumbing, wiring, corded phones and metal structures, which is how people are struck indoors.
**Direct strike**, which is the rarest and the one everyone pictures.
«SRC: NWS, "Lightning Science: Five Ways Lightning Strikes People" — direct strike, side flash, ground current, conduction and streamers. Direct strikes are less common than the other mechanisms but the most deadly; side-flash victims are typically within a foot or two of the struck object, most often sheltering under a tree. https://www.weather.gov/safety/lightning-struck · retrieved 2026-07-29»

**Costs:** house fires, which are the serious property outcome; electronics and appliance destruction,
both from direct strikes and from surges coupling into wiring from strikes nearby; well pumps and HVAC
control boards, which are common and expensive casualties; and tree damage that then becomes a structural
problem.

## Before

- **120 — unplug on warning**, the free protection, applied habitually.
- **46 — whole-home surge protection** at the panel, plus point-of-use protectors on what matters, plus
  protection on the data lines — coaxial and telephone lines carry surges as readily as the mains.
- **48 — working smoke alarms**, on every level, because the mechanism that turns a lightning strike into
  a catastrophe is fire in a roof space at three in the morning.
- **124 — the rule taught to everyone in the household**, especially the children and especially anyone
  who coaches, works outdoors or plays sport.
- **58 — CPR training.** In no other hazard in this book does a household skill map so directly onto a
  survivable death. If one protection in this playbook is done, make it this one.
- **A lightning protection system** — air terminals, conductors and grounding, professionally designed to
  standard — is worth costing only in genuinely high-strike locations, for tall or isolated buildings,
  or where the contents justify it. It is a real engineered system, not a decorative rod, and it is out of
  scope for most houses.
  «CITE: the current US standard for lightning protection system installation, by number and name.»

**The thing to decide in advance** is where "inside" is for the places your household regularly spends
time. The pitch, the pool, the site, the campsite, the beach. A substantial building or a hard-topped
vehicle counts. A dugout, a picnic shelter, a marquee, a tent, a porch and a bus stop do not. Knowing the
answer in advance is what makes leaving at the first thunder possible rather than debatable.

---

## During

**When thunder roars, go indoors.** If you can hear thunder, you are within range of the next strike.
There is no safe count of seconds between flash and bang, because the next strike does not have to be
where the last one was.

**Wait thirty minutes after the last thunder** before going back out. This is the part everybody skips,
and the reports show casualties on both sides of the storm — before it visibly arrives and after it
appears to have passed.

**Safe shelter is a substantial building** with wiring and plumbing, or **a hard-topped vehicle with the
windows up**. In the vehicle, do not touch metal surfaces, and understand why it works: the metal shell
conducts current around the occupants. A convertible, a golf cart, an open tractor cab and a motorbike
are not shelter.

**Indoors, during a close storm:** off corded phones, away from plumbing — no shower, no bath, no washing
up — and away from anything plugged into a wall. Stay off porches and away from windows and doors. Mobile
phones not connected to a charger are fine.

**If you are genuinely caught outdoors with no shelter**, minimise your risk rather than pretending to be
safe. Get off high ground and out of open fields. Get away from water, from metal fences and from tall
isolated trees, which are the worst place available. Spread the group out so a single strike cannot injure
everyone. Do not lie flat — that maximises your contact with ground current. Keep moving toward real
shelter rather than settling in.

**The crouch you may have been taught is no longer recommended as advice.** It offers so little benefit
that teaching it wrongly implies there is a safe way to be outside in a thunderstorm. If you have ended up
in that position, the honest framing is that you have already lost most of your protection and the only
remaining move is to get somewhere real.

**In or on water is the worst case.** Get out of the pool, off the lake, off the beach and away from the
shoreline at the first thunder, and off a boat and to shore before the storm arrives rather than as it
does.

---

## After

**Treat the person first, and do not hesitate to touch them.** A lightning victim carries no residual
charge. This myth costs lives every year.

**Check for breathing and a pulse. If either is absent, start CPR immediately** and send someone to call
for help. This is the highest-yield first aid in this book — cardiac arrest from lightning frequently
responds to prompt resuscitation because the heart is otherwise healthy.

**In a group casualty situation, reverse the usual triage.** Attend to the people who are not moving
first: the quiet ones are the ones in arrest, and those who are conscious and calling out are, by
definition, breathing.

**Then get everyone to medical care, including people who feel fine.** Lightning injury includes cardiac
rhythm disturbances, burns that develop over hours, blast injury to ears and eyes, and neurological
effects that appear later. Anyone struck or in close proximity to a strike needs assessment, and anyone
with hearing changes, confusion, chest symptoms or numbness needs it urgently.

**Then the building.** A strike to a structure frequently starts a fire in a concealed space — the attic,
a wall cavity, the roof deck — that smoulders before it shows. If your house is struck, get everybody out
and call the fire service even if there is nothing visible. Have them check with a thermal camera. This
is the difference between a scorched rafter and a total loss.

**Then the systems.** Check what has died and what has been damaged but is still running: HVAC control
boards, the well pump, the septic controller, the garage door opener, the router, the alarm system.
Surge damage frequently kills things days later. Replace any point-of-use surge protector that took the
hit, and photograph everything for the claim — a lightning strike is a covered peril under a standard
homeowner policy, which makes this one of the few hazards in the book where the insurance is usually
straightforward.

**And re-check the smoke alarms**, because a strike that damaged wiring may have damaged them.
