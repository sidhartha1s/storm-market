## Structure hardening

Everything in the last six chapters protects a person. This chapter protects the building, and it
matters more than it sounds, for a reason established in Part One: **most of the trauma in a wind
event is delivered by your own house.** Wind does not usually carry a house away. It finds an
opening, pressurises the interior, and pushes the roof off from underneath. Everything that then
injures you is material that used to be your home.

So the highest-value protections against trauma are not personal equipment. They are the ones that
keep the envelope intact.

This is also the only chapter where the money argument is real and documented. Mitigation earns
insurance credits in several states — mandated by statute in some — and it is the one place where a
protection can pay for itself annually rather than once.

Three honest warnings before the list.

**Most of this is not a DIY chapter.** Roof work, structural connections, and anything involving a
ladder above single-storey height is hire-it work. The book tells you what to buy and what to
demand; it does not teach you to do it.

**Renters can skip most of this chapter** and should not feel behind. Every protection here is marked
for whether it is possible in a rental, and the highest-value items in the book for a renter are
elsewhere.

**Time these to work you were already doing.** Nearly all of it is dramatically cheaper at
re-roofing, re-siding, or window-replacement time than as a standalone project. A protection you do
during a planned replacement can cost a small premium; the same protection alone can cost ten times
that.

### 69 · Sealed roof deck

**Prevents:** water destroying the inside of a house whose roof covering has gone.
**Cost:** small premium at re-roof; $1,000–4,000 standalone (Florida's state programme prices a deck
nailing upgrade at $1,000–4,000 and a secondary water barrier at $500–2,500) · **Time:** with a re-roof
· **Materials:** taped or fully-adhered underlayment · **Regions:** Hurricane Coast rank 4,
Tornado Alley rank 8. Owners only.

The single highest-return wind upgrade there is, and almost nobody asks for it.

When shingles come off in a wind event, what stops the rain is the deck beneath them. An ordinary
deck is plywood sheets with open seams and a layer of felt. A **sealed roof deck** tapes or fully
adheres those seams so that a roof stripped of its covering is still a water barrier. The difference
between a repairable roof and a gutted interior is frequently exactly this.

This is the core of the recognised fortified-construction standards. IBHS describes taping over all
horizontal and vertical joints before the underlayment goes on as **"one of the most cost-effective and
easy-to-install elements"** of its FORTIFIED Roof programme — and it is moving out of the voluntary
standard and into law: Connecticut amended the 2015 International Residential Code to require exactly
that step on roof decking.
«SRC: Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety, "Lead with the Roof" / FORTIFIED Home technical
standards — sealed roof deck; Connecticut IRC amendment requiring taped deck joints. |
https://ibhs.org/fortified/roof/ | retrieved 2026-07-29»

It is designed to be done at re-roofing time — which is why the instruction is to **decide now and put
it in the file**, so that when your roof is next replaced you specify it rather than discovering it
afterwards.

Get the documentation. It is what earns the insurance credit in Protection 104.

**What this does not do:** it does not keep the shingles on and it is not a substitute for
Protection 70. It also earns nothing at your insurer without the paperwork proving it was done to
standard.

### 70 · Ring-shank renailing, timed to the re-roof

**Prevents:** the deck itself lifting off the framing.
**Cost:** small premium at re-roof · **Time:** with a re-roof · **Materials:** ring-shank nails
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, Tornado Alley. Owners only.

Older decks were often attached with smooth-shank nails or staples, which withdraw under uplift far
more easily than ring-shank nails. Renailing the deck to current specification during a re-roof is
inexpensive as a line item and effectively impossible afterwards.

**The specification to write on the contract** is the one the FORTIFIED standard uses, because it is
published, unambiguous, and what an evaluator will check: **8d ring-shank nails at 6 inches on centre
into every framing member**, with 7/16-inch sheathing preferred. If your existing deck already has 8d
or larger nails, the standard also allows augmenting rather than replacing — adding 8d ring-shanks so
that the gap between old and new nails is no more than 4 inches on all framing members. An evaluator
documenting the work expects **at least thirteen nails in each four-foot span.** IBHS's own summary of
why is the sentence to quote to a contractor who resists: ring-shank nails *"nearly double the strength
of your roof against the forces of winds."*

Ask for it by name, in writing, on the contract, and have the inspector confirm it. On a re-roof this is
a small line item; the same work later means tearing the covering off again.
«SRC: Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety, FORTIFIED Engineering Standards Technical
Bulletin 2016-04, "Roof Deck", and the FORTIFIED Roof high-wind roofing compliance form — 8d ring-shank
nails at 6 in. o.c. on all framing members, 7/16 in. sheathing preferred, maximum framing spacing 24
in.; augmentation option with combined spacing not greater than 4 in.; at least 13 nails per 4-ft span
for documentation. | https://fortifiedhome.org/wp-content/uploads/2016-04_technical-bulletin_roof-deck.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**What this does not do:** nothing for the covering above it, and nothing at all if the roof is not
being replaced. This is a timing protection.

### 71 · Impact-rated shingles where hail is the claim driver

**Prevents:** a roof replacement every few years, and the deductible each time.
**Cost:** $500–2,500 premium at re-roof · **Time:** with a re-roof · **Materials:** Class 4 rated
covering · **Regions:** Tornado Alley, hail corridor, Wildfire West front range. Owners only.

In hail country the roof is a consumable, and impact-rated coverings are the one upgrade that changes
that. Insurers commonly discount for them, which makes this the rare protection with a defined annual
return.

**But the rating deserves a hard look, and this is where the book has to be honest with you.** The test
that produces the familiar Class 1 to Class 4 impact ratings is **Underwriters Laboratories 2218**, and
it works by dropping **steel ball bearings** on shingles. IBHS, which runs the largest hail research
programme in the country, replicated that test and its conclusion is worth reading twice: the UL 2218
test *"has limited ability to recreate the physics of actual hail,"* and IBHS's own results *"differed
from the protections marketed to consumers."* Real hailstones are not steel; they shatter, they vary in
density, and they behave differently on impact.

IBHS's response was to build a new hail impact standard for asphalt shingles using measured properties
of actual hailstones, and to publish product ratings from it. **So the practical instruction is: ask for
a covering that performs well on the IBHS hail ratings, not only one that carries a Class 4 label.** A
Class 4 sticker is a real signal and better than nothing — it is also a steel-ball result, and the
industry that studies hail hardest has said so in public.

Ask your carrier for the discount **before** buying, because the credit is carrier-specific and
sometimes conditional on the exact rating — and ask which rating system they recognise.
«SRC: Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety, "FORTIFIED Roof — Roof Product Performance
Testing" and IBHS hail risk research — the current UL 2218 test for hail resistance of asphalt shingles
is based on impacts of steel ball bearings and "has limited ability to recreate the physics of actual
hail"; IBHS replicated it and "produced research results that differed from the protections marketed to
consumers"; IBHS developed a new hail impact test standard using measured hailstone properties and
publishes impact-resistant shingle ratings. | https://ibhs.org/fortified/roof/ | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**What this does not do:** impact-rated is not hail-proof, and very large stones damage everything.
It also does nothing for the siding, the vehicles, or the AC condenser, which are a large share of
hail claims.

### 72 · Hurricane straps and gable-end bracing

**Prevents:** the roof structure separating from the walls.
**Cost:** $800–3,000, the range Florida's state grant programme publishes for roof-to-wall
reinforcement · **Time:** days · **Materials:** metal connectors, bracing lumber
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, Tornado Alley. Owners only.

Older houses frequently rely on nails alone at the roof-to-wall connection. Metal connectors tie the
structure together against uplift, and gable-end bracing keeps a large flat wall from being pushed
in — a gable is a sail, and an unbraced one is a common failure point.

Both jobs are usually done from inside the attic, which makes them possible without touching the
roof covering, and both belong to a contractor.

**What this does not do:** connectors do not compensate for a rotted or termite-damaged connection,
and they do nothing if the opening protection fails first and the house pressurises. This protection
only pays when Protections 73 and 74 are also done.

### 73 · Opening protection — shutters, impact glass, or plywood, with the real spec

**Prevents:** the breach that lets the wind inside.
**Cost:** $50–200 per opening for plywood; $5,000–15,000 for shutters or impact glass, the range
Florida's state grant programme publishes
· **Time:** varies · **Materials:** rated shutters, impact windows, or correctly specified sheathing
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast rank 2. Owners, and renters with permission.

Once a window fails, the house becomes a pressure vessel and Protection 72 is fighting a load it was
not designed for. Protecting the openings is therefore upstream of most other structural work.

Three tiers. **Rated shutters** — accordion, roll-down, or panel — are the practical answer and are
what most credit schemes recognise. **Impact-rated glazing** is the invisible, permanent, expensive
option. **Plywood** is the last resort, and it only works to a real specification: correct thickness,
correct fastener, correct edge distance, and pre-cut and labelled per window *before* the season,
because cutting plywood in a queue at a hardware store two days before landfall is not a plan.

The weather service's own hurricane guidance gives both the ranking and the specification in two
sentences: *"permanent storm shutters offer the best protection for windows,"* and the second option is
to *"board up windows with 5/8 inch exterior grade or marine plywood, built to fit and ready to
install."* Note the three separate requirements packed into that — **5/8 inch**, **exterior grade or
marine** (interior-grade ply delaminates when it gets wet, which it will), and **built to fit and ready
to install**, which means cut, labelled by window, and stored, before the season. Fasteners must reach
the framing around the opening rather than the siding, so buy screws or lag bolts long enough to
penetrate the studs, and buy them at the same time as the plywood.
«SRC: NOAA National Weather Service, "Hurricane Safety for You and Your Family" one-page safety sheet
— permanent storm shutters offer the best protection for windows; second option is to board up windows
with 5/8 inch exterior grade or marine plywood, built to fit and ready to install; buy supplies before
hurricane season. | https://www.weather.gov/media/owlie/HurricaneSafety-OnePager-07-03-18.pdf | retrieved
2026-07-29»

**All** openings must be protected to earn the full credit in most schemes; a single unprotected
window can void it. That includes doors, skylights, and the garage.

**What this does not do:** taping windows does nothing at all and never has. It does not reduce
breakage and it produces larger shards.

### 74 · Garage door bracing

**Prevents:** the largest opening in your house folding inward.
**Cost:** $120–450 kit; $900–2,400 rated door · **Time:** an afternoon for a kit
· **Materials:** manufacturer-matched kit, or a wind-rated door · **Regions:** Hurricane Coast rank 3,
Tornado Alley rank 7. Owners.

A double garage door is sixteen feet of unsupported sheet metal and the weakest large surface on most
American houses. When it fails inward the wind fills the house, pushes up on the roof deck from
underneath, and turns a wind event into a structural one — the sequence behind many roofs described
as having been "torn off".

Read the label inside the door: a wind-pressure rating there is worth photographing and filing with
Protection 104. No rating means no rating — buy the bracing kit matched to your door's brand and
width, and install the posts and floor brackets while the weather is boring.

credit on its own; only a rated door verified at inspection does. It also does nothing for the
pedestrian door into the house, which is the path the water takes.

### 75 · Soffit and vent sealing

**Prevents:** wind-driven rain and embers entering the attic.
**Cost:** $100–600 · **Time:** a weekend · **Materials:** sealant, fasteners, mesh
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, Wildfire West rank 1 (as 76). Owners.

Soffits are lightweight panels that blow out at pressures far below what the roof withstands, and
once they are gone the attic takes wind-driven rain directly — which is why so many hurricane claims
are ceiling damage in houses with intact roofs. Securing and sealing them is cheap and unglamorous.

The same openings are the ember entry path in a wildfire, which is why this protection and the next
one are usually done together.

**What this does not do:** sealing soffits must not block the ventilation the roof assembly needs.
Seal against water and embers, not against airflow — that is a real distinction and a contractor
question.

### 76 · Ember-resistant vents and Zone 0

**Prevents:** the mechanism that actually burns houses down in a wildfire.
**Cost:** $200–1,200 · **Time:** a weekend · **Materials:** ember-resistant vents, non-combustible
ground cover · **Regions:** Wildfire West rank 1. Owners, and renters for the Zone 0 part.

Houses in wildfires are mostly not consumed by a wall of flame arriving. They are ignited by
**embers**, carried ahead of the fire, landing in vents, gutters, deck gaps, and the first five feet
around the foundation.

Two actions. **Replace attic and crawlspace vents with ember-resistant units**, or fit fine
non-combustible mesh to the specification your state fire authority publishes. And clear **Zone 0** —
the first five feet around the structure — of everything combustible: bark mulch, dead plants,
firewood stacks, door mats, patio furniture cushions, propane tanks, and the recycling bin.

The Zone 0 instruction gets argued with more than any other in this chapter, usually along the lines
that the shrubs by the door are green and watered. IBHS ran **17 controlled experiments** on exactly
that question and the finding was that vegetation within the first five feet **"significantly
increases heat exposure during a wildfire and can amplify damage, even when plants are well-watered."**
Watered is not the same as non-combustible.
«SRC: Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety, "Vegetation in Zone 0: Amplifying Damage to
Structures" — 17 controlled experiments. | https://ibhs.org/wildfire/vegetationinzone0/ |
retrieved 2026-07-29»

California has put the ember-resistant zone into statute rather than guidance. Public Resources Code
§ 4291 requires 100 feet of defensible space around a structure in the state responsibility area, with
**"more intense fuel reductions being utilized between 5 and 30 feet around the structure, and an
ember-resistant zone"** inside five feet — the latter added by AB 3074 (2019–20) and phased in, taking
effect for existing structures three years after it takes effect for new ones. If you are in
California, this is a legal obligation with an inspection regime, not advice.
«SRC: California Public Resources Code § 4291 (defensible space; ember-resistant zone added by AB 3074,
2019–20 Regular Session). |
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PRC&sectionNum=4291 |
retrieved 2026-07-29»

Gutters full of needles are ember collectors. Clean them, and clean under the deck.

**What this does not do:** none of this survives a fire front arriving with a house not otherwise
prepared, and it is not a substitute for evacuating. It changes the probability of ignition, which is
the variable that actually determines whether the house is there afterwards.

### 77 · Defensible space, zones one and two

**Prevents:** the fire reaching the structure with enough energy to ignite it.
**Cost:** $0–2,000 · **Time:** a weekend, then annually · **Materials:** tools, or a contractor
· **Regions:** Wildfire West rank 2. Owners; renters should raise it with the landlord.

Beyond Zone 0, two more bands. The nearer zone is thinned and irrigated where possible, with plants
spaced so fire cannot run between them. The farther zone is thinned rather than cleared, with **ladder
fuels removed** — the low branches and brush that let a ground fire climb into the tree canopy, which
is the transition that makes a fire unstoppable.

Specific distances and requirements are set by state and sometimes by county, and in several states
they are legally mandated with inspections. California is the clearest case and a useful model even if
you live elsewhere: PRC § 4291 requires **100 feet** of defensible space, more intense fuel reduction
**between 5 and 30 feet**, and an **ember-resistant zone within 5 feet.** The same statute separately
requires you to remove any part of a tree extending within **10 feet of a chimney or stovepipe outlet**,
keep plants adjacent to or overhanging the building free of dead or dying wood, and keep the roof clear
of leaves and needles.
«SRC: California Public Resources Code § 4291. |
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=PRC&sectionNum=4291 |
retrieved 2026-07-29»

Look up your own state's version by searching your state fire authority's name and "defensible space."
Where a state has no mandate, the California distances are the best-tested numbers available and there
is no reason to use worse ones.

Do it annually, before fire season, not during it.

**What this does not do:** defensible space protects the structure and gives firefighters somewhere
to stand. It is not a guarantee, and in an extreme wind-driven event it can be overwhelmed.

### 78 · Gutter and downspout capacity, discharged away

**Prevents:** the cheapest water damage there is.
**Cost:** $0–400 · **Time:** an afternoon · **Materials:** extensions, brackets
· **Regions:** all five. Owners and many renters.

Undersized or clogged gutters overflow against the wall and into the foundation, and downspouts that
discharge at the foundation deliver the roof's entire catchment straight to your basement. Extensions
carrying water several feet away from the wall cost very little and stop a startling amount of
damage.

Clean them twice a year. In an ice climate this also reduces ice damming; in a wildfire region it
removes ember fuel. One chore, three protections.

**What this does not do:** gutters manage roof water. They do nothing about groundwater or a rising
water table, which is Protections 79 and 36.

### 79 · Grading and foundation drainage

**Prevents:** water arriving at the house instead of leaving it.
**Cost:** $200–5,000 · **Time:** days · **Materials:** soil, or a contractor
· **Regions:** all five. Owners.

The ground should fall away from the house on every side. Many houses have settled the other way, and
many have a flower bed built up against the wall that acts as a reservoir.

Fix the slope first because it is cheap. Only after that consider the expensive answers — French
drains, exterior waterproofing, a regraded lot — and get a proper assessment before spending, because
this is a category where money disappears without diagnosis.

**What this does not do:** grading handles surface water. It does not stop a rising water table, a
flooded river, or a sewer backup, and no amount of grading makes a floodplain house dry.

### 80 · Flood openings and wet floodproofing

**Prevents:** hydrostatic pressure destroying a foundation wall.
**Cost:** $500–3,000 · **Time:** days · **Materials:** engineered flood vents
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, flood-exposed households. Owners.

This is the protection that reverses most people's instinct. In a flood-prone crawlspace or
enclosure, the correct engineering answer is usually **to let the water in and out** through
engineered openings, rather than to seal it out — because water outside and not inside creates a
pressure differential that can collapse the wall.

**The federal minimum is written into regulation, and it is worth knowing before you talk to anyone.**
Under 44 CFR 60.3(c)(5), an enclosed area below the lowest floor used solely for parking, access or
storage must be designed *"to automatically equalize hydrostatic flood forces on exterior walls by
allowing for the entry and exit of floodwaters."* The design must either be certified by a registered
professional engineer or architect, or meet these minimums verbatim:

> *"A minimum of two openings having a total net area of not less than one square inch for every square
> foot of enclosed area subject to flooding shall be provided. The bottom of all openings shall be no
> higher than one foot above grade. Openings may be equipped with screens, louvers, valves, or other
> coverings or devices provided that they permit the automatic entry and exit of floodwaters."*

Three numbers, and each one is a place installations go wrong: **two openings, not one** (water has to
be able to move through, not just in); **one square inch per square foot** of enclosed floor area, so a
600-square-foot crawlspace needs 600 square inches of net open area; and **no higher than one foot above
grade**, because an opening above the water line does nothing. *Net* area is the free area after the
screen or louver, which is why engineered flood vents are rated and a hole with a grille over it may not
count for what it appears to.

Doing this correctly is also what flood insurance rating requires for enclosures below the flood
elevation, and it can materially change a premium. This is engineering, not a weekend project. Get it
specified properly, and keep the engineer's certificate — it is the document your insurer will ask for.
«SRC: 44 CFR 60.3(c)(5) (NFIP floodplain management criteria) — enclosed areas below the lowest floor
must automatically equalise hydrostatic flood forces; minimum of two openings, total net area not less
than one square inch per square foot of enclosed area, bottom of all openings no higher than one foot
above grade, screens/louvers/valves permitted if they allow automatic entry and exit of floodwaters. |
https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-44/section-60.3 | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**What this does not do:** wet floodproofing accepts water into the enclosure. Everything in there
must be flood-resistant or elevated, which is Protection 81.

### 81 · Elevating mechanicals

**Prevents:** losing the furnace, panel, and water heater to eighteen inches of water.
**Cost:** $500–8,000 · **Time:** days · **Materials:** contractor
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, flood-exposed households. Owners.

In a flooded basement the expensive losses are the mechanical systems — furnace, air handler,
electrical panel, water heater, well equipment — and each one is both costly and slow to replace at a
moment when every contractor in the region is busy.

Raising them above the expected flood level converts a catastrophic loss into a wet floor. Do it when
any of these equipment items is being replaced anyway; the incremental cost then is small.

**What this does not do:** elevating protects the equipment, not the finishes. And it needs a defined
elevation to work against, which means knowing your actual flood risk rather than guessing.

### 82 · Sewer backwater valve

**Prevents:** the municipal sewer discharging into your basement.
**Cost:** $500 at new construction; $2,000–5,000 retrofit · **Time:** days, with excavation
· **Materials:** backwater valve, plumber · **Regions:** Ice & Snow North rank 6, all combined-sewer
cities. Owners.

When a combined sewer surcharges in heavy rain, the excess has to go somewhere, and the somewhere is
the lowest connected fixture in the neighbourhood — which may be your floor drain. What comes up is
sewage, which turns a water-damage claim into a health event and a far larger remediation.

A backwater valve is a one-way gate on the sewer lateral. Retrofitting is expensive because it means
excavating to reach the line, which is why it is dramatically cheaper during any other work on that
line. **Check for a municipal subsidy** — many cities part-fund these because they reduce the city's
own liability, and almost nobody asks.

Water-backup coverage is usually a separate, inexpensive endorsement on a homeowner's policy and is
frequently absent. Check yours in Protection 102.

**What this does not do:** a backwater valve stops flow from the street. It does nothing about
groundwater seepage, which is the sump pump in Protection 36 — different problem, different device,
and houses often need both.

### 83 · Seismic: anchoring what will otherwise fall on you

**Prevents:** the most common earthquake injuries, and the post-quake fire.
**Cost:** $30–200 · **Time:** an afternoon · **Materials:** straps, latches, brackets
· **Regions:** Quake West rank 1. Owners **and renters** — most of this is removable.

The cheapest genuinely life-saving protection in this chapter, and it is a Saturday with a drill.

**Strap the water heater** to the wall studs at two points with proper seismic strapping. A toppled
water heater breaks its gas line and its water line simultaneously, which is a fire and a flood at the
moment the fire service is least able to respond. **Anchor tall furniture** — bookcases, wardrobes,
dressers — to studs. **Latch cabinets**, especially the ones above where people stand and the one
holding the heavy crockery. **Move heavy objects off high shelves**, and get anything heavy off the
wall above a bed.

Then the free part: shoes and a torch at the bedside, per Protection 43, because the floor afterwards
is glass.

**What this does not do:** anchoring does not make an unreinforced building safe. If you live in
unreinforced masonry or a soft-storey structure, that is a retrofit conversation and a much larger
one, and it should be raised with a structural engineer.

### 84 · Seismic: gas flex connectors and automatic shutoff

**Prevents:** the fires that follow the shaking.
**Cost:** $50 per appliance; $250–800 for an automatic valve · **Time:** hours, with a licensed
fitter · **Materials:** flexible connectors, seismic shutoff valve · **Regions:** Quake West rank 2.
Owners.

A large share of post-earthquake destruction is fire, and a large share of that fire comes from
ruptured gas connections at appliances that moved. **Flexible connectors** let an appliance shift
without breaking the line and are inexpensive per appliance.

An **automatic seismic shutoff valve** closes the supply when shaking exceeds a threshold, which
matters because the standard advice — shut the gas off yourself — assumes you are home, conscious, and
able to reach the meter.

Both are licensed-fitter work. Do not touch a gas line yourself.

Know the manual procedure too, and its rule: **shut the gas off only if you smell gas, hear it
escaping, or are told to. Once it is off it stays off until the utility restores it — never relight it
yourself.** Keep the wrench at the meter, per Protection 35.

**What this does not do:** an automatic valve can trigger in an event that did no damage, and
restoring service requires the utility, which may take days in a regional event. That is a real
trade-off and worth knowing before you install one.
