Target: 16 pages · ~5,600 words · draft 1, 2026-07-28

## The six mechanisms

A tornado does not kill anyone. Neither does a hurricane, a flood, a wildfire, or a week without
power. What kills people is one of six physical things happening to a human body, and every one of
those six can be described, predicted, and in most cases interrupted.

This is not a semantic point. It is the most useful idea in this book, and it is the reason the
book is organized the way it is. If you prepare for *hazards*, you end up with a garage full of
equipment and no plan, because there are twenty-three hazards in these pages and you cannot buy
your way through all of them. If you prepare for *mechanisms*, the list collapses. The same head
protection that matters in a tornado matters in an earthquake. The same carbon monoxide alarm that
matters after a hurricane matters after an ice storm. The same thirty-day supply of a prescription
matters in every single chapter of this book.

Six mechanisms. Learn them once and every hazard chapter that follows becomes a variation on
something you already understand.

They are:

1. Trauma — being struck, crushed, or thrown
2. Drowning — water in the airway, usually in a vehicle
3. Thermal failure — the body running too hot or too cold to function
4. Asphyxia and toxic atmosphere — the air itself becoming the problem
5. Medical decompensation — an existing condition losing the support that was holding it steady
6. Delayed care — an injury or illness that was survivable becoming fatal because help could not
   arrive

You will notice something about that list as you read the rest of the chapter. The first two are
what people picture when they imagine dying in a disaster. The last two — medical decompensation and
delayed care — are the ones almost nobody prepares for, and the official counts say they are not the
small category.

The National Hurricane Center keeps direct and indirect deaths in separate columns, and the columns are
instructive. **Katrina: 520 direct deaths and 565 indirect** — the majority of the indirect ones
cardiovascular — plus 307 whose cause was never determined. **Michael: 16 direct deaths and 43
indirect**, from falls during clean-up, traffic accidents, and medical conditions the hurricane made
worse. In both cases the storm killed fewer people than the aftermath of the storm did.
«SRC: National Hurricane Center, Tropical Cyclone Report: Hurricane Katrina (AL122005) — 1,392 total:
520 direct, 565 indirect (318 cardiovascular), 307 indeterminate. |
https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL122005_Katrina.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»
«SRC: National Hurricane Center, Tropical Cyclone Report: Hurricane Michael (AL142018) — 16 direct
deaths; 43 indirect deaths, all in Florida, from post-storm falls, traffic accidents and medical
issues compounded by the hurricane. |
https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/tcr/AL142018_Michael.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Read those two pairs of numbers and the argument of this whole book is already made. The wind and the
water are the part with footage. The dying happens afterwards, quietly, to people whose insulin got
warm or whose oxygen concentrator had no power or who climbed a ladder at sixty-eight with a
chainsaw.

## One · Trauma

Trauma is the mechanism people mean when they say a storm killed someone. It is the wind event
mechanism, the earthquake mechanism, the landslide mechanism. Something moves at speed and a body
is in the way, or something stops moving and a body is underneath it.

Within trauma, the finding to hold onto is not about one body part. It is that being indoors was
not the protection people assumed it was.

CDC reviewed all 338 deaths from the April 2011 outbreak across five southeastern states. Ninety
and a half percent of the injuries happened **indoors** — and 37 percent of the bodies were
recovered outside. Nearly ninety-six percent of the deaths were multisystem trauma, and 21.9
percent of the death certificates specifically noted a head injury. Forty-seven percent of the
dead were in single-family homes and 26.6 percent in mobile homes. Eighty-nine and a half percent
of the deaths came from EF-4 and EF-5 tornadoes.
«SRC: "Tornado-Related Fatalities — Five States, Southeastern United States, April 25–28, 2011."
MMWR Morb Mortal Wkly Rep 2012;61(28):529–533. |
https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/preview/mmwrhtml/mm6128a3.htm | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Read those numbers together and they say something specific. *Multisystem* trauma means the body
was struck in several places at once, which is what happens when a house comes apart around a
person rather than a single object arriving. Debris in a tornado is not one thing — it is a graded
spectrum from grit and glass through roofing material to framing lumber and appliances. A person
crouched in an interior hallway is protected against grit. They are not protected against the
hallway itself. That is why the first protection in this book's tornado order is not a purchase at
all — it is Protection 23, choosing a room whose walls are not going to become the debris.

Protection 25 is a bicycle helmet, and it is here on the strength of about one death in five
carrying a head injury, not on any claim that a helmet is the difference between living and dying.
CDC is unusually direct about this and it is worth quoting: *"Currently, no data are available
regarding the effectiveness of helmet use to prevent head injuries during a tornado,"* and
*"choosing to use helmets to protect the head should not be considered an alternative to seeking
appropriate shelter."* CDC still recommends keeping helmets and blankets in the safe room. So does
this book — as a fifteen-dollar addition to a good room, never as a substitute for one, and kept
*in* the room, because a helmet you have to go and find is a helmet you will not be wearing.

The second thing about trauma is that most of it is delivered by the building you are in, not by
the storm. Wind does not usually pick up a house and carry it away. It finds an opening, enters,
pressurizes the interior, and pushes the roof off from underneath while pulling it from above. The
sequence starts at the weakest large opening, and on most American houses that is the garage door —
sixteen feet of unsupported sheet metal, which is why Protection 74 exists and why it is ranked so
high on the Hurricane Coast list. Once the envelope is breached, the failure cascades: roof deck,
then wall connections, then the structure. Everything that then injures you is material that used
to be your house.

This has a practical consequence that runs through every wind chapter. The protections that reduce
trauma most are not the ones that protect *you*. They are the ones that keep the building intact —
sealed roof deck, roof-to-wall connections, opening protection, garage bracing. You cannot armor a
person effectively against a collapsing structure. You can keep the structure from collapsing.

**Where trauma is the leading mechanism:** tornado, hail and derecho, earthquake, hurricane wind,
landslide and debris flow, and the aftermath of every one of them, when people walk into a
destroyed building in the dark wearing the shoes they sleep in.

**What changes the outcome:** the room you chose in advance (23), a helmet (25), shoes and a light
where you sleep (43), a building envelope that holds (69, 72, 73, 74), and anchoring the heavy
objects that will otherwise come down on you in a quake (83).

## Two · Drowning

Water is the deadliest thing in this book, and the way it kills is almost always the same, and
almost never the way people expect.

More deaths occur due to flooding than from any other thunderstorm-related hazard, and **over half
of all flood-related drownings happen when a vehicle is driven into floodwater.**
«SRC: NWS, "Turn Around Don't Drown" — 6 inches of fast-moving water knocks over an adult; 12 inches carries away most cars; 2 feet carries away SUVs and trucks. Cites CDC that over half of all flood-related drownings occur when a vehicle is driven into floodwater, with walking into or near floodwater the next largest share. https://www.weather.gov/safety/flood-turn-around-dont-drown | retrieved 2026-07-29» That single sentence should reframe how you think about flood risk. Most people
picture rising water in a living room. The reality is a car, at night, on a familiar road, and a
driver who could not see that the road surface was gone.

The physics is unforgiving and worth knowing precisely. Moving water exerts force proportional to
the square of its velocity, which means water that feels manageable when you step into it is
dramatically stronger when it is moving. **Twelve inches of rushing water carries away most cars, and two feet carries away SUVs
and trucks** — and once a vehicle floats, the driver has no control of it at all. It is no longer a car.
It is a boat with the buoyancy of a car, which is to say briefly.
«SRC: NWS, "Turn Around Don't Drown" — 6 in of fast-moving water knocks over an adult, 12 in carries away most cars, 2 ft carries away SUVs and trucks. https://www.weather.gov/safety/flood-turn-around-dont-drown | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Two things make this mechanism so lethal. The first is darkness: you cannot judge depth on a
flooded road at night, and the road you cross every day gives you a false confidence that a
stranger would not have. The second is that flash flooding is fast in a way that does not match
human intuition. The rain that produces it may be falling somewhere you cannot see, on ground that
cannot absorb it — a burn scar, a paved suburb, a canyon — and arrive at you as a wall rather than
a rise.

This is why the extreme rainfall chapter is separate from the riverine flooding chapter. They are
different mechanisms of arrival with different warning times and different decisions. Riverine
flooding usually gives you a forecast and a gauge to watch. Flash flooding gives you minutes.

There is a second drowning pattern that deserves naming: storm surge. Surge is not rain and it is
not a river. It is the ocean being pushed inland as a mass, and it is the reason hurricane
evacuation orders are drawn as zones rather than as distances from the coast. People who die in
surge overwhelmingly die because they stayed, and people who stayed overwhelmingly did so for
reasons that made sense to them at the time — the last storm was fine, the traffic looked worse
than the water, the dog, the house, the job.

That last point matters more than any statistic. Nobody drowns because they were told water is
dangerous and did not believe it. They drown because leaving had a cost and the water had not
arrived yet.

**Where drowning is the leading mechanism:** extreme rainfall and flash flood, riverine and coastal
flooding, hurricane storm surge, tsunami.

**What changes the outcome:** a leave-early trigger written down before the season (95), two
evacuation routes chosen when you were calm (106), knowing your evacuation zone rather than your
distance from the water (91), never entering moving water on foot or in a vehicle (115), and a
window breaker in the car for the case where you got it wrong.

## Three · Thermal failure

The body maintains a narrow internal temperature range and spends energy constantly to do it. When
the environment wins, function degrades in a predictable order: judgment first, then coordination,
then consciousness, then organ function. The judgment-first part is what makes this mechanism so
dangerous — by the time you are in trouble, the faculty you would use to recognize it is the
faculty you have already lost.

Heat is, over the long run, the deadliest weather hazard in the United States, and it kills with
almost no drama. The Weather Service keeps the tally, and the thirty-year annual averages are not
close:

| Hazard | 30-year annual average US deaths | 10-year annual average |
|---|---|---|
| **Heat** | **180** | **279** |
| Flood | 94 | 118 |
| Tornado | 73 | 51 |
| Hurricane | 50 | 25 |
| Winter | 33 | 27 |
| Lightning | 31 | 19 |
| Cold | 30 | 35 |
| **All weather** | **619** | **658** |

«SRC: National Weather Service, Office of Climate, Water and Weather Services, "Natural Hazard
Statistics: 81-Year List of Severe Weather Fatalities" (1940–2025), 10-year and 30-year average rows. |
https://www.weather.gov/media/hazstat/81year_2025.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Two things in that table deserve attention. Heat is the largest single category by roughly a factor of
two over the next one. And heat is the only major category where the recent ten-year average is
dramatically *higher* than the thirty-year average — 279 against 180 — while tornado, hurricane and
lightning deaths have all fallen. Warning systems and building codes have been winning against the
dramatic hazards. Nothing has been winning against the quiet one.

There is no
footage of it. Heat deaths happen indoors, alone, to people who were old or ill or poor or all
three, in apartments without functioning air conditioning, over several days. The victim usually
does not call anyone. Heat waves also frequently coincide with grid strain, which is why the
extreme heat chapter and the grid failure chapter cross-reference each other so heavily — the
compound event, heat plus no power, is far more dangerous than either alone.

Cold works the other direction and on a longer clock. A house without heat in a hard freeze does
not become dangerous in an hour; it becomes dangerous over a night and a day, and the people it
takes are again the old, the ill, and the ones living alone. Cold also introduces a mechanism that
heat does not: it breaks the building. Pipes freeze, burst, and thaw into water damage, and the
resulting scramble for warmth is where the next mechanism — carbon monoxide — comes in.

There is a specific and correctable failure in both directions: people try to condition the whole
house. Do not. Consolidate. One room, chosen for the fewest exterior walls and the smallest volume,
with the household in it, is the single highest-return thermal decision in an outage. In cold, that
means Protection 26 and a sleeping bag with a real temperature rating rather than a decorative one.
In heat, it means Protection 29 and identifying, in advance, the coolest room in the house and the
nearest public building with reliable air conditioning.

The other correctable failure is dehydration, which is upstream of every heat illness and is
trivially preventable right up until the moment it is not. Heat illness is a progression, not an
event: heat cramps, then heat exhaustion, then heat stroke, which is a medical emergency with a
mortality that rises with every minute of delay. Learning to recognize the transition — confusion,
loss of sweating, altered behavior — is Protection 64, and it is free.

**Where thermal failure is the leading mechanism:** extreme heat, extreme cold and deep freeze,
blizzard and ice storm, and any long grid failure in either season.

**What changes the outcome:** a stored water supply and the discipline to drink it (1, 2), one
consolidated room (26, 29), a real sleeping-bag rating (28), knowing where the nearest cooled or
heated public building is, and recognizing the progression early (63, 64).

## Four · Asphyxia and toxic atmosphere

This mechanism is the one that turns a survived disaster into a fatal one, and it does it during
the recovery, in the dark, to people who did everything else right.

Carbon monoxide is the central case. It is colourless, odourless, and produced by every fuel-burning
engine and appliance, which means it is produced by exactly the equipment people reach for when the
power is out: the generator, the charcoal grill, the camp stove, the vehicle idling in the garage
for warmth or for a phone charge. It binds to haemoglobin far more readily than oxygen does, so a
concentration that seems trivial displaces a large fraction of your blood's oxygen-carrying
capacity. The early symptoms — headache, nausea, fatigue, confusion — are indistinguishable from
the ordinary exhaustion of a bad week after a storm, and they arrive with the judgment impairment
that stops you from acting on them. People go to sleep and do not wake up. Whole families.
CDC's rules on the equipment that produces it are stated as absolutes, and this book repeats them as
absolutes: **never use a generator inside a home or garage, "even if doors and windows are open"**;
operate one **outdoors, more than 20 feet from any window, door or vent**; and when running one, **use a
battery-powered or battery-backup CO detector in the house.**
«SRC: CDC, "Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics" — never use a generator inside a home or garage, even
with doors and windows open; operate generators outdoors more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents;
use a battery-powered or battery-backup CO detector. |
https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Note the specific number, because "outside" is where people think they already are. Twenty feet. A
generator on a porch, in a breezeway, under an open window, or just inside an open garage door is not
outside for this purpose.

The rules are simple, absolute, and they are stated as absolutes in this book on purpose:

A generator runs outdoors. Not in the garage. Not in the garage with the door open. Not on the
porch, not under an overhang, not by an open window. Outdoors and well clear of the building, with
the exhaust pointed away from it. Nothing that burns fuel is ever operated indoors for heat —
not a grill, not a camp stove, not an oven with its door open. And every home has working carbon
monoxide alarms with in-date sensors, because CO alarms expire and almost nobody checks. Those are
Protections 32 and 47, and between them they cost less than a tank of fuel.

Smoke is the second case, and it has broadened enormously in the last few years. It is no longer
only a problem for people near a fire. Wildfire smoke travels across the continent, and a fire in
Canada can put a city a thousand miles away into unhealthy air for a week. Fine particulate matter
penetrates deep into the lungs and enters the bloodstream, and the people it hurts first are the
ones with asthma, COPD, and cardiac disease. This is genuinely one of the highest-leverage chapters
in the book, because the mitigation is so cheap: a better furnace filter, the HVAC set to
recirculate, one room sealed, and a box fan with filters taped to it — a build that costs the price
of a takeaway meal and measurably cleans the air in a room. Protections 49, 50, 51, and 52.

Volcanic ash and post-collapse dust belong here too. Both are respiratory hazards that a cloth mask
does nothing for, and both are reasons the N95 supply in Protection 52 is on the list rather than
in the nice-to-have pile.

**Where asphyxia and toxic atmosphere is the leading mechanism:** grid failure, extreme cold,
wildfire smoke and air quality, hazmat released by a disaster, volcanic ashfall, and the aftermath
of every wind event where people run generators.

**What changes the outcome:** generator siting (32), in-date CO alarms (47), never burning fuel
indoors (16), filtration and one sealed room (49, 50, 51), and N95s you already own (52).

## Five · Medical decompensation

Here the book departs from what most preparedness writing covers, and here is where most modern
American disaster deaths actually occur.

A large fraction of the population is alive because of something that requires continuous support.
Insulin that requires refrigeration. Oxygen that requires electricity. Dialysis that requires a
functioning clinic, a road to reach it, and staff who could get there themselves. A cardiac
medication that must be taken daily. An electric wheelchair. A CPAP. Nebulized medication. None of
these are exotic; collectively they describe an enormous number of households, including many that
do not think of themselves as medically fragile.

Take away the power, the road, or the pharmacy, and none of these people are injured by the storm.
They decompensate. And it does not happen at the peak of the event when attention is highest — it
happens on day three, day five, day nine, after the news trucks have gone and the official death
toll has been published and everyone believes the disaster is over.

This is why the mortality of a major disaster is so often revised upward months afterward, and revised
upward by a lot. The direct toll counts people killed by wind and water. The full toll counts everyone
who died who would not otherwise have died — and the second number is frequently a multiple of the first.

You do not need a contested study to see it; the National Hurricane Center's own columns show it.
**Katrina: 520 direct deaths, and a total of 1,392.** **Michael: 16 direct deaths, and 59 in total.**
Between two and nearly four times the headline figure, in the official record, from the agency that
writes the headline figure.

The protections here are unglamorous and enormously effective:

A thirty-day buffer on every prescription in the house, which requires knowing your state's
emergency refill law — many states allow an early refill under a declared emergency, and almost
nobody knows this before they need it. That is Protection 59. A written plan for refrigerated
medication in an outage, because insulin has real thermal tolerances and guessing is not a plan
(60). Enrollment in your utility's medical registry, which exists in most service territories and
which prioritizes restoration and provides notification for households with life-sustaining
equipment (42). A contingency for dialysis and oxygen that includes the phone number of a second
clinic in a different direction from the first (61). And a power priority list written in advance,
so that when you have a limited battery you already know that the oxygen concentrator outranks the
refrigerator, which outranks the phone, which outranks everything else (37).

If you take one thing from this chapter and apply it tonight, make it the prescription buffer. It
is the highest ratio of lives saved to effort expended anywhere in this book.

**Where medical decompensation is the leading mechanism:** every long-duration event — grid
failure, hurricane aftermath, extreme heat, extreme cold, ice storm, wildfire evacuation — and it is
the dominant mechanism in nearly all of them once you count beyond the first forty-eight hours.

**What changes the outcome:** the prescription buffer and your state's refill law (59), the
refrigerated-medication plan (60), the utility medical registry (42), a second clinic in a different
direction (61), and a power priority list (37).

## Six · Delayed care

The last mechanism is not a thing that happens to a body. It is a thing that fails to happen.

An emergency medical system works because of assumptions: that a call connects, that a vehicle can
reach you, that a hospital has capacity, that the road exists. A disaster invalidates all four at
once, and it does so precisely when demand spikes. Roads are blocked or flooded. Cell networks are
congested or down. The hospital is on generator power, or is itself damaged, or is full. Crews are
responding to hundreds of calls in a system built for dozens, and some of them cannot get to work
because their own neighborhoods are cut off.

The result is that survivable becomes fatal. A bleeding wound that would be nothing on an ordinary
Tuesday is life-threatening if nobody arrives for six hours. A cardiac event with a twenty-minute
response time is a different event with a two-hour response time. A broken leg becomes a crisis if
the person is alone and cannot move.

There is a hard idea in this and it is better to say it plainly than to imply it: for some period
after a serious disaster, in your area, you may be the highest level of medical care available to
the people in your house. Not as a philosophy. As an operational fact, for some number of hours.

That is not an argument for playing doctor, and this book will not teach you to. It is an argument
for a very short list of specific, teachable interventions that buy time, and for knowing the
boundary of your own competence precisely:

Bleeding control. This is the single highest-value skill for a layperson, because severe bleeding
kills in minutes and is often stoppable by direct pressure or a tourniquet applied by someone with
no medical background. Stop the Bleed training is widely available and frequently free, and it is
Protection 58. The kit that goes with it is 57, and it lives where you can reach it in the dark
rather than in a cupboard.

CPR. Same argument, same availability.

Everything else is triage and transport decisions: recognizing what cannot wait, knowing which
hospital is in which direction, understanding that the nearest facility may be the wrong one, and
accepting that self-transport is sometimes correct and sometimes catastrophic.

And then the boundary. This book contains no medication dosing, no suturing, no advanced airway
management, no instructions for reducing fractures. Not because those things do not matter, but
because a paragraph of confidence about them is worse than nothing, and because the failure mode of
an amateur attempting them is a person who dies of the intervention rather than the injury.

**Where delayed care is the leading mechanism:** the aftermath of every hazard in this book,
proportional to how many people were hurt at once and how thoroughly the roads were cut.

**What changes the outcome:** a trauma kit within reach (57), bleeding control and CPR training
(58), the medical realities of isolation understood in advance (67), and a household that knows
where the second hospital is.

## Who the six take

There is a pattern in who dies in American disasters, and it is consistent enough across hazards
that it is worth stating directly, because it changes what you should do.

The mechanisms do not select randomly. They select for isolation, for age, for dependence, and for
housing.

**Living alone** runs through every one of the back-loaded mechanisms. CDC's own list of who is at
elevated risk of health harm from heat names **people who are socially isolated** alongside adults over
65, people with chronic conditions, people with disabilities, and people who lack access to cooling —
which is to say the risk factors this book keeps returning to are the ones the clinical guidance already
lists.
«SRC: CDC, "Clinical Overview of Heat" — risk factors for health harms from heat. |
https://www.cdc.gov/heat-health/hcp/clinical-overview/index.html | retrieved 2026-07-29»

The mechanism behind that is not mysterious. Every one of these failures begins with impaired judgment,
and a household of one has nobody present to notice it. The person does not decide not to call for
help. They stop being able to recognize that they should. This is why the check-in agreement in the
extreme heat playbook — a specific named person contacting a specific isolated person daily, with a
physical visit if there is no answer — is free, unglamorous, and one of the highest-value actions in
the book.

**Age** compounds it. Thermoregulation becomes less efficient, thirst signaling becomes less
reliable, medication dependence rises, and mobility falls — which means the same house at the same
temperature is a materially different hazard for a person of eighty than for a person of forty.

**Dependence on equipment or medication** is the factor that has grown fastest and is least
accounted for in preparedness writing. It is the whole subject of the fifth mechanism.

**Housing type** determines the front-loaded mechanisms almost entirely. Manufactured and mobile
homes are the clearest case: in a violent wind event no room inside one is survivable, which is why
the tornado chapter tells those readers to leave on the watch and not the warning, and why that
instruction appears before any advice about interior rooms. Renters are systematically less prepared
than owners, not through carelessness but because most hardening protections require permission they
do not have — which is why this book flags, protection by protection, which ones work in a rental.
Upper-floor apartments face heat differently. Basements face water differently.

**Income** cuts across all of it, and the honest version of that sentence is uncomfortable: a
meaningful share of the protections in this book cost money, and the households most exposed to
every one of the six mechanisms are the ones least able to buy their way out. That is precisely why
the sixty-day sequence front-loads the free week, and why the protections are individually priced
with a time cost as well as a dollar cost. A reader with no budget can still do the region list, the
document vault, the video inventory, the communication plan, the alert enrollment, the trigger
thresholds, the room selection, and the prescription buffer. That is not a consolation prize. Those
are among the highest-value items in the entire book, and they cost an evening each.

The practical instruction is this: **run the six mechanisms against the actual people in your
household, not against an average family.** Who lives alone. Who is over seventy. Who needs power
for something. Who cannot walk out. Who is in a mobile home. The answers determine which chapters
matter most to you, and they matter far more than which state you live in.

## When mechanisms combine

The last thing to understand before the clock is that these six rarely arrive one at a time. The
most dangerous situations in this book are compounds, and each one is more than the sum of its
parts.

**Heat plus grid failure.** The deadliest realistic scenario in the modern United States is not an
exotic one. It is a multi-day heat wave that takes the grid down with it — demand spikes, equipment
fails, and air conditioning stops for a large number of people at the same time as the outdoor
temperature makes indoor air lethal over hours. Thermal failure and medical decompensation arrive
together, and the cooling centers fill.

**Freeze plus generator.** A hard freeze knocks out power, the house gets cold, and people bring
combustion indoors. Thermal failure creates the pressure and toxic atmosphere collects the bill.
This pairing is why the carbon monoxide rules in this book are stated as absolutes with no
exceptions — because the exception is always invented at two in the morning by a cold, tired person
with impaired judgment.

**Rain plus sewer.** Extreme rainfall overwhelms combined sewers and the water that enters the house
is contaminated rather than clean, which converts a property-damage event into a health event and a
much larger insurance question.

**Wildfire plus wind, then rain.** Wind drives the fire, the fire strips the slope, and the first
heavy rain on the burn scar produces debris flow — which is why landslide does not have its own
chapter in this book but does appear as the aftermath section of the wildfire chapter, where the
action actually exists.

**Any event plus delayed care.** This is the universal multiplier. Every mechanism becomes more
lethal in proportion to how thoroughly the roads are cut and how many people needed help at once.

The design response to compounds is the reason this book has seven cross-cutting chapters rather
than folding them into the hazards. Grid failure, systems dark, water and sanitation failure,
electronics and records, hazmat, radiological, and medical care when EMS cannot reach you are not
hazards in their own right. They are the things that arrive *with* the hazards and turn a bad day
into a bad month.

## What this means for the rest of the book

Read the six again and notice how they distribute.

Trauma and drowning are front-loaded. They happen during the event, in minutes, and the decisions
that govern them have to be made before the warning — which room, which route, which trigger. There
is no time to think, so the thinking is done in advance. That is what the safe room, the leave-early
trigger, and the written thresholds in Protection 95 are for.

Thermal failure, toxic atmosphere, medical decompensation, and delayed care are back-loaded. They
unfold over days, in a degraded environment, while everyone is exhausted and the official emergency
is considered over. They are governed by supplies, by plans on paper, and by having asked one
boring question early: what in this house depends on something that will not be there?

The hazard chapters in Part Three all follow the same five beats — the event, what kills and what
costs, before, during, after — and you will now recognize the mechanisms inside each of them. A
tornado chapter is mostly trauma with a tail of delayed care. A hurricane chapter is surge drowning
and wind trauma at the front, then a long tail of every back-loaded mechanism at once. An extreme
heat chapter is thermal failure and medical decompensation almost exclusively. A wildfire chapter is
evacuation timing, then smoke, then months of air quality.

Same six mechanisms. Different mixes.

The next chapter puts them on a clock — because knowing *what* kills is only half of it, and the
other half is knowing *when*, which turns out to be the part almost everyone gets wrong.
