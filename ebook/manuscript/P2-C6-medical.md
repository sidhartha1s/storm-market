# Part Two, Chapter Six — Medical

Protections 57–68 · Target: 12 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-28

**Boundary declared once, and held for the whole chapter:** this chapter contains no medication
dosing, no suturing, no advanced airway management, and no instructions for reducing fractures. Not
because those things do not matter, but because a paragraph of confidence about them is worse than
nothing, and because the failure mode of an amateur attempting them is a person who dies of the
intervention rather than the injury.

---

## Medical

This is the chapter that separates a preparedness book from a shopping list.

The most likely medical crisis in your household during a disaster is not a dramatic injury. It is a
prescription running out, a refrigerated medication spoiling, a dialysis appointment that cannot be
reached, or an oxygen concentrator with a dead battery. That is the fifth mechanism from Part One, and
it is what kills people in the week after the news trucks leave.

So this chapter is ordered by likelihood, not by drama. The trauma kit is first only because it is the
one thing with a clock measured in minutes.

---

### 57 · The trauma kit, and where it lives

**Prevents:** bleeding to death in the window before EMS can reach you.
**Cost:** $40–120 · **Time:** an hour to assemble · **Materials:** tourniquet, pressure dressing,
gauze, gloves, shears · **Regions:** all five. Rank 5 Quake West.

Severe bleeding kills in minutes and is frequently stoppable by a person with no medical background.
That combination — fast, fatal, and fixable by you — is what makes this the highest-value medical item
in the house.

The kit is small and specific: a **commercial tourniquet** from a recognized design rather than an
improvised strap or a novelty copy, a **pressure dressing**, **haemostatic gauze**, plain gauze, gloves,
trauma shears, and tape. Buy from a reputable supplier, because the counterfeit tourniquet market is
real and a tourniquet that fails under load is worse than none because of the time it costs.

**Where it lives is half the protection.** One kit with the safe room, one in each vehicle. Not in a
bathroom cabinet behind the shampoo. Everyone in the house knows the location, and nothing is stored on
top of it.

Then the ordinary supplies, separately: adhesive dressings, antiseptic, burn dressings, an elastic
bandage, tweezers, a thermometer, and a two-week supply of the over-the-counter medications your
household actually uses — pain relief, antihistamine, anti-diarrhoeal, rehydration salts.
Rehydration salts matter more than people expect in both heat events and gastrointestinal illness.

**What this does not do:** owning a tourniquet is not knowing how to use one. Applied wrongly it does
not stop the bleeding and it costs the minutes that mattered. That is Protection 58, and the kit is
close to worthless without it.

### 58 · Bleeding control and CPR training

**Prevents:** the kit being useless in the hands that own it.
**Cost:** $0–80 · **Time:** two to four hours, once · **Materials:** a course
· **Regions:** all five. Rank 6 Quake West.

Two courses. **Stop the Bleed** is run by the American College of Surgeons — the original US Department
of Defense STOP THE BLEED partner — and it teaches **three basic actions to control severe bleeding**:
direct pressure, wound packing, and a tourniquet. More than five million people have taken it. Courses
are found through the program's own directory and are frequently free; kits are sold separately and
the course does not require you to buy one.
«SRC: American College of Surgeons, ACS Stop the Bleed — "Bleeding Is the #1 Cause of Preventable Death
after Injury"; three basic actions; original US DoD STOP THE BLEED partner; more than 5 million people
trained. | https://www.stopthebleed.org/ | retrieved 2026-07-29» **CPR**
is equally available and equally worth an afternoon.

Take them with the whole household — everyone over about twelve. The point is redundancy: the injured
person may be the one who took the course.

Then rehearse the location. A drill where somebody says "trauma kit" and someone else produces it in
under thirty seconds is worth more than a second course.

**What this does not do:** these courses cover the two interventions that are fast, teachable, and
decisive. They do not make you a medic, and this book is not going to pretend that a chapter can.

### 59 · The thirty-day prescription buffer, and your state's refill law

**Prevents:** the single most likely medical failure in a disaster.
**Cost:** copays · **Time:** two phone calls · **Materials:** the prescriptions you already have
· **Regions:** all five. **The highest ratio of lives saved to effort in this book.**

If you do one thing from this chapter, do this.

Aim for a thirty-day buffer on every prescription in the household. Getting there is a conversation
with your prescriber and your pharmacist, not a trick: ask for a ninety-day supply where the
medication and the insurance allow it, and ask specifically about an emergency supply provision.

Then learn the thing almost nobody knows: **many states have a law allowing an early or emergency
refill under a declared emergency.** Florida's is the clearest and worth reading as a model, because it
tells you exactly what to say. Fla. Stat. § 252.358 requires insurers and managed care organizations to
**waive time restrictions on refills — including suspending the electronic "refill too soon" edits sent
to pharmacies — and to authorize payment for at least a 30-day supply regardless of when the
prescription was last filled**, when the person's county is under a **hurricane warning from the
National Weather Service**, or under a **state of emergency declared by the Governor**, or has
**activated its emergency operations center and emergency management plan.** The request has to be made
within 30 days of that condition starting, and there must be authorized refills remaining.
«SRC: Florida Statutes § 252.358, "Emergency-preparedness prescription medication refills." |
https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2024/252.358 | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Note the mechanism, because it is the useful part: the barrier is usually not the pharmacist's judgment
but an automated insurance rejection, and the statute works by switching that rejection off. So the
sentence at the counter is not "please can I have it early" — it is *"my county is under a hurricane
warning; the refill-too-soon edit should be waived."*

**Your state's version is different, and the differences matter** — how many days, which medications,
whether a declaration is required. Yours is in the Appendix B directory entry for your state.

Keep a written list of every medication, dose, prescriber, and pharmacy in the document vault, because
an out-of-area pharmacist can often help a displaced patient who has the list and cannot help one who
does not.

**If you take a blood thinner.** Put it on the list by name: warfarin, or whichever one you take. If
you fall and hit your head while on it, get checked, even if you feel fine. Call your provider, or 911
if no one answers. Also ask your doctor now: "If a long outage means I cannot get my blood test, what
do I do?" Write the answer on the list.
«SRC: MedlinePlus (US National Library of Medicine), "Taking warfarin" (n.d.) — contact your provider
if you have a serious fall or if you hit your head, or have headache, dizziness or weakness; avoid
walking on ice, wet floors or other slippery surfaces. |
https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000292.htm | retrieved 2026-10-27»

**Controlled substances are the hard case** and are frequently excluded from emergency provisions. If
your household depends on one, ask now rather than at the counter during an evacuation.

**What this does not do:** a buffer is a buffer. It does not solve a multi-week displacement, and it
does nothing for medication requiring refrigeration — that is Protection 60.

### 60 · The refrigerated medication plan

**Prevents:** insulin, and everything like it, spoiling silently.
**Cost:** $0–60 · **Time:** one phone call plus a cooler · **Materials:** cooler, frozen bottles,
thermometer · **Regions:** all five.

Refrigerated medications have real thermal tolerances, in both directions, and the guidance is
medication-specific. **Some are damaged by freezing as well as by warmth**, which is why "keep it cold"
is not a plan and a bag of ice can destroy a month's supply.

Do three things. **Ask the pharmacist** for the specific storage range and the manufacturer's guidance
on how long the medication remains usable at room temperature — for several common medications this
period is longer than people assume, and knowing the number prevents both spoilage and panic. **Put a
thermometer in the cooler**, so the decision is a reading rather than a guess. And **pre-freeze water
bottles** rather than relying on ice you will not be able to buy.

Write the storage range and the room-temperature window on the form in Part Six. In an emergency the
household needs to read it, not remember it.

**If you use insulin.** Insulin out of the fridge is not ruined in hours. The FDA says insulin in its
original vial or cartridge may be left unrefrigerated between 59°F and 86°F for up to 28 days. Some
products differ, so ask your pharmacist about yours. Never use insulin that has frozen. Keep it out of
direct sun and away from heat. If you use ice, do not let it touch the insulin.
«SRC: US Food and Drug Administration, "Information Regarding Insulin Storage and Switching Between
Products in an Emergency" (n.d.) — insulin in the manufacturer's vial or cartridge "may be left
unrefrigerated at a temperature between 59°F and 86°F for up to 28 days"; do not use insulin that has
been frozen; if using ice, avoid freezing the insulin; keep it away from direct heat and out of direct
sunlight; some products (e.g. Awiqli) have different limits. |
https://www.fda.gov/drugs/emergency-preparedness-drugs/information-regarding-insulin-storage-and-switching-between-products-emergency
| retrieved 2026-10-27»

**What this does not do:** a cooler is a delay measured in days. For a long displacement the answer is
a pharmacy or clinic with power, and the trigger for going there belongs in Protection 95.

### 61 · Dialysis, oxygen, and infusion contingency

**Prevents:** a treatment schedule failing because one road closed.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** an hour of calls · **Materials:** phone numbers on paper
· **Regions:** all five.

For a household on scheduled treatment, the disaster plan is a clinic plan.

Call your clinic and ask what their emergency protocol is, whether they have generator power, and
where their patients are directed if they close. Then identify a **second clinic in a different
direction** — not the next nearest, which will likely be cut off by the same flooded road or the same
downed trees. Write both on paper.

For oxygen: know the concentrator's battery runtime, whether cylinders are available as backup, how
many you can keep, and what the supplier's emergency delivery policy is. Cylinders are the outage
answer; concentrators are the electricity answer.

**If you use oxygen.** Ask your supplier two questions and write both answers on paper. "How many
hours does one full tank last at my usual flow rate?" And: "How many hours at the higher rate I use
when I am short of breath?" Carry enough for the whole drive, plus a long delay in traffic. A
concentrator needs a backup tank for when the power goes out. In the car, stand the tank upright on
the floor or the seat and hold it with a seat belt. Keep a window open a crack. Nobody smokes in the
car, and the oxygen stays far from any open flame, including a gas stove.
«SRC: American Lung Association, "Traveling with Oxygen" (n.d.) — in a car, place the oxygen unit
upright on the floor or the seat beside you and secure it with a seat belt; do not smoke or let anyone
else smoke in the car; keep the windows open at least a crack; do not leave oxygen units in a hot car. |
https://www.lung.org/lung-health-diseases/lung-procedures-and-tests/oxygen-therapy/traveling-with-oxygen
| retrieved 2026-10-27»
«SRC: MedlinePlus Medical Encyclopedia (US National Library of Medicine), "Using oxygen at home" (n.d.)
— do not smoke when using oxygen; keep oxygen far away from open fire, like a gas stove, or any other
heating source; concentrator users must have a backup tank of oxygen gas in case the power goes out. |
https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000048.htm | retrieved 2026-10-27»

**If you use a CPAP.** Find the watts printed on the label of the power brick. Multiply by the hours
you sleep. That gives the watt-hours your battery must hold, as in Protection 38. Then add margin.
Here is the arithmetic with a made-up label: **if your label says 60 watts** and you sleep 8 hours,
that is 60 × 8 = 480 watt-hours. Use your own label, not this example. On battery, turn the humidifier
off. It draws a lot of power, and the manufacturer says to switch it off to make the battery last.
«SRC: ResMed, "AirSense 10 with HumidAir — User Guide" (n.d.), Traveling section — "If you are using
an external battery, you should turn off the humidifier in order to maximize the life of your battery.
Do this by turning the Humidity Level to Off." |
https://document.resmed.com/documents/products/machine/airsense-series/user-guide/airsense-10-device-with-humidifier_user-guide_amer_eng.pdf
| retrieved 2026-10-27»

For infusion and other scheduled therapy, ask what the maximum safe delay is. That number determines
whether you shelter or evacuate, and it is a clinical question with a real answer.

**What this does not do:** planning does not create capacity. In a regional event every clinic is
strained at once, and the households that do best are the ones that called on day one rather than
day four.

### 62 · Burn care basics

**Prevents:** making a burn worse in the first two minutes.
**Cost:** $10–25 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** burn dressings, cling film
· **Regions:** all five — burns rise sharply in outages, from candles, stoves, and generators.

**The first decision is minor or major, and the two get opposite treatment. If you are not sure, treat
it as major.**

**Minor burn, skin unbroken:** run **cool water** over it or soak it in a cool water bath — *not* ice
water — and **keep it under water for 5 to 30 minutes.** Then cover with a dry sterile bandage or clean
dressing, and protect it from pressure and friction. Remove jewellery and constricting clothing early,
before swelling. Once it has cooled, moisturiser with aloe helps; if the skin is broken an ointment is
preferred, because it stops the dressing sticking.

**Major burn:** call 911 first. Then — and this is the counter-intuitive one, so read it twice —
**do not put a severe burn in cold water; it can cause shock.** Do not apply oil, butter, ice,
medicines, cream or any household remedy. Do not breathe, blow or cough on it. Do not disturb blistered
or dead skin. Do not remove clothing that is stuck to the skin. Do not give anything by mouth. And if
there is an airway burn, **do not put a pillow under the head** — it can close the airway.
«SRC: MedlinePlus Medical Encyclopedia (US National Library of Medicine), "Burns" — first aid for minor
and major burns, and the DO NOT list. | https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000030.htm |
retrieved 2026-07-29»

Know what needs care you cannot give: burns that are large, deep, circumferential, or on the face,
hands, feet, or genitals, and any burn on an infant or an elderly person. Airway burns from smoke or
steam are an emergency regardless of how the skin looks.

**What this does not do:** this is a stabilization paragraph, not treatment. Serious burns need a
hospital, and this section exists to keep the first two minutes from making that worse.

### 63 · Hypothermia recognition and rewarming

**Prevents:** missing it, because the person cannot tell you.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** blankets, dry clothing, warm sweet drinks
· **Regions:** Ice & Snow North, Quake West, all cold-climate households.

Hypothermia in an unheated house is slow and quiet, and the early signs are behavioural: shivering,
clumsiness, slurred speech, confusion, and apathy. **Judgment fails before the body does**, so the
person will not report it and may insist they are fine.

Get them out of wet clothing, into dry layers, insulated from the floor, and into the consolidated
warm room. Warm sweet drinks if they are fully alert and swallowing safely. Skin-to-skin contact under
blankets works. Warm the core and the trunk, not the extremities.

Handle a severely hypothermic person **gently.** The clinical guidance is explicit on both halves of
this: someone severely hypothermic should be moved out of the cold **"with as little exertion as
possible,"** because exertion shunts warmth from the core out to the muscles — and **"do not use direct
heat (such as hot water, a heating pad, or a heat lamp) to warm the person."** Warm compresses go to the
**neck, chest wall and groin.** Cover the head and neck. Get wet or tight clothing off and dry, loose
clothing on. No alcohol.

Two more instructions from the same source that matter in a disaster: **call 911 for any suspected
hypothermia**, especially if there is confusion or difficulty thinking — and **do not assume that
someone found lying motionless in the cold is already dead.**
«SRC: MedlinePlus Medical Encyclopedia (US National Library of Medicine), "Hypothermia" — first aid and
the DO NOT list. | https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000038.htm | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Watch the vulnerable: infants lose heat fast, the elderly may not shiver at all, and alcohol
accelerates the whole process while making the person feel warm.

**What this does not do:** severe hypothermia is a hospital problem. Nobody is declared beyond help in
the field, which is a real clinical principle and a reason to keep going.

### 64 · Heat illness recognition and active cooling

**Prevents:** the deadliest weather mechanism in the country, in your own house.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** water, cloths, a cool room
· **Regions:** all five.

Heat illness is a progression, and catching the transition is the whole skill. Cramps and heavy
sweating with normal mental status is the early stage: stop, cool, hydrate, rest. **Heat stroke is when
mental status changes** — confusion, agitation, slurred speech, collapse, and sometimes hot dry skin
where sweating has stopped. That is a medical emergency with mortality that rises with every minute of
delay.

For heat stroke, cool aggressively while help is coming: out of the sun, clothing off, water on the
skin with air moving over it, ice packs at the neck, armpits, and groin, and if a tub or a body of cool
water is available, immersion is the most effective method.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Extreme Heat" — heat stroke signs: body temperature **above 103°F**; red, hot, dry skin with no sweat; rapid strong pulse; dizziness, confusion or unconsciousness. Response: **call 911 or get the person to hospital immediately**, move to cool shade, remove outer clothing, cold wet cloth or ice packs to **head, neck, armpits and groin**, or soak the clothing with cool water, and elevate the feet. https://www.ready.gov/heat | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Do not give fluids by mouth to anyone with altered mental status.

**What this does not do:** cooling is not a substitute for emergency care, and recovery of alertness
does not mean the emergency has passed.

### 65 · Wound care after floodwater

**Prevents:** a small cut becoming the reason you needed a hospital.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** clean water, soap, dressings
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, all flood-exposed households.

Floodwater is sewage, agricultural runoff, chemicals, and whatever was in the buildings upstream. Any
wound it touches is a contaminated wound, and the infections that follow are the medical story of
every flood recovery.

Irrigate thoroughly with clean or treated water and soap, as soon as possible. Cover with a clean dry
dressing and change it whenever it is wet or dirty. Then watch for spreading redness, swelling,
increasing pain, warmth, pus, red streaking, or fever — any of which needs care.

Two specific issues to raise with a clinician when you can: **tetanus status**, because a
contaminated puncture is a classic tetanus mechanism and many adults are overdue, and the fact that
floodwater exposure carries specific infection risks that shape which antibiotic is appropriate. That
is a decision for a clinician, and this book will not name a drug.

Keep wounds out of floodwater entirely if you can — waterproof dressings and boots, and stay out of the
water where there is any alternative.

**What this does not do:** irrigation reduces contamination; it does not sterilize. Wounds that are
deep, gaping, from a crush mechanism, or contaminated with debris you cannot remove need professional
care, and "we cleaned it" is not a treatment plan for those.

### 66 · Children, and the mind after the event

**Prevents:** treating the psychological toll as somebody else's problem.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** ongoing · **Materials:** attention
· **Regions:** all five.

The most common long-term outcome of a disaster is not injury. It is psychological, and in children it
is shaped almost entirely by the adults around them.

What helps is unglamorous and consistent across the evidence: routine restored quickly, honest
age-appropriate information rather than either false reassurance or adult detail, physical presence,
limited exposure to repeated coverage of the event, and letting children help with a real task, which
restores a sense of agency. Regression — bedwetting, clinginess, sleep disruption — is common and
usually temporary.

For adults: sleep deprivation and decision fatigue are why the injury spike in the recovery window
happens, so this is a physical safety issue as much as an emotional one. FEMA's own list of signs that
an adult may need crisis counseling is worth knowing because none of it sounds like a mental health
crisis from inside: difficulty communicating thoughts, difficulty sleeping, a low threshold of
frustration, limited attention span, tunnel vision or muffled hearing, reluctance to leave home,
overwhelming guilt and self-doubt, fear of crowds or of being alone.
«SRC: Ready.gov / FEMA, "Coping with Disaster" — signs of disaster-related stress in adults; refers
readers to SAMHSA's Disaster Distress Helpline. | https://www.ready.gov/coping-disaster |
retrieved 2026-07-29»

**Two numbers.** The **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** — dial or text **988** — is the general national
line and operates 24 hours, with Spanish-language call, text and chat.
«SRC: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. | https://988lifeline.org/ | retrieved 2026-07-29»
The **Disaster Distress Helpline** is SAMHSA's line specifically for distress related to natural or
human-caused disasters, and FEMA points readers to it directly. Its current number and text options are
published on SAMHSA's page — write them on Form 4 tonight rather than trusting this page to still be
current when you need it, because a printed helpline number is exactly the kind of thing that changes
between an edition and an emergency.

**What this does not do:** this is not therapy and not a substitute for it. Its job is to name the
thing so a household expects it rather than being ambushed by it.

### 67 · When EMS cannot reach you

**Prevents:** waiting for something that is not coming, or driving into something worse.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** an evening of planning · **Materials:** written decisions
· **Regions:** all five.

For some number of hours after a serious disaster, you may be the highest level of medical care
available to the people in your house. Not as a philosophy — as an operational fact.

Plan for it concretely. **Know two hospitals in different directions**, and know which is the trauma
center, because the nearest facility is sometimes the wrong one. **Decide in advance who drives** and
who stays with the children. **Understand that self-transport is sometimes right and sometimes
catastrophic** — moving a person with a possible spinal injury, or driving through flooded roads,
creates a second casualty.

Try to call even when you expect failure, because text messages frequently get through when voice
calls do not, and dispatch may be able to advise even when they cannot send anyone.

And keep the boundary in view. The interventions in this chapter — bleeding control, CPR, cooling,
rewarming, wound irrigation, burn cooling — are the ones that are fast, teachable, and decisive.
Beyond them, the correct action is transport and honesty about what you cannot do.

**What this does not do:** none of this replaces a paramedic. It buys time, and time is frequently the
whole difference.

### 68 · Spares: glasses, mobility, hearing, and the rest

**Prevents:** a functioning adult becoming a dependent one over something trivial.
**Cost:** $20–200 · **Time:** an afternoon · **Materials:** the spares
· **Regions:** all five.

A person who cannot see cannot drive, cannot read a warning, and cannot help. An old pair of glasses
in the go-bag solves it for the price of not throwing them away. The same logic covers a spare cane,
hearing aid batteries in quantity, spare CPAP supplies, an extra blood glucose meter and strips, spare
catheters or ostomy supplies, and a manual backup for anything powered.

Also: a written copy of the household's medical summary — conditions, medications, allergies,
prescriber contacts, insurance numbers — in the document vault and in the go-bag, because the person
who needs it may not be the one who can recite it.

**What this does not do:** spares assume you can reach the go-bag. Which is why it is packed, and by
the door, and not a plan to pack one.
