# Part Three, Playbook 21 — Hazmat Released by a Disaster

Target: 8 pages · draft 1, 2026-07-29

---

## The event

On 3 February 2023 a freight train derailed at East Palestine, Ohio. The NTSB's probable cause, published
in June 2024, was mundane: **a bearing on a hopper car failed and overheated.** Three days later the local
incident commander ordered a **"vent and burn"** of the tank cars carrying vinyl chloride monomer — the
black plume everybody remembers.

And here is the finding this book cannot leave out. NTSB concluded that the decision to vent and burn
**"was based on incomplete and misleading information provided by Norfolk Southern officials and
contractors"** and that **it "was not necessary to prevent a tank car failure."** The most frightening
image of the event was an avoidable one.

Two more items from NTSB's list of safety issues are directly relevant to a household. **The hazardous
materials placards burned away**, which prevented responders from immediately identifying what they were
dealing with. And emergency-response training for the volunteer first responders who arrived was
inadequate. The people at the front of this were working blind.
«SRC: National Transportation Safety Board, "Failed Wheel Bearing Caused Norfolk Southern Train Derailment
in East Palestine, Ohio" (board meeting, 25 June 2024) — probable cause; vent-and-burn decision
"unnecessary" and based on incomplete and misleading information; placards burned away; inadequate
volunteer responder training; continued use of DOT-111 tank cars. |
https://www.ntsb.gov/news/press-releases/Pages/NR20240625.aspx | retrieved 2026-07-29»

The reason it belongs in this book is not the train. It is the reader's position. Thousands of households
discovered, in an afternoon, that they lived close enough to a rail line to be evacuated by it — and almost
none of them had ever thought of that line as a hazard.

**The premise of this playbook is that hazardous materials move through ordinary neighborhoods
continuously**, and that a natural disaster is the most likely thing to release them. Flooded fuel
terminals. Damaged pipelines. A refinery losing power. A chemical plant's containment compromised in a
hurricane. A ruptured anhydrous ammonia tank at an agricultural supplier. A flooded industrial site
releasing what was stored on its floor — which is the same failure as Protection 128 in your own garage, at
industrial scale.

And the natural-hazard link is direct and documented. The clearest federally investigated case is the
**Arkema plant at Crosby, Texas, during Hurricane Harvey.** The facility stored organic peroxides that
must be kept refrigerated or they decompose and burn. Harvey's rainfall flooded the site — which sits in
both the 100-year and 500-year floodplain — and **exceeded the design elevations of the equipment**,
taking out power, then backup power, then the critical refrigeration, then the standby refrigerated
trailers brought in to replace it.

On **31 August 2017** the peroxides in a refrigerated trailer decomposed and caught fire. Across three
fires between 24 August and 4 September, **in excess of 350,000 pounds of organic peroxide products
combusted.** More than **200 residents within 1.5 miles evacuated and could not return home for a week**,
and **twenty-one emergency responders sought medical attention** after exposure to the fumes from the
decomposing product — on a public highway that ran through the evacuation zone and was kept open until
the first fire.

The federal investigators' conclusion is the part that should change how you read the industrial sites near
you. The board reviewed the existing industry guidance on flood hazards and found it *"either too generic
or did not require sufficiently conservative precautions"* — specifically, it **did not require elevating
critical equipment above the flood levels a storm like Harvey would produce.** The plant was not
negligent by the standards of its industry. The standards were the problem.
«SRC: US Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board, *Extreme Weather, Extreme Consequences: CSB
Investigation of the Arkema Crosby Facility and Hurricane Harvey* (executive summary) — incident developed
over twelve days from 24 August to 4 September 2017; fire on 31 August 2017 when refrigerated organic
peroxides decomposed; in excess of 350,000 pounds of organic peroxide products combusted across three
fires; facility located in the 100-year and 500-year floodplain, flooding exceeded equipment design
elevations and took out power, backup power, refrigeration and standby refrigerated trailers; more than
200 residents within 1.5 miles could not return home for a week; twenty-one emergency responders sought
medical attention; existing industry flood-hazard guidance "either too generic or did not require
sufficiently conservative precautions" and did not require elevating critical equipment. |
https://www.csb.gov/arkema-inc-chemical-plant-fire-/ | retrieved 2026-07-29»

## What actually kills, and what actually costs

**Inhalation, overwhelmingly.** Most substances of concern harm by being breathed, and most are heavier than
air, which means they pool in low ground, basements, ditches, underpasses and valley bottoms. That single
physical fact drives the entire response: **up and crosswind**.

**Then contact and ingestion**, through skin, through contaminated water, and through food.

**The acute effects** vary by substance and the pattern of symptoms is worth recognizing as a group rather
than individually: burning eyes, nose and throat; coughing and difficulty breathing; chest tightness;
headache, dizziness, nausea and confusion; skin irritation or burns. A strong chemical smell where there
should not be one, several people in the same place feeling ill at once, or dead birds or animals, are the
environmental tells.

**And the mistake that kills bystanders:** approaching to look, or going in to help. Hazmat incidents produce
secondary casualties among the people who walked toward them, which is why the professional discipline is
distance first and assistance second.

**Costs:** displacement, sometimes prolonged. Property value damage, which is real and slow. Medical
surveillance. Loss of a well or garden. And a class of expense the insurance industry handles badly:
pollution and contamination are frequently excluded or narrowly limited under standard homeowner policies,
which means the recovery route is typically the responsible party's liability and litigation rather than
your own insurer.

## Before

- **125 — the proximity lookup**, and here it is the whole protection. Find out what is near you: rail lines
  carrying tank cars, designated hazardous-materials truck routes, pipelines, refineries, chemical plants,
  fuel depots, agricultural suppliers holding ammonia or fertiliser, and water treatment works holding
  chlorine. Pipelines are publicly mapped, and your local emergency management office maintains the hazard
  analysis naming specific facilities and substances.
- **Then learn one fact about your house: which way the prevailing wind blows.** That determines whether a
  release at a given facility reaches you, and it is the fact that turns a map into a plan.
- **126 — the shelter-in-place protocol**, and a room chosen for it: interior, few windows, with plastic
  sheeting and tape stored in it. Because most substances of concern are heavier than air, an upper floor is
  usually the better choice — but the instruction at the time overrides this, which is why the protocol is
  **go in, stay in, tune in** rather than a fixed rule.
- **51 — sealing that room**, practiced once so you know it takes ten minutes and not two.
- **49 — HVAC shutdown**, and knowing how to kill the system entirely — not recirculate, off — and where the
  fresh air intake is.
- **52 — N95 supply**, with the honest limitation stated plainly: an N95 filters particles and does **nothing**
  for gases and vapors, which are the main hazard here. It is worth having and it is not protection against
  a chemical plume. Nothing in a household kit is. Distance and sealed space are the protections.
- **85, 86 — two alert paths and county enrollment**, because a hazmat evacuation is announced by the
  county and by wireless emergency alert, and it comes with minutes of notice.
- **107, 108 — go-bags and the five-minute list**, because this hazard produces immediate short-notice
  evacuations more often than almost any other in the book.
- **128 — your own chemicals**, off the floor and sorted, because in a flood your garage is a hazmat site.
- **1 — stored water**, and if you are on a well near an industrial site or a rail line, a baseline water
  test done **now** while nothing has happened. That baseline is the single most valuable document you can
  hold if a release ever occurs, because without it you cannot prove what changed.

**And know the honest limit of household preparation here.** You cannot filter, neutralize or ride out a
serious chemical release. Your protections are: know what is near you, get the alert, seal a room or leave
fast, and follow the instruction. That is the whole toolkit, and it is enough.

---

## During

**If you are told to shelter in place — go in, stay in, tune in.**

Everyone inside, including pets. Close and lock every window and door. **Shut the HVAC off completely** —
heating, cooling, ventilation, extract fans, range hood — and close the fireplace damper. Go to the chosen
room, usually upstairs. Seal the door edges and any vents with plastic sheeting and tape. Take a radio, water,
medication and phones.

Then **stay put and listen**, and do not leave until you are told the emergency has passed. In this hazard,
self-directed evacuation is frequently worse than sheltering, because a car is not sealed and the road may
run downwind.

**If you are told to evacuate — go immediately, and go crosswind.** Take the five-minute list and nothing
else. Follow the route given rather than your usual one, because the route is chosen against the plume.

**If you are outdoors, or you encounter a release with no instruction yet:**

**Move upwind and uphill.** Across the wind rather than with it, and gain height, because the vapor pools
low. Get out of ditches, underpasses and basements. Cover your nose and mouth with cloth, understanding that
it does very little. Keep going until you are clear of the low ground and out of the plume's line, then get
inside a building and call it in.

**Do not approach**, do not drive toward it to see, do not photograph it from close range, and do not enter
to help someone. Report the location and let the people with breathing apparatus do it. This is the rule that
saves bystanders and it is counter to every instinct.

**If someone is contaminated:** get them out of the area, get the contaminated clothing off — cut it off
rather than pulling it over the head where possible — and flush skin and eyes with large amounts of water for
many minutes, longer than feels necessary. Bag the clothing and keep it, both to limit further exposure and
because it is evidence. Do not let contaminated people into the sealed room or into a vehicle you then sit
in.

**In an evacuation, note what you were told and by whom**, and note the time. Records of official
instructions matter enormously later, and nobody keeps them.

## After

**Return only when officials clear the area**, and understand that "cleared" for re-entry does not always
mean "no residual contamination" — ask specifically what was tested, what the results were, and what was not
tested.

**Ventilate the house thoroughly** on return: open everything, run fans outward, and give it hours rather than
minutes. Then replace the HVAC filter.

**Clean hard surfaces wet**, and be prepared to discard porous items that were exposed to a vapor or a
residue — soft furnishings, carpet, bedding, children's soft toys — because they hold what hard surfaces
release.

**Do not use garden produce** that was exposed until authorities advise, and be skeptical of reassurance
that does not come with a test result. Same for a private well: test it, and compare against your baseline if
you have one. If you do not, test anyway, because a result now is a baseline for later.

**Then document everything, because this is a liability hazard rather than an insurance hazard.**

Write a timeline while it is fresh: when you first noticed anything, what it smelled or looked like, what you
were told, when you left, when you returned. Photograph everything. Keep every receipt — lodging, food,
travel, cleaning, replaced belongings, medical visits. Keep the bagged clothing. Record every symptom in
every household member, with dates, even mild ones, because chemical exposure claims turn on documented
contemporaneous symptoms and nothing else.

**Get medical assessment for anyone exposed, even if they feel well**, and make sure the exposure is recorded
in the medical notes with the substance named if it is known. This is the single most important thing you can
do for a household's long-term interests, and it is routinely skipped because everyone feels fine.

**Register with any official health registry or monitoring program** that is set up. These are established
after significant releases and they are how long-term effects get recognized.

**Then get advice before you sign anything.** After a significant release, the responsible party's
representatives will offer payments, sometimes quickly and sometimes with a release of future claims attached.
Understand what you are signing, and understand that pollution exclusions in your own homeowner policy make
the responsible party your realistic route to recovery. This is one of the few places in this book where
recommending a lawyer is the honest advice rather than an evasion.
