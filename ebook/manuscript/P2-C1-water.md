## Water

Water is first in this book for the same reason it is first in every serious preparedness document:
the timeline is brutally short and completely non-negotiable. A household can function badly without
power for a week and without food for longer than anyone enjoys thinking about. Water is measured in
days, and the days are few.

It is also the resource most likely to fail in ways people do not anticipate. The common assumption
is that water fails by disappearing — faucets run dry. In practice it fails four different ways, and
three of them leave the faucets working:

- **Pressure loss.** Pumping stations need power. High ground loses pressure first.
- **Contamination with pressure intact.** A main break, a flood, or a treatment failure means water
  still comes out and is no longer safe. This is the most common failure in the United States, and
  it arrives as a boil-water advisory.
- **Well failure.** No grid, no pump, no water — for a large fraction of rural households.
- **Freeze.** The supply is intact and the pipe delivering it is solid, or split.

The protections below cover all four. They are ordered so that the free and instant ones come first.

### 1 · Fourteen-day drinking water store

**Prevents:** the shortest and least survivable shortage in this book.
**Cost:** $0–60 · **Time:** one afternoon · **Materials:** commercial bottled water or food-grade
containers · **Regions:** all five. Rank 1 Quake West, rank 3 Hurricane Coast, rank 9 Ice & Snow.

The planning figure is **one gallon per person per day**, which covers drinking and modest sanitary
use — not laundry, not a shower. The federal guidance behind it is worth knowing precisely: a normally
active person needs about three quarters of a gallon of fluid a day, and the extra quarter is the
sanitation margin. Children, nursing mothers and sick people need more, and **in very hot temperatures
water needs can double** — which is the line most households miss when they size a store in
January.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Water" — Determining Water Needs. https://www.ready.gov/water | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Two weeks, not three days — and it is worth being exact about where each number comes from, because
they are not the same authority saying different things. **Ready.gov says one gallon per person per
day "for several days."** That is the federal floor and it is written for the common case. **The
two-week figure is state guidance from the places that expect help to be slow**: Oregon's Department
of Emergency Management and Washington's Emergency Management Division both run a public program
called **"Be 2 Weeks Ready,"** and both say plainly that after a large regional event the emergency
system will be saturated and *you are the help until help arrives.*
«SRC: Oregon Department of Emergency Management, "Be 2 Weeks Ready." |
https://www.oregon.gov/oem/hazardsprep/pages/2-weeks-ready.aspx | retrieved 2026-07-29»
«SRC: Washington State Emergency Management Division, "Be 2 Weeks Ready." |
https://mil.wa.gov/preparedness | retrieved 2026-07-29»

This book uses two weeks as its planning figure, and the reason is the arithmetic of scale rather
than pessimism: three days assumes the disaster is local and the region around it is intact. Every
event where that assumption failed — a Cascadia rupture, a multi-state ice storm, an island
hurricane — is an event where the region around you needs the same trucks you do.

Run your own number rather than accepting a generic one, and write it on the form in Part Six:
household members, plus pets, times fourteen. Pets are not a rounding error — a large dog can drink
as much as an adult.

The cheapest compliant answer is commercially bottled water, because it is sealed, dated, and
requires no judgment. If you use your own containers, they must be food-grade and previously used
only for food or drink. Do not store drinking water in containers that held anything else, and do not
reuse milk jugs — the residual proteins and sugars cannot be reliably removed and the plastic is
designed to break down.

Store it cool, dark, and off bare concrete. Distribute it — some in the basement, some upstairs, some
in the car — because a single stack in one room can be crushed, contaminated, or on the wrong side of
a collapsed doorway.

**What this does not do:** it does not cover washing, flushing, or cooking anything that needs
simmering. That is a separate and much larger quantity, and it comes from Protection 3 and Protection
5, not from your drinking store. Never spend drinking water on a toilet.

### 2 · A rotation system that survives being forgotten

**Prevents:** a fourteen-day supply that is four years old when you finally need it.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** ten minutes, then twice a year · **Materials:** a marker
· **Regions:** all five.

Stored water does not spoil the way food does, but containers fail, seals leak, plastic degrades, and
commercial bottles carry a date the manufacturer chose for a reason. The failure mode is not that the
water becomes dangerous. It is that you discover a slow leak and an empty container on the day you
need it.

Write the date on every container in marker, on the side rather than the cap, in figures large enough
to read by flashlight. Then attach the check to something you already do twice a year — the clock
change, a birthday, the day the smoke alarm batteries get done. The rotation itself is trivial:
oldest containers get poured on the garden and refilled.

**What this does not do:** a date on a container is not a purity test. If a container is cloudy,
swollen, smells of plastic, or has been sitting in sunlight, replace it rather than reasoning about
it.

### 3 · The pre-storm fill protocol

**Prevents:** having only your stored supply when you had hours of warning and a house full of empty
containers.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** twenty minutes when a watch is issued · **Materials:** optional tub bladder
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, Ice & Snow North, Wildfire West.

This is the highest-return free action in the entire water chapter, and it belongs in your trigger
plan rather than in your memory.

When a watch is issued — not a warning, a watch — you fill everything. Every pot, every pitcher, both
bathtubs, the washing machine, buckets, the cooler. A standard bathtub holds far more than your
entire drinking store, and that water is for flushing, washing, and cleaning, which is where the
volume actually goes.

Two refinements worth the small cost. A tub bladder — a food-grade liner that fills inside the tub —
keeps the water clean enough to drink and stops it draining past a tired plug. And if you are on a
well, fill *before* the power goes, because after it goes you have no pump and the fill is over.

Label the bathtub water clearly as non-potable unless you used a bladder, and keep the bathroom door
shut if there are small children or pets in the house. An open tub of standing water is a drowning
hazard, and that is not a theoretical concern.

**What this does not do:** it is not a substitute for Protection 1. Flash floods, earthquakes, and
grid failures arrive with no watch at all, and this protection produces nothing in those events.

### 4 · The water heater as a reserve

**Prevents:** overlooking forty to eighty gallons of drinkable water already inside the house.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** fifteen minutes to learn, once · **Materials:** a hose, a wrench
· **Regions:** all five.

A conventional tank water heater holds a large volume of potable water at all times. It is the
biggest single reserve most houses have and almost nobody knows how to get it out.

The sequence matters and the order is not optional:

1. **Kill the energy first.** Gas to pilot or off at the valve; electric off at the breaker. A tank
   that fires with no water in it destroys itself and can start a fire.
2. **Shut the cold-water inlet** to the tank, so you are drawing from the tank rather than from a
   contaminated main.
3. **Open a hot faucet** somewhere in the house to admit air. Without this the tank will barely drain.
4. **Draw from the drain valve** at the base into a clean container. The first flow will be
   discolored with sediment; let it run clear.
5. **Do not refill or re-energize** until you know the supply is safe and the tank is full.

Learn this on a calm afternoon, not by flashlight. Hang the hose and the wrench on the tank itself.

**What this does not do:** it produces nothing in a tankless system, which stores no water at all.
It also produces hot, sediment-heavy water that needs to cool and settle, and if your supply was
already contaminated before the tank filled, the tank contents are contaminated too.

### 5 · Rain capture for everything that is not drinking

**Prevents:** spending drinking water on flushing, washing, and cleaning.
**Cost:** $40–200 · **Time:** an afternoon · **Materials:** barrel, downspout diverter, screen
· **Regions:** Hurricane Coast, Ice & Snow North, Drought regions.

Non-potable demand is where the gallons actually go, and a roof produces a startling amount of water
from a modest amount of rain. A barrel at a downspout, screened against mosquitoes and debris, covers
flushing and washing indefinitely in any wet climate.

Two constraints. First, check local rules — a small number of jurisdictions regulate rainwater
capture, and it is worth thirty seconds of searching rather than a fine. Second, treat roof runoff as
non-potable by default: it carries whatever is on the roof, which in a wildfire region includes ash
and in every region includes bird droppings and asphalt granules.

**What this does not do:** this is not drinking water, and filtering it into drinking water is a
larger project than this book covers. It also produces nothing in a drought, which is precisely when
you want it — which is why the drought chapter treats storage rather than capture as the answer.

### 6 · Choosing a filter that matches the threat

**Prevents:** buying the wrong device and trusting it.
**Cost:** $30–120 · **Time:** an hour of reading, once · **Materials:** a gravity or hollow-fiber
filter · **Regions:** all five.

Filters are the most misunderstood item in preparedness, because the word covers devices that do
completely different things. What matters is the specific threat you are filtering for:

- **Sediment and turbidity** — nearly anything works, including cloth pre-filtering.
- **Bacteria and protozoa** — this is what a good hollow-fiber or ceramic filter is for, and it is
  what most municipal contamination and most surface water requires.
- **Viruses** — most portable filters do **not** remove viruses, because viruses are smaller than
  the pore size. This requires either a purifier rated for viruses or a chemical or thermal step.
- **Chemicals, heavy metals, and salt** — filters do essentially nothing. Boiling makes salt and most
  chemicals *worse* by concentrating them. This is the case where you do not treat the water at all;
  you use a different source.

That last line is the important one. If the failure in your area is industrial or chemical, no
household filter is the answer, and any advice that implies otherwise is dangerous.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Water" — Water Treatment comparison table: boiling and chlorination kill microbes
but remove neither heavy metals, salts nor most other chemicals; only distillation removes them.
https://www.ready.gov/water | retrieved 2026-07-29»

Buy a filter with a published specification and keep the specification with it. Then read the
boil-water advisory when it arrives, because it will tell you what the problem actually is.

**What this does not do:** a filter does not make chemically contaminated water safe, does not
desalinate, and does not remove most viruses. It also has a finite capacity, which is printed on the
box and which nobody tracks.

### 7 · Chemical disinfection

**Prevents:** having water and no way to make it safe.
**Cost:** $5–15 · **Time:** minutes · **Materials:** plain unscented household bleach, or purification
tablets · **Regions:** all five.

Chemical disinfection is the cheapest backstop there is, and the details are the whole protection.

**Use only plain, unscented household bleach with sodium hypochlorite as the sole active
ingredient.** Not scented. Not color-safe. Not "splash-less". Not any product with added
surfactants, thickeners, or fragrance. The additives are not treatable and some are toxic. Check the
label for the concentration, because household bleach is sold at more than one strength and the dose
depends on it.

Bleach also degrades. An opened bottle loses potency over months, which means the bottle under your
sink is not a reliable emergency supply. Purification tablets are more expensive per liter, do not
degrade nearly as fast, and are what belongs in a go-bag.

**The ratio is not written from memory in this book.** The federal figure, for regular unscented
household bleach containing **5.25 to 6.0 percent sodium hypochlorite**, is **one-eighth of a teaspoon
per gallon** of water. Stir it, then **let it stand thirty minutes**. The water should then have a
slight bleach odor — **if it does not, repeat the dose and stand another fifteen minutes, and if it
still does not smell of chlorine, discard it and find another source.** Never use scented, color-safe
or cleaner-added bleach, and do not substitute iodine or camping products whose only active ingredient
is not sodium hypochlorite at those concentrations.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Water" — Water Treatment, Chlorination. https://www.ready.gov/water | retrieved 2026-07-29» It goes on the form in Part Six and on a
card taped inside the cabinet with the bleach, so that the number is read rather than recalled.

Before disinfecting, filter or settle cloudy water — organic matter consumes the disinfectant and
protects the organisms in it.

**What this does not do:** chemical disinfection does not remove chemicals, metals, or salt, and it
is less effective against some protozoa than boiling or filtration. It is a bacterial and viral
answer, not a universal one.

### 8 · Boiling, and reading a boil-water advisory

**Prevents:** treating a chemical problem as a biological one, and vice versa.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** minutes · **Materials:** a heat source you already have
· **Regions:** all five.

Boiling is the most reliable household treatment for biological contamination and requires no
consumables. **A rolling boil for one full minute** kills what matters. Let it cool before drinking,
and expect some of the volume to evaporate. If it tastes flat afterwards, pour it between two clean
containers to put the air back in.
«SRC: Ready.gov, "Water" — Water Treatment, Boiling. https://www.ready.gov/water | retrieved 2026-07-29»

The harder skill is reading the advisory. A boil-water notice is issued for different reasons and the
correct response differs:

- **Precautionary, after a pressure loss or main break** — the most common. Boiling is the answer.
- **Confirmed bacterial detection** — boiling is the answer.
- **Chemical or industrial contamination** — **do not boil.** Boiling concentrates it. Use only
  bottled or hauled water until the advisory is lifted.

Advisories also carry instructions people ignore: ice from the machine is unsafe, so are drinks made
before the notice; brush teeth with treated water; dishes and infant feeding equipment need treated
water. Read the notice rather than assuming you know what it says.

**What this does not do:** boiling does nothing for chemicals, metals, or salt, and it makes some of
them worse. It also uses fuel, which is finite in the same outage.

### 9 · Wells: the power-loss plan

**Prevents:** discovering that your water is entirely dependent on the grid.
**Cost:** $0–500 · **Time:** an evening to plan · **Materials:** stored water, optionally a hand pump
· **Regions:** rural households in all five.

If you are on a private well, your water is electric. No power, no pump, no water — and unlike a
municipal customer, there is no utility restoring anything for you.

Three tiers, and the first is free. **Store more.** A well household should hold more than the
fourteen-day figure, because there is no partial service to fall back on. **Know your system:** where
the pressure tank is, what the pump's power requirement is, and whether your generator can actually
start it, since well pumps have a starting draw much higher than their running draw and this is a
common and expensive surprise. **A hand pump or a generator-fed circuit** is the durable answer, and
it is a real expense — price it honestly rather than treating it as an afterthought.

After any flood that reaches the wellhead, the well is presumed contaminated. **EPA's own threshold is
lower than most owners assume:** a well *"more than 10 years old or less than 50 feet deep"* is *"likely
to be contaminated, even if there is no apparent damage."* No apparent damage is not evidence.

Two warnings come before any disinfection, and both are printed in capitals in EPA's guidance because
they are the parts that hurt people. **Do not turn on the pump** — *"there is danger of electrical shock
and damage to your well or pump if they have been flooded"* — and do not restore power until the wiring
has been checked by a qualified electrician or pump contractor. **Do not wash with the water either:**
*"people drinking or washing with water from a private well that has been flooded will risk getting
sick."*

The disinfection itself is nine steps and EPA says plainly that drilled, driven and bored wells *"are
best disinfected by a well or pump contractor, because it is difficult for the private owner to
thoroughly disinfect these wells."* If you are doing it yourself, the shape of it is: run the water
clear, pour a gallon of unscented household bleach down the casing, run an outside hose back into the
casing until you smell chlorine, open every cold faucet inside and out until each one smells of chlorine
and then close them all, and **wait six to twenty-four hours** without drinking, cooking, bathing or
washing with any of it. Then flush to an area away from plants, streams and the septic field until the
smell is gone. **Then wait seven to ten days and have the water tested for bacteria** — the wait is so
the chlorine has cleared and the test is measuring the well rather than the bleach. If you have a water
treatment system, switch it to bypass before you put chlorine through the house.

Two limits worth knowing before you trust a single clean result. Disinfection *"will not provide
protection from pesticides, heavy metals and other types of non-biological contamination"* — it kills
organisms and does nothing to chemistry. And a clean test is not a permanent verdict: EPA's caution is
that *"your well may not be a safe source of water for many months after the flood"* because
groundwater keeps moving, so *"it will be necessary to take long range precautions, including repeated
testing."* Get the specific ratios and any local requirements from your county health department, which
knows your aquifer.
«SRC: US Environmental Protection Agency, "What to Do After the Flood" (EPA 816-F-05-021, updated
October 2024) — do not turn on the pump; do not wash with well water; wells over 10 years old or under
50 feet likely contaminated; nine-step disinfection with one gallon unscented bleach and a 6–24 hour
wait; test for bacteria 7–10 days after; disinfection does not address pesticides or heavy metals;
repeated long-range testing needed. | https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2015-05/documents/epa816f05021.pdf | retrieved 2026-07-29»

**What this does not do:** storing water does not restore your well, and a hand pump does not work on
every well depth or casing type. Check yours before buying anything.

### 10 · Cisterns and pressure tanks

**Prevents:** mismanaging a system you already own.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** thirty minutes to understand · **Materials:** none
· **Regions:** rural and drought regions.

If you have a cistern, you have a large buffer and an obligation. Know the volume, know how to read
the level without power, know where the inlet screening is, and know how you would haul water into it
if it ran dry — including whether your access allows a delivery truck to reach it, which is a
question worth answering before the summer you need it.

A pressure tank holds a small usable volume above the pump, which is why a faucet sometimes runs for a
minute after the power fails. That minute is real and worth catching in a container.

**What this does not do:** neither is a treatment system. Stored volume says nothing about quality.

### 11 · Gray-water reuse under restriction

**Prevents:** wasting water that has one more job left in it.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** none · **Materials:** a bucket
· **Regions:** Drought regions, and any long outage.

In a shortage, water gets used twice by design rather than by accident. Water that has washed dishes
or hands flushes a toilet. Water that has boiled vegetables cools and goes on the garden. A bucket in
the shower catches what runs while the water warms.

Two limits. Do not store gray water — it grows bacteria quickly, so use it the same day. And keep
water that has touched raw meat, soiled diapers, or cleaning chemicals out of the reuse chain
entirely.

**What this does not do:** gray water is never drinking water, never washes food, and is not for
anyone with a compromised immune system.

### 12 · Freeze protection for stored water

**Prevents:** a fourteen-day supply that is a fourteen-day block of ice, in split containers.
**Cost:** $0 · **Time:** twenty minutes · **Materials:** none
· **Regions:** Ice & Snow North, Quake West at altitude.

Water expands as it freezes, which splits full rigid containers and pops the seals on the rest. If
your supply lives in a garage, an unheated basement, a shed, or a car, this is the protection that
keeps it usable.

Leave headspace in every container — a few inches, not a topped-off fill. Keep the primary supply
inside the conditioned envelope of the house, and treat any outdoor cache as a bonus rather than the
plan. And note the obvious upside: in a winter outage, frozen water is also cold storage, so an ice
block in the freezer from Protection 15 is doing two jobs.

**What this does not do:** headspace prevents container failure, not freezing. Frozen water is not
drinking water until it thaws, and thawing a five-gallon block in a cold house takes far longer than
anyone expects — which is the argument for keeping some supply in smaller containers.
