About

The reports get written.
Almost nobody reads them.

After every major American storm, the government investigates itself. The National Weather Service writes a service assessment. NIST runs a technical investigation. FEMA publishes the mitigation studies. Those documents name, in plain language, what killed people and what would have saved them — and then they sit in PDF archives, read by almost no one they were written about.

The Warning Hour exists to read them out loud. Each episode takes one storm and tells the story the documents tell — what the warnings said, what people heard, what changed afterward. The Storm-Ready Home is the other half of the work: the findings turned into 130 numbered protections a household can actually do, every figure cited to the document it came from.

The method, in four rules

  1. 01Primary documents only. Service assessments, technical investigations, storm-event databases, the agencies' own directories. Never a retelling of a retelling.
  2. 02Every figure carries its source. If a number appears — a wind speed, a death toll, a dollar figure — the issuing document is named. In the book, the citation is printed next to the claim.
  3. 03Say what it does not do. Every protection in the book ends with its honest limits. A plan that oversells is worse than no plan.
  4. 04Turn findings into work. A finding is only useful once it becomes a Saturday's task with a cost, a time, and a checklist line. That translation is the whole product.

Where the work lives

The episodes are on The Warning Hour, with a written dispatch for each in Field Notes. The free tools — the emergency binder, the trigger plan, the checklists, and a storm page for every state — are free and stay that way, no email asked. The book is the deep end.
