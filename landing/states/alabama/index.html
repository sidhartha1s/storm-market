The hazards Alabama actually faces

  • Tornado

    Peak season: Apr

    Tornadoes form fast, and even a well-run warning system only buys minutes. The National Weather Service's after-action reports on the deadliest US tornadoes trace back to the same pattern: people heard a warning, stepped outside to look for the funnel, and lost the time a sheltered household would have had. A named safe room — the lowest floor, smallest interior room, most walls between you and outside — and a rehearsed trigger point close most of that gap.

  • Hail and derecho

    Peak season: Mar–Aug

    Hail and derecho winds arrive inside the same thunderstorm complex, often with less lead time than a tornado warning because the threat is the whole storm line, not a single funnel. Large hail totals more property-damage claims nationally than any other severe-storm hazard, and derecho wind gusts can match hurricane-force winds hundreds of miles from open water. Vehicles under cover and loose yard items secured before the line arrives are the cheap fix.

  • Lightning

    Peak season: Jun–Jul

    Lightning is the most common severe-weather hazard by frequency, and most strikes happen well before or after the heaviest rain — the 30-30 rule (shelter if under 30 seconds between flash and bang, wait 30 minutes after the last one) still holds. It is also the hazard households prepare for least, because it rarely triggers a county-level warning of its own.

Rules worth knowing

  • Emergency prescription refills: Standing emergency refill is a 72-hour supply; a governor's emergency proclamation can extend it to a 30-day supply. Not for Schedule I/II drugs.
