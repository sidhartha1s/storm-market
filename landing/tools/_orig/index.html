Joplin, Missouri · May 22, 2011 · 5:11 p.m.

It was a Sunday evening. Graduation day. Families were driving home from the high-school ceremony with the windows down when the sirens started — and most people did what most people always do. They waited. They looked at the sky. They turned on the TV to see if it was serious this time.

The sirens sounded at 5:11. The tornado touched down at 5:34. In those twenty-three minutes almost nobody moved. One hundred and fifty-eight people were killed directly that evening, and four more in the days that followed. The National Weather Service assessment written afterwards is blunt about why: most residents did not take protective action until they had gone and found confirmation — seeing it themselves, hearing it said on air, or waiting for the second siren. The families who moved at 5:11 had decided what 5:11 meant long before it happened.

Every disaster report says the same quiet thing: the survivors decided before the storm did. This book is that decision, written down.

The money chapter

What the average household gets, and what the program allows.

Federal disaster aid is not insurance and it is not compensation. It is a floor. How close you land to the ceiling is decided almost entirely by what you can prove — and most of that proof has to exist before the event.

What this is not

This is not a promise that documentation moves you from three thousand dollars to forty-three thousand. Awards are determined by verified loss, insurance status and eligibility, and most households will never approach the maximum. What documentation removes is the loss you cannot prove — which is the only part of the gap you control.

Three of the protections cost nothing at all. The twenty-minute video walkthrough of every room. The document vault. The insurance audit that makes you read the five numbers on your own policy. All three sit in week four of eight — the paper week, which the book places before every week that costs money.

Who wrote this

Paul Raymond

I got tired of watching the same three mistakes kill people in the report afterwards. Not exotic mistakes. The same three, in county after county, storm after storm. This book is those reports turned back into things a family can do on a Saturday.

The manual is built from the documents most people never read — National Weather Service service assessments, NIST investigations, day-after archives — and converted into numbered actions with real prices next to them. Every hazard chapter opens with an event that actually happened and ends with what would have changed it.

Before you buy

Questions people ask.

What exactly do I get, and in what format?

A single PDF of 486 pages, delivered to your inbox immediately after payment, plus the printable binder pages and your state packets. Readable on a phone, tablet or computer, and printable at a kitchen table. No app, no DRM, no subscription.

Isn't this information free from FEMA?

Some of it is, and the book cites those documents rather than hiding them. What the federal guides give you is pamphlet prose — no prices, no build steps, no fillable forms, no regional ordering and no state directory. This book was written because reading the source documents and turning them into a Saturday's work is the part nobody does.

I rent. Is this useful to me?

Yes, and the book flags it protection by protection. Most of the highest-value items need no permission at all — the room selection, the water store, the document vault, the alert enrollment, the prescription buffer, the trigger plan, the binder. The structural hardening chapters are marked so you can skip what you can't do.

Which region is mine?

The front matter walks you through it in about five minutes, and most readers are in two regions rather than one — which is fine, because regions only change the order you work in, not what you read. Every chapter is in every copy.

Is this a prepper book?

No. There is no bunker content, no firearms, no grid-down-forever scenario and no politics. It is a family safety manual about weather and natural disasters, and every instruction is traceable to a citable authority.

I'm not good with technology. Will I manage?

If you can open an email, you can use this book. It arrives as one ordinary PDF — no app to install, no account to create, no login to remember. Print any page at home or at a copy shop, and the binder is designed to be filled in with a pen.

What's your refund policy?

Seven days, no questions asked. Reply to the delivery email and the $9.99 comes back. You keep the files.
