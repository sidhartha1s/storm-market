## Beat 4 · The agency on record · 201 words
The agency on record is the Japan Meteorological Agency, JMA, the government body responsible for tracking every active volcano in Japan, including Sakurajima. Its bulletins are technical activity reports, not press statements. They are built from seismographs, gas sensors, and cameras trained on the crater around the clock. Each one is issued on a set schedule rather than in response to any single event. The most recent bulletin, timed sixteen hundred Japan Standard Time on September fourteenth, closes with this line from the agency itself. Volcanic gas discharge remains generally abundant. Magma continues accumulating beneath the caldera under the bay. Eruptive activity is expected to continue. That assessment does not describe a single day's weather. It describes a system still filling from below. The eruptions at the surface are treated as the release, not the whole event. The agency is not forecasting an ending to this activity. It is describing a process it expects to keep running past this bulletin. That process is expected to continue past the next one too, for as long as the deep supply keeps arriving beneath the bay. No individual spokesperson signs this assessment. It stands as the agency's own institutional line, carried bulletin after bulletin.

## Beat 5 · Cause · 316 words
The mechanism behind this week's numbers reaches back months, not days. Sakurajima sits on the edge of a much larger, mostly submerged volcanic basin, the caldera under the bay. Japan's monitoring agency has tracked magma slowly refilling that deep reservoir for an extended stretch. A field survey earlier this month measured the mountain's sulfur dioxide output at roughly two thousand one hundred tonnes a day. The agency calls that rate generally abundant. Gas at that volume signals fresh magma still pushing up from depth. That refilling explains something that looks contradictory in the daily count. Volcanic earthquakes at Sakurajima have been falling, from nine a day down to three a day across four days. Explosions dropped to zero in that same window. Yet the summit crater still produced six separate eruptions in that period. Fewer quakes usually track with less rock breaking underground as magma forces a path upward. But the agency's own bulletin ties the pattern to a deep supply that keeps accumulating steadily rather than surging. That steady supply lets the mountain vent in a more continuous rhythm. It replaces the sharp, quake heavy pulses that come with sudden pressure spikes. Continuous does not mean minor. In May, Sakurajima produced a pyroclastic flow, a fast moving cloud of hot gas and rock. It travelled roughly six hundred meters down the mountainside. It was the volcano's first flow of that kind in roughly eight years. Around that same period, monitors also recorded the mountain's own body swelling, as magma pooled inside it ahead of that flow. The agency reads this week's steady eruptions, the falling quake count, and the abundant gas discharge as different signs of one process. That same refilling process produced May's flow. Nothing in the current data suggests that process has stopped. It suggests the mountain has settled into a quieter, steadier way of releasing what keeps arriving from below the bay.

A second crater nearby stays closed under that same order. The law cites danger from thrown rock and from fast moving flows. Monitoring continues around the clock, feeding each new eruption straight into the agency's next bulletin. Nothing in the public record points to evacuation orders this week.
